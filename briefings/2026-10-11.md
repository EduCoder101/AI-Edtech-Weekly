# AI in EdTech Weekly -- 11 October 2026

*A research briefing for the AI Integration Lead, Sydney independent K-12 school. Covering 5--11 October 2026.*

---

## Top Stories

### 1. National study: one in three victimised children tell no one, or tell a chatbot

**EducationHQ / AAP | 9 October 2026**

A national study of Australian adolescents, AI and non-AI online child sexual victimisation, led by Tim Cubitt and colleagues at the Australian Cybercrime Observatory at Adelaide University and discussed publicly this week by AFP Commissioner Krissy Barrett, found that around one in four children under 18 has experienced online sexual victimisation or knows someone who has, with roughly 15 per cent of those cases involving AI. The most confronting finding is about disclosure: 35 per cent of victimised children either told no one or turned to an AI chatbot for support, and among victimised boys the figure was two in five. More than 15 per cent of young people said they seek personal or counselling advice from AI at least weekly. The AFP is now working with industry on chatbot responses that direct young people to trusted adults, and is adding AI-assisted flagging to its reporting tool.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This is the clearest Australian evidence yet that AI companions are a child-protection issue before they are an academic-integrity one. If a third of victimised students disclose to a bot or to nobody, pastoral systems built on the assumption that a distressed child will eventually tell an adult are missing a growing share of cases. The practical implications sit with wellbeing staff as much as with the AI lead: explicit teaching, from primary years up, that a chatbot is not a counsellor and that a trusted adult is the right first call, and awareness among staff that victimisation clusters in friendship groups. This belongs on the agenda of the next child-safe standards review, not just the AI working group.

Sources: ['Heartbreaking': sexually exploited young people turning to AI for a shoulder to cry on](https://educationhq.com/news/heartbreaking-sexually-exploited-young-people-turning-to-ai-for-a-shoulder-to-cry-on-215779/) -- EducationHQ / AAP, 9 October 2026.

### 2. UNSW study shows chatbot guardrails fail under manipulation, with every method tested

**The Educator K-12 | 7 October 2026**

UNSW researchers led by Dr Aditya Joshi, with Anudeex Shetty and Professor Salil Kanhere, showed that large language models including GPT-4 can be reliably jailbroken by inducing "drunk" behaviour, through prompting alone or by fine-tuning in ways that mirror how commercial AI products are actually customised and deployed. Across all three methods tested, the altered models answered harmful questions they are designed to refuse and became significantly more likely to disclose confidential information. Professor Kanhere's summary: "We cannot assume that protections that work under normal conditions will remain effective."

**Why it matters for K-12:** Most school-facing AI tools are a vendor's customisation layer on top of a foundation model, and this study shows that the customisation step itself can quietly weaken the safety behaviour schools think they are buying. It converts vendor due diligence from a privacy exercise into a safety one: has the product been red-team tested after customisation, how does it handle deliberate manipulation by students, and who is accountable when guardrails fail? The article points to NSW's NSWEduChat, with its layered jailbreak filtering, as an example of what deliberate safety design looks like; that is a useful benchmark to hold commercial vendors against in procurement conversations.

Sources: [AI safety in schools: UNSW study exposes chatbot guardrail flaws](https://www.theeducatoronline.com/k12/news/ai-safety-in-schools-unsw-study-exposes-chatbot-guardrail-flaws/289245) -- The Educator K-12, 7 October 2026.

### 3. "Spot the Sycophant": Australian researchers release a primary-years tool for teaching children to question AI

**EducationHQ | 9 October 2026**

Researchers from the ARC Centre of Excellence for Automated Decision-Making and Society, the ARC Centre of Excellence for the Digital Child and the QUT GenAI Lab have released an interactive voice-based chatbot, Spot the Sycophant, hosted on the GenAI Arcade site. Children switch the bot between an "always agree" mode and a more careful mode, then compare the advice each gives, making AI sycophancy, the tendency to flatter and agree at the expense of accuracy, something they can hear for themselves. It builds on a pilot study in which primary-aged children acted as "expert informants" on AI risks and showed they could reason about authenticity, trust and bias; the research is being presented at the Association of Internet Researchers conference this month.

**Why it matters for K-12:** Critical AI literacy for young children is usually a policy aspiration with no classroom materials behind it. This is a rare, free, Australian-built and research-grounded exception, designed for supervised use and pitched squarely at the primary years. For a Year 5 classroom it is close to a ready-made lesson: experience both modes, discuss why a machine might agree with you, and anchor the rule that agreement is not evidence. It also demonstrates a transferable design pattern, letting students experience an AI failure mode safely rather than being lectured about it, which the team plans to extend to misinformation, deepfakes and privacy.

Sources: [Helping kids question AI: interactive voice-based chatbot explores trust, accuracy and advice](https://educationhq.com/news/helping-kids-question-ai-interactive-voice-based-chatbot-explores-trust-accuracy-and-advice-215786/) -- EducationHQ, 9 October 2026.

### 4. "Cart before the horse?": the Australian evidence debate on classroom AI sharpens

**The Educator K-12 | 7 October 2026**

A substantial feature pulled together the state of the Australian argument over whether schools adopted AI ahead of the evidence. On the caution side: the University of Sydney and Barker Institute review finding little local evidence and engagement gains that do not clearly convert into learning, CQU work linking heavy AI essay use to weaker neural activity and recall, PISA 2025 data showing students who drafted writing with AI did worse, and federal minister Jason Clare's "digital detox" language alongside NSW's NESA review. On the other side, figures including UCL's Professor Rose Luckin oppose any blanket pause, arguing for tools designed to give hints rather than answers and for teaching students to use AI well, while school-sector voices argue the horse has bolted and governance plus judgement is the only workable response.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This is the single best current map of the terrain the reader operates in, and its practical convergence point is useful: almost every voice quoted, on both sides, lands on the same two moves, protect tasks where students must think unaided, and teach critical use of AI everywhere else. That convergence, rather than the pause-versus-adopt headline, is what is worth taking to the principal. The feature also confirms how fast the policy layer is moving underneath schools: pen-and-paper NAPLAN is under ministerial discussion and NESA's review is live, so school-level positions written this term should anticipate supervised-assessment requirements rather than react to them.

Sources: [Exclusive: Have schools gone 'cart before the horse' with AI?](https://www.theeducatoronline.com/k12/news/exclusive-have-schools-gone-cart-before-the-horse-with-ai/289182) -- The Educator K-12, 7 October 2026.

### 5. UK universities begin walking away from Turnitin as the AI-detection retreat accelerates

**Times Higher Education | 9 October 2026**

Newcastle University will phase out Turnitin after 2026-27, Westminster will let its contract lapse in July 2027, and Southampton announced its exit earlier this year, with Aberdeen reviewing and Cambridge publicly refusing any licence changes that touch data privacy or intellectual property. The stated reasons stack up: Turnitin's proposed licence changes raised concerns about student data being used for AI purposes, Westminster called the AI detector "probabilistic rather than conclusive" with a recognised false-positive risk, and sector voices note the originality checker is largely ineffective against generative AI anyway. Turnitin postponed its licence changes to September 2027 after sector pushback.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The institutions with the largest integrity caseloads in the English-speaking world are concluding that detection software is not worth its cost, its error rate or its data terms, and that assessment design is where integrity actually lives. That is the same position NESA has already signalled to NSW schools, so this is confirmation, not news, of the direction: any school still leaning on a detector score as evidence in a misconduct conversation is now out of step with both its regulator and the research. The data angle deserves separate attention, as the trigger for Newcastle was a vendor seeking rights over student work for AI purposes, exactly the clause to check for in every school edtech renewal.

Sources: [More universities end Turnitin contracts as concerns mount](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/745401) -- Times Higher Education, 9 October 2026; [Unis and schools are moving away from AI detection software. They should stop using it altogether](https://educationhq.com/news/unis-and-schools-are-moving-away-from-ai-detection-software-they-should-stop-using-it-altogether-215462/) -- Mark Bassett (Charles Sturt University) via EducationHQ, 29 September 2026.

## Worth Watching

**1. New York City has given itself a year to write K-12 AI guidelines, under a moratorium**

Education Week's Market Brief reports that New York City, having imposed a moratorium on generative AI for younger students, is now running a year-long process to decide what K-12 AI guidelines should look like. NYC is the largest school district in the US, and this is the first large-scale, deliberate test of the "pause first, design the rules, then re-introduce" model that some Australian voices are urging. How it handles teacher voice, age thresholds and vendor rules will be quotable evidence for whichever side of the local debate it favours.

Source: [What Should AI Guidelines for K-12 Look Like? New York City Has a Year to Figure It Out](https://marketbrief.edweek.org/regulation-policy/what-should-ai-guidelines-for-k-12-look-like-new-york-city-has-a-year-to-figure-it-out/2026/10) -- EdWeek Market Brief, October 2026.

**2. The Digital Duty of Care Bill would shift the safety burden from schools to platforms**

The Alannah & Madeline Foundation's consultation findings this week, drawn from more than 800 students, feed directly into the federal Online Safety Amendment (Digital Duty of Care) Bill 2026, which would oblige online services, AI tools included, to prevent foreseeable harm before it reaches children. Schools currently absorb the downstream cost of platform harms; a duty of care with real penalties would be the first structural correction to that, and its definitions of foreseeable harm will matter for AI companions in particular. Worth tracking the bill's progress and the sector submissions.

Source: [Students say online platforms are falling short on safety](https://www.theeducatoronline.com/k12/news/students-say-online-platforms-are-falling-short-on-safety/289251) -- The Educator K-12, 9 October 2026.
