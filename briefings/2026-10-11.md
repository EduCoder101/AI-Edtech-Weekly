# AI in EdTech Weekly -- 11 October 2026

*A research briefing for the AI Integration Lead, Sydney independent K-12 school. Covering 5--11 October 2026.*

---

## Top Stories

### 1. NESA's take-home assessment cap is now in force for HSC courses

**NSW Education Standards Authority | From Term 4, 2026**

The cap announced by NESA in August is no longer prospective: from the start of Term 4 this week, HSC courses at every NSW school -- including independents -- are limited to one take-home assessment task, worth a maximum of 15 per cent of the school-based assessment mark. Because school-based assessment comprises half the HSC result, the cap works out to a maximum of approximately 7.5 per cent of the total HSC mark coming from unsupervised work. The ACE Rules were updated before the end of Term 3 to reflect the change (NESA 34/26). Preliminary courses follow from Term 1, 2027. Courses with a major project or HSC practical exam -- Visual Arts, Drama, Music 1 and 2, Design and Technology -- are exempt.

The ACT reviewed the NSW approach but has not yet followed it. An ACT government spokesperson noted that academic integrity breaches across the territory represent about 0.1 per cent of all assessments. University of Canberra lecturer Dr Beth Chapman has argued publicly that "returning to a 1996 assessment model is not going to solve a 2026 problem" and called for AI guidelines instead.

**Why it matters for K-12:** Independent schools in NSW need to confirm their Term 4 HSC assessment programs comply now, not at the end of term. The practical question for teachers is how to redesign tasks that previously carried more weight: shifting to in-class oral presentations, practical demonstrations, or supervised drafting processes. For Year 5 and primary settings the direct compliance obligation does not apply, but the design principles that drove the cap -- building tasks that demonstrate process and cognition rather than product -- are worth embedding across all year levels before they become a regulatory requirement.

### 2. Common Sense Media rates ChatGPT for Teens an "Unacceptable Risk"

**Common Sense Media Youth AI Safety Institute | 7 October 2026**

Common Sense Media's Youth AI Safety Institute tested more than 4,000 prompts on ChatGPT accounts registered to teenagers and rated the product "Unacceptable Risk" for all users under 18. The headline finding: in testing, a teen could spend an hour discussing self-harm without a single parental alert being sent. The Institute also found that ChatGPT missed more than one in four crisis referrals it had itself determined were warranted. The report recommends that ChatGPT remain restricted to adults only until OpenAI demonstrates, through independent auditing, that the safeguards work as described.

OpenAI disputed the findings. The company said the testing did not accurately represent how the teen safeguards work in practice and suggested the parental alert testing may have been conducted before the teen and parent accounts were fully linked. OpenAI released a separate post on October 7 titled "Helping teens learn, plan, and shape the future of AI."

**Why it matters for K-12:** OpenAI's teen-mode product is now publicly available and is the kind of product parents and older students will reach for independently. The gap between what vendor marketing says about teen safety features and what systematic testing finds is precisely the domain where a school's AI framework needs to speak clearly. For schools currently using or considering ChatGPT Education licenses, the Common Sense finding is a prompt to check: (a) what parental notification settings are active in your tenancy, and (b) what guidance students have received about using personal or free accounts outside the school environment. Australia's eSafety Commissioner has previously flagged the same "parental controls that don't work in practice" pattern for AI companion apps.

Sources: [ChatGPT for Teens Poses Unacceptable Risk to Kids, Common Sense Media Finds](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/press-releases/chatgpt-for-teens-poses-unacceptable-risk-to-kids-common-sense-media-finds) -- Common Sense Media, 7 October 2026; [AI Risk Assessment: ChatGPT for Teens](https://institute.commonsensemedia.org/risk-assessments/chatgpt-teens) -- Common Sense Media Youth AI Safety Institute, October 2026; [ChatGPT for Teens is not necessarily 'safer than the previous version,' Common Sense Media finds](https://www.cnbc.com/make-it/2026/10/09/chatgpt-for-teens-safety-common-sense-media.html) -- CNBC Make It, 9 October 2026; [Common Sense Media rates ChatGPT for Teens an unacceptable risk](https://qz.com/common-sense-media-chatgpt-teens-unacceptable-risk-100726) -- Quartz, October 2026.

### 3. New US survey: AI is widespread in K-12 but teachers say training and time are not

**Hanover Research | 8 October 2026**

Hanover Research released findings from a national survey of nearly 300 parents and educators, conducted in August 2026. The headline figure: 54 per cent of parents and staff report that students already use AI tools for schoolwork. Yet the survey also shows that teacher support has not kept pace with adoption. Forty-one per cent of staff say they need to make significant changes to their teaching practices to use AI effectively, only 50 per cent feel they have had enough time to teach students AI skills, and 67 per cent want more AI training for themselves. A companion Hanover report on AI integration trends adds that most parents and staff say students have received more instruction on *when* to use AI than on *how* to use it.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The pattern here -- widespread use, underprepared teachers, and guidance that covers the rule but not the skill -- is consistent with what Australian surveys have found in this school year. The 67 per cent wanting more training is the actionable number: it is not a finding about resistant staff, it is a finding about staff who are trying but lack structured support. This directly informs the argument for a sustained, curriculum-integrated professional learning programme rather than a one-off AI briefing session.

Sources: [Hanover Research Releases National Survey Results on AI Use and Concerns in K-12 Education](https://www.hanoverresearch.com/press-releases/k-12-education/hanover-research-releases-national-survey-results-on-ai-in-k-12-education/) -- Hanover Research, 8 October 2026; [K-12 AI Integration Trends: Insights on the Use of Artificial Intelligence in Schools](https://www.hanoverresearch.com/reports-and-briefs/k-12-education/k-12-ai-integration-trends/) -- Hanover Research, 2026.

### 4. Stanford review: only 20 causal studies on AI in K-12 exist, and teacher involvement is the main predictor of positive outcomes

**Stanford SCALE Initiative | 2026**

Stanford's Center to Accelerate Learning (SCALE) published a systematic review of the evidence base for AI in K-12 education, screening more than 800 papers and identifying just 20 high-quality causal studies that rigorously measure how AI tools affect outcomes for students or educators. The review found no such high-quality studies conducted in US K-12 school settings. The headline synthesis: teacher-mediated and AI-augmented tutoring may promote student learning, while student-facing AI tools -- tools used independently, without teacher involvement -- show mixed effects, and some gains disappear when students are assessed without AI support.

The review also flags an adoption-evidence gap: survey data show 54 per cent of middle and high school students report using AI for school, yet the number of rigorous studies measuring impact remains at 20.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This is the most credible summary of what is and is not known about AI in schools, and it is directly useful in three ways. First, it supports the case for teacher-mediated rather than student-autonomous AI use, which aligns with both NESA's assessment shift and the SA Royal Commission's likely framing. Second, the finding that gains can disappear when AI support is removed is the cognitive-dependency risk in rigorous form -- useful in any parent communication or curriculum discussion. Third, for a school making procurement decisions, the 20-studies figure is a reminder that vendor claims about learning outcomes almost certainly outrun the actual evidence.

Sources: [Understanding the Evidence Base on AI in K-12 Education](https://scale.stanford.edu/research-in-action/understanding-evidence-base-ai-k12-education) -- Stanford SCALE Initiative, 2026; [The Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A 2026 Review](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20Report.pdf) -- Fesler, Martinez, Agnew and Loeb, Stanford SCALE, 2026; [Stanford report questions AI impact in K-12 education](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/stanford-report-finds-limited-evidence-behind-ai-impact-in-k-12-classrooms) -- EdTech Innovation Hub, 2026.

### 5. WEF expert calls for AI literacy in school curricula alongside child safety measures

**World Economic Forum / Business Standard | 11 October 2026**

Cathy Li, Head of AI, Data and the Metaverse at the World Economic Forum, published commentary this week calling for governments and schools to give equal weight to AI innovation and child safety, and arguing that AI literacy must be integrated into school curricula rather than left as an optional add-on. Li's core argument: children are already using AI at scale -- a February 2026 eSafety Commissioner survey found 78 per cent of Australian children aged 10 to 17 had used an AI assistant -- and the choice is not between exposure and protection but between structured and unstructured engagement.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The argument maps directly onto the tension visible in Australian schools this term: jurisdictions are responding to AI use through assessment restriction (NSW) and chatbot safety enforcement (eSafety), while the WEF and UNESCO position is that these responses work only if paired with genuine AI literacy built into normal curriculum delivery. For a Year 5 classroom, the practical question is what structured AI use looks like at that age level: guided co-writing, explicit critique of AI output, and age-appropriate conversations about how AI works are the options most supported by the emerging evidence.

Sources: [Children and AI: WEF expert calls for equal focus on innovation, child safety](https://business-standard.com/technology/artificial-intelligence/children-ai-wef-expert-calls-for-equal-focus-on-innovation-child-safety-126101100186_1.html) -- Business Standard, 11 October 2026; [AI assistants now used by 4 in 5 Australian children](https://www.theeducatoronline.com/k12/news/ai-assistants-now-used-by-4-in-5-australian-children/289101) -- The Educator K-12, 2026; [Talking to Machines survey findings](https://www.esafety.gov.au/newsroom/blogs/ai-chatbots-and-companions-risks-to-children-and-young-people) -- eSafety Commissioner, 2026.

## Worth Watching

**1. SA Royal Commission education issues paper -- expected shortly**

The SA Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence, which commenced on 1 October with a mandate to put education first, has not yet published its first issues paper. When it does, the window for structured input from the independent school sector will open quickly. Given the commission's compressed nine-month timeline (final report due 1 July 2027), expect the paper before the end of October. ISA and AISNSW remain the practical route for coordinating a sector contribution; the most useful input an independent school can provide is documented evidence from actual classroom AI integration rather than policy opinion.

Sources: [Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence](https://ai.sa.gov.au/royal-commission-into-artificial-intelligence) -- SA Government Office for AI; [SA Premier announces three experts to lead Royal Commission into AI](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-10-01/three-experts-appointed-to-lead-sa-s-royal-commission-into-ai/107215456) -- ABC News, 1 October 2026.

**2. Federal Joint Select Committee on AI reports in seven weeks**

The Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence, whose terms of reference include the adequacy of Australia's education system for an AI-transformed workforce, is due to report by 30 November 2026. Submissions closed on 14 September. With the SA Royal Commission also active, two significant federal and state-level processes will produce recommendations within the same six-week window. Schools should track both for signals about where binding policy is heading in 2027.

Sources: [Establishment of a Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence](https://www.liberal.org.au/2026/08/20/establishment-of-a-joint-select-committee-on-artificial-intelligence) -- Liberal Party of Australia, 20 August 2026; [From patchwork to playbook: the Joint Select Committee on AI and what it means for business](https://www.claytonutz.com/insights/2026/august/from-patchwork-to-playbook-the-joint-select-committee-on-ai-and-what-it-means-for-business) -- Clayton Utz, August 2026.
