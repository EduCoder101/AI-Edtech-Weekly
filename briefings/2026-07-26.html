A quiet week in Canberra, but AI companions consolidate as the next safeguarding frontier and parents converge with teachers on guardrails

Issue of 26 July 2026

At a glance

With no new federal moves ahead of August's National Cabinet, the running was made by researchers and platforms. Australian academics analysed China's new AI companion law, in force since 15 July, in the same week the eSafety Commissioner ran educator training on companion chatbots and deepfakes. Instructure's 2026 survey shows parents, teachers and students now share the same top three AI concerns, and OpenAI let parents lock teen ChatGPT accounts to its Socratic Study Mode by default.

Key signals

  • China's AI companion law took effect on 15 July, forcing major companion services to restrict emotionally responsive chat; University of Tasmania and RMIT-linked researchers warned in The Conversation (23 July) of an AI-induced 'loneliness spiral' among young users.
  • The eSafety Commissioner ran a professional learning webinar for educators on 23 July covering AI companions, deepfakes, AI influencers and AI-facilitated cyberbullying.
  • Instructure's State of AI in Education 2026 survey (21 July) found 45% of K-12 educators have had no formal AI training, and only 8% call their training comprehensive.
  • The same survey found parents, educators and students share the same top concerns (overreliance, loss of critical thinking, integrity) and all accept AI for support tasks but not for grading decisions.
  • OpenAI's teen safety rollout lets parents make Study Mode the default for linked teen accounts, drawing a moratorium call from advocacy group Schools Beyond Screens.

Considerations for the Executive

  • Treat AI companion use as a wellbeing and pastoral care matter now: brief wellbeing staff and consider the eSafety educator webinar series before an incident forces the issue.
  • Use the Instructure findings in parent communication; the community consensus on guardrails rather than bans makes this a good moment to socialise the school's AI position.
  • Note the parent-controlled Study Mode default when advising families: home AI use may already be more structured than school guidance assumes.

1

AI companions come under the regulatory microscope, and Australian researchers ask what schools should learn

The Conversation | 23 July 2026

Australian academics Joseph Crawford (University of Tasmania) and Michael Cowling published an analysis this week of China's new AI companion law, which took effect on 15 July and forced major companion services including features of Doubao and Qwen to shut down or restrict emotionally responsive chat, with millions of users losing conversation histories. The authors examine what Australia, which has led internationally on youth social media restrictions, should take from China's harder line. They warn of an AI-induced "loneliness spiral": lonely and socially anxious young people turn to conversational AI for relief, which can displace human friendships and deepen the original problem. In the same week, the eSafety Commissioner ran a professional learning webinar for educators (23 July) covering AI companion chatbots, sexual and non-sexual deepfakes, AI influencers, and AI-facilitated cyberbullying.

Sources: China has cracked down on AI companions. What can we learn from this? – The Conversation, 23 July 2026; China AI Companion Law Takes Effect – Tech Times, 15 July 2026; Professional learning for educators – eSafety Commissioner, webinar 23 July 2026.

Why it mattersAI companions are the fastest-moving child-safety issue in the AI space, and this week shows both ends of the response spectrum: China has legislated bluntly, while Australia is so far working through eSafety enforcement notices and educator capability. For a school, the practical takeaway is that companion use is a wellbeing issue, not a technology issue: it belongs in pastoral care and parent communication, not just the acceptable use policy. The eSafety educator webinar programme is a low-cost way to build staff awareness before an incident forces the conversation.
2

Instructure's 2026 State of AI in Education survey: parents, teachers and students now share the same three worries

Instructure / K-12 Dive | 21 July 2026

Instructure released its State of AI in Education 2026 survey at InstructureCon on 21 July, drawing on 1,125 US respondents across K-12 educators, higher education students and K-12 parents. Use is now mainstream: 68% of K-12 educators and 73% of parents report using AI or seeing their children use it at least occasionally. Strikingly, all three groups converge on the same top concerns: overreliance on AI, loss of critical thinking and deep learning, and academic integrity. Around 60-69% across groups worry that AI "sounds confident even when wrong". All groups accept AI more readily for support tasks (explaining concepts, brainstorming, finding resources) than for grading or academic decisions. The training gap remains stark: 45% of K-12 educators report no formal AI training, and only 8% describe their training as comprehensive.

Sources: AI in education research: what the data shows – Instructure, 21 July 2026; AI embraced by more students and educators, Instructure finds – K-12 Dive, July 2026; Survey: Gap exists between students, educators when it comes to using, understanding AI – Deseret News, 22 July 2026.

Why it mattersThe alignment between parents and teachers is the useful finding. A school proposing clear guardrails (AI for support tasks, never for assessment decisions, always with teacher oversight) is now pushing on an open door with its parent community, and the survey gives concrete language for that conversation. The training figures also give benchmarking ammunition for a professional learning budget: if under one in ten teachers anywhere describe their AI training as comprehensive, a school that invests properly here differentiates itself.
3

OpenAI consolidates teen safety controls and lets parents make Study Mode the default

OpenAI | 13-19 July 2026

Just ahead of this coverage window, and not covered in last week's issue, OpenAI rolled out a consolidated set of teen-focused changes under a policy post titled "Why teens deserve access to safe AI" (16 July). Parents with linked teen accounts can now make Study Mode, the Socratic tutoring feature that guides rather than answers, the default for every new chat their teen starts. Parental safety alerts were widened on 13 July to include accounts deactivated for violent threats, and teens now get more frequent break reminders and education-focused starter prompts. Coverage and analysis of the changes continued through this week. The launch drew immediate criticism from advocacy group Schools Beyond Screens, which called on 16 July for a moratorium on default-on AI tools on school-issued devices.

Sources: OpenAI Expands Teen Safety Features and Study Mode in ChatGPT – The University Network, July 2026; ChatGPT Introduces Teen Safety Features with Break Reminders, Study Mode, and Quiet Hours – iTech Post, 17 July 2026; OpenAI Parent Alerts Expand to Teen ChatGPT Violence Bans – TechMyMoney, 19 July 2026.

Why it mattersThis is the clearest signal yet that the major AI platforms expect regulated, parent-mediated teen access to become the norm, rather than age bans. For schools, the parent-controlled Study Mode default is worth knowing about in detail: it is the first mainstream mechanism that lets families enforce a pedagogically defensible mode of AI use at home, and parents will ask about it. It also complicates the school's messaging if school guidance treats ChatGPT as a single undifferentiated tool when home use may already be locked to a tutoring mode.
4

Teacher AI capability moves up the global policy agenda: Egypt launches a national competency framework, UNESCO opens a global ethics MOOC

UNESCO | 20-21 July 2026

Two UNESCO-linked developments this week point the same way. Egypt launched a national AI competency framework for teachers on 21 July, one of the first countries to translate UNESCO's 2024 AI competency framework for teachers into a binding national structure. A day earlier, UNESCO and LG AI Research launched a free global MOOC on the ethics of AI (20 July). These sit alongside the ongoing UNESCO-EU FutureProof Education project, which this month moved into a phase of turning teacher co-design workshops into practical toolkits for schools across six European systems.

Sources: Artificial intelligence in education – UNESCO (Egypt framework, 21 July 2026; ethics MOOC, 20 July 2026); UNESCO-EU school AI project moves to teacher-led toolkit phase – EdTech Innovation Hub, July 2026; UNESCO AI competency framework for teachers – UNESCO.

Why it mattersAustralia has a national framework for generative AI in schools but no national teacher AI competency framework; AITSL guidance remains advisory. When countries as different as Egypt, Ireland and North Rhine-Westphalia converge on formal teacher AI competency structures, it becomes more likely that Australian regulators follow, plausibly through AITSL standards or the promised national AI standards. A school that maps its professional learning against the UNESCO teacher competency framework now will find any future compliance exercise largely done.

Where experts disagree

Live debates worth understanding before deciding

How to respond to AI companion risks for young people

One viewChina's approach: legislate hard, shut down emotionally responsive chat for minors outright
The other viewCrawford and Cowling: blunt bans destroy user data and trust; targeted safeguards on dependency and displacement are more defensible
The tensionAustralia's National Cabinet process will have to pick a point between these poles, and the choice will shape what schools must police versus what platforms must prevent

Whether teens should have AI access at all on school devices

One viewOpenAI: "teens deserve access to safe AI"; the answer to risk is safer product design and parental controls
The other viewSchools Beyond Screens: default-on AI tools on district devices expose children to "known and lasting harms"; a moratorium is warranted
The tensionThe disagreement is about who bears the burden of proof: platforms claiming safety features suffice, or schools defaulting to off until harms are ruled out

What rising AI use means for thinking skills

One viewInstructure adoption data: use is now mainstream across educators, students and parents, and majorities see accessibility and time-saving benefits
The other viewThe same survey's respondents: loss of critical thinking and deep learning is a top-three concern for every group, with about 60% of K-12 educators worried AI sounds confident while wrong
The tensionThe community using the tools most is the same community most worried about them; policy has to resolve a tension that exists within people, not just between camps

Worth watching

Developments to keep an eye on

Queensland's Artificial Intelligence in Education Conference, Cairns, 29 July

Queensland's education department holds its second AI in Education conference in Cairns on 29 July, following the statewide rollout of Corella, its government-owned AI assistant for teachers and students. Watch for implementation lessons from the first cohort of Corella schools: Queensland is running the largest state-built classroom AI deployment outside NSW, and what surfaces here tends to reach the independent sector's conversation a term later.

Sources: Artificial Intelligence in Education Conference, Queensland – Informa/Queensland Department of Education, 29 July 2026.

National Cabinet's August meeting on draft national AI standards

Carried over from last week and now imminent: the draft framework of legislated national AI standards, explicitly covering education and training, goes to National Cabinet in August. No new detail emerged this week, which itself is worth noting; schools should expect the substance to land with little additional warning. The open questions remain whether independent schools are named alongside government schools and whether teacher and student AI literacy appear as obligations.

Sources: AI in Australia's Interests – Prime Minister of Australia, 15 July 2026.
