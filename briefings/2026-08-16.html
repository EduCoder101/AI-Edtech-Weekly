Weekly briefing · 10 August – 16 August 2026

NSW moves to end unsupervised assessment as South Australia calls the nation's first AI royal commission, and the pastoral blind spot around AI companions widens

Issue of 16 August 2026

At a glance

Australian AI policy hardened in a single week: NSW asked NESA to consider a moratorium on unsupervised take-home assessment tasks from Term 4 while it reviews AI's effect on learning, and South Australia announced the country's first Royal Commission into AI with education squarely in scope. Federal Education Minister Jason Clare reinforced the direction, framing learning integrity and student privacy as the centre of the national response. Internationally, Stateline documented US districts spending billions on AI tools they cannot evaluate, and clinicians published sobering evidence on teenagers quietly using AI chatbots for emotional support. The message across all of it is that governance is moving from guidance to machinery, and schools should position ahead of binding rules.

Key signals

  • NSW Deputy Premier Prue Car asked NESA to urgently consider a moratorium on unsupervised, take-home assessment tasks while a broader review of AI's impact on learning runs, with interim changes possible from the start of Term 4 2026 and major projects expected to need separate arrangements.
  • South Australia announced Australia's first Royal Commission into AI on 10 August, covering education alongside work and public services, with commissioners starting 1 October 2026 and a final report due by 1 July 2027.
  • Learning First's NSW survey behind the assessment debate found 80 per cent of lower secondary teachers who report student AI use say students use it to complete assessment tasks, and about half of secondary teachers do not know how to prevent AI-enabled plagiarism.
  • Stateline's 12 August investigation found US districts choosing among an average of 3,001 available digital tools while students and educators actively use about four, with little independent evidence or federal guidance for AI purchases.
  • UMass Chan clinicians report 72 per cent of US teens have used AI companion chatbots and 63 per cent of those seeking mental health advice from a chatbot told no one, while Linewize found 60 per cent of schools say students confide in AI instead of adults.

Considerations for the Executive

  • Start contingency planning for Stage 6 assessment schedules now rather than waiting for NESA's advice: if the moratorium proceeds, supervised replacements for take-home tasks will need timetable space in Term 4, and the school should have an agreed position on the instructional-time trade-off before parents raise it.
  • Track the SA royal commission's terms of reference when they land next month and flag the submission window with the principal; a practising school's classroom evidence, routed directly or through ISA and AISNSW, will carry more weight than another peak-body position paper.
  • Brief wellbeing staff on the AI companion evidence and eSafety's information sheet, treating student reliance on AI chatbots as a disclosure pathway rather than a technology-use breach, and fold companionship (not just homework) into the next round of parent communication about AI.

1

NSW moves to bar unsupervised take-home assessments while NESA reviews AI's impact on learning

NSW Government / NESA | 9-11 August 2026

Deputy Premier and Education Minister Prue Car has asked NESA to urgently examine measures to protect student learning and the integrity of the HSC as AI use accelerates, and as an immediate step to consider a moratorium on unsupervised, take-home assessment tasks while a broader review runs. The review will examine the evidence on AI's effect on students' cognitive development and whether current assessment methods, including major projects and creative works, still safeguard authentic student work; NESA has also been asked to develop a common approach schools can use to identify inappropriate AI use. Subject to NESA's advice, interim changes would land by the start of Term 4 2026, giving the HSC class of 2027 certainty before their final year, with major projects in subjects like visual arts and design and technology expected to need separate arrangements rather than a blanket ban. Car's framing: "If students rely on AI to bypass that learning process, we risk undermining both their education and the integrity of the HSC." The evidence trail behind the move includes Learning First's NSW survey of almost 3,400 teachers and 750 school leaders (May 2026), in which 80 per cent of lower secondary teachers reporting student AI use said students use it to complete assessment tasks, and about half of secondary teachers said they do not know how to prevent AI-enabled plagiarism.

Sources: Minns Government examines measures to halt AI's impact on cognitive development of a generation of NSW students – NSW Government, c. 10 August 2026; NSW plans to ban take-home assessments over AI concerns – The Daily Aus, 11 August 2026; Matter of urgency: NSW assessment overhaul aiming to curb AI in schools – EducationHQ, c. 11 August 2026; 'Act now': assessment overhaul to curb AI in schools – AAP via The Canberra Times, c. 10-11 August 2026.

Why it mattersThis is the most consequential Australian assessment decision of the year so far, and it will not stay contained to Year 12: NESA's "common approach" to identifying inappropriate AI use would flow to every NSW school, including independents, and the moratorium logic (supervise the assessment rather than trust detection) is exactly the direction independent schools have been debating internally. Two practical notes: any school with Stage 6 students should be planning for a Term 4 shift in assessment schedules now rather than waiting for NESA's advice, and the critique already circulating (moving take-home tasks into class time consumes instructional time and narrows the skills assessed) is worth having an agreed school position on before parents ask.
2

South Australia announces the country's first royal commission into AI, with education in scope

SA Government | 10 August 2026

Premier Peter Malinauskas announced a Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence, the first inquiry into AI at that level anywhere in Australia. It will examine AI's effect on work, the creative industries, education and public services, with terms of reference due within four to six weeks, an international search underway for three commissioners, hearings starting 1 October 2026, and a final report due by 1 July 2027. Malinauskas put schooling at the centre of his pitch, saying he worries about "kids graduating from high school and they've learned how to use ChatGPT, but not how to read, write, and do arithmetic", while also saying he wants students taught to use AI within a regulated framework.

Sources: Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence announced – SA Department of the Premier and Cabinet, 10 August 2026; Australia is getting its first major inquiry into AI. It's starting in South Australia – SBS News, c. 10-11 August 2026; SA royal commission to test AI's limits – The Mandarin, c. 11 August 2026; SA to hold AI royal commission – Government News, August 2026.

Why it mattersA royal commission is the heaviest inquiry instrument an Australian government holds, and this one will compel evidence on exactly the questions schools are wrestling with: cognitive effects, assessment, teacher workload and children's safety. Its findings will not bind other states, but royal commission reports habitually set the national agenda (and the education terms of reference, due next month, will show how central schooling is to it). Worth noting the contrast with NSW in the same week: one state is acting first and reviewing second, the other is building a year-long evidence base before regulating. Submissions will open after October; the sector bodies (ISA, AISNSW) will almost certainly make them, and a practising school voice with real classroom evidence would carry weight.
3

Federal signals: Clare puts learning integrity and privacy at the centre, with a new commission coming

The Educator / Ministers' Media Centre | coverage published this week (event 7 August 2026)

Sector coverage this week reported federal Education Minister Jason Clare's remarks at the Australian Education Awards in Sydney, where he named AI in the classroom as one of the three pressing issues facing Australian schools, alongside the new Australian Teaching and Learning Commission and persistent inequity. On AI his line was blunt: "It's not going away. We've got to make sure that we use it in a way that helps young people to learn. It doesn't replace their learning", with the policy response to centre on learning integrity and student privacy. The Teaching and Learning Commission, agreed in principle by education ministers to better connect curriculum, teaching, assessment, research and reporting, is the structure most likely to carry national AI-in-schools work forward.

Sources: Education Minister Jason Clare says AI must support learning, not replace it – The Educator K/12, c. 10-11 August 2026; Australian Education Awards 2026 Opening Address – Ministers' Media Centre, 7 August 2026; National teaching, learning commission to bring greater coordination – The Mandarin.

Why it mattersPut alongside NSW's assessment move and SA's royal commission, the federal minister's framing completes a picture: every level of Australian government is now converging on integrity and student protection as the organising principles for school AI policy, not innovation or productivity. For a school writing or revising its AI policy this term, aligning its stated principles with that language is low-cost insurance that the policy will still look right when national settings firm up.
4

US districts are spending billions on AI with almost nothing to guide what is worth buying

Stateline | 12 August 2026

Stateline's investigation of US school AI procurement found districts deciding which AI tools are safe and worth thousands or millions of dollars with little independent evidence and, per the report, minimal guidance from the US Department of Education. The US edtech market generated almost USD 48 billion in 2024 and is projected past USD 90 billion by 2030, yet the ground-level picture is chaotic: an Instructure analysis found districts had access to an average of 3,001 digital tools while students and educators actively used about four. The report's practical recommendations are notably modest: let adults test AI tools thoroughly before students touch them, and require teachers to review AI-generated material before classroom use.

Sources: Schools spend billions on AI, but struggle to figure out what's worth buying – Stateline, 12 August 2026; Schools spend billions on AI, but struggle to figure out what's worth it – District Administration, August 2026.

Why it mattersThe 3,001-versus-4 figure is the sharpest procurement statistic of the year and translates directly to the Australian context, where the vendor push into schools is just as intense and the independent evidence base just as thin. It strengthens the case for a small, deliberately curated AI toolset with proper evaluation, over accumulating licences. The two recommendations (adults first, human review of AI output) are also usefully quotable as minimum standards in any school's AI procurement or acceptable-use documents.
5

Clinicians set out the evidence on teens using AI chatbots for emotional support

The Conversation (UMass Chan Medical School) | 13 August 2026

Three child psychiatry and psychology researchers at UMass Chan Medical School published an evidence summary on adolescents' use of AI chatbots for emotional support: 72 per cent of US teens have interacted with AI companion chatbots, 13 to 20 per cent sought AI support for emotional concerns within a two-month period in 2025, and in one recent study 63 per cent of teens and young adults who used a chatbot for mental health advice had told no one. The risks they document include inaccurate advice, emotional dependence, reduced human connection and inadequate responses to self-harm disclosures, because general-purpose chatbots were built to simulate empathy, not to provide validated mental health support. This lands alongside school-side data from Linewize's See the Signs survey of nearly 1,000 schools across the US, UK, Australia and New Zealand: 60 per cent of US schools say students confide in AI tools instead of teachers, counsellors or parents, and almost half have observed students forming emotional attachments to AI companions.

Sources: Teens are turning to AI chatbots for emotional support – here's how to keep kids safe – The Conversation (Moore, Kelly and Dvir, UMass Chan Medical School), 13 August 2026; New Linewize Survey Finds 90% of U.S. Schools Concerned About Online Mental Health Impacts as Students Turn to AI Chatbots for Support – Linewize / PR Newswire, survey published January 2026; AI companions: information sheet – eSafety Commissioner.

Why it mattersThe pastoral dimension of AI is arriving faster than the academic one for many schools, and mostly invisibly, since the defining finding is that students do not tell adults. Concrete implications: wellbeing staff should know the major companion apps and their (lack of) crisis safeguards, pastoral protocols should treat "student relies on an AI companion" as a disclosure pathway rather than a discipline matter, and parent communication about AI should cover companionship, not just homework. eSafety's AI companions information sheet is the ready-made Australian resource to attach to any of that work.

Worth watching

Developments to keep an eye on

National Cabinet and the national AI standards: still no reported outcome, fifth week on watch

The Prime Minister's 15 July commitment to take the proposed Australian AI standards to National Cabinet in August, coordinated by the new Office of AI whose remit explicitly spans education, has still produced no public outcome as at 16 August. With NSW and SA now moving unilaterally, the federal decision, when it lands, will determine whether Australia gets a coordinated national approach to AI in schools or a patchwork of state responses. The education provisions remain the thing to read first.

Sources: AI in Australia's Interests – Prime Minister of Australia, 15 July 2026; Australian Government announces mandatory AI standards for large-scale data centres and new Office of AI – Gilbert + Tobin, July 2026.

The EU quietly deferred its school AI rules, and vendors noticed

The EU AI Act's high-risk obligations for education (covering AI used in admissions, student evaluation and exam scoring) were due to bite on 2 August 2026, but the Digital Omnibus amendment approved in May pushed enforcement to December 2027, leaving only the lighter transparency duties in force. Flagged because the EU rules function as a de facto global compliance baseline for edtech vendors selling into Australia, and because the deferral is a live case study in the same act-now-versus-wait tension playing out between NSW and SA. The obligations themselves have not changed, only the date.

Sources: EU AI Act education deadline arrives, most rules deferred – WINS Solutions, c. early August 2026; The EU AI Act and assessment: December 2027 is not a snooze button – UNIwise, 2026.
