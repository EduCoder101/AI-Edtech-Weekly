# AI in EdTech Weekly -- 1 November 2026

*A research briefing for the AI Integration Lead, Sydney independent K-12 school. Covering 25 October -- 1 November 2026.*

## Top Stories

### 1. South Australia's AI Royal Commission opens for public submissions

**Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence (SA) | 29 October 2026**

The first formal call for public submissions to South Australia's Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence was issued on 29 October 2026. The inquiry, which commenced 1 October and is chaired by former Federal Court judge Dr Iain Ross AO, is examining AI's implications for the state economy, workforce, education system and public services. Education -- including schools, higher education and vocational training -- is an explicit part of the terms of reference. The commission must deliver its final report to the SA government by 1 July 2027.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This is the first royal commission into AI anywhere in Australia, and its education findings will carry the weight of a formal judicial inquiry rather than a policy paper. The submissions process is now open, which means independent school associations, peak bodies such as ISA and NCEC, and individual schools can put evidence on the record. If this reader's school has experience to share on AI in Year 5 classrooms, academic integrity, or staff professional learning, this is an unusually direct channel to government. The commission's findings will likely inform SA state education policy from mid-2027, and other states will watch closely. Check [royalcommissionai.sa.gov.au](https://www.royalcommissionai.sa.gov.au/) for submission details and closing dates.

Sources: [Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence](https://www.royalcommissionai.sa.gov.au/) -- official site; [South Australia Launches AI Royal Commission](https://app.govly.com/public/signals/166059) -- Govly, October 2026; [South Australia names AI Royal Commission panel](https://ia.acs.org.au/article/2026/south-australia-names-ai-royal-commission-panel.html) -- ACS, 2026.

### 2. NESA's HSC take-home assessment limits now in effect for Term 4

**NESA / The Educator K-12 / The Mandarin | October 2026**

NESA's new rules limiting take-home tasks in Stage 6 school-based assessment took effect at the start of Term 4 2026, following a ministerial request in August. Under the new framework, HSC courses may include no more than one take-home task worth at most 15% of the school-based component; the remainder of assessment must occur in supervised conditions. The rules are expected to extend to Preliminary courses from Term 1 2027. NESA's guidance also clarifies that AI-detection software must be one input in a teacher's professional judgement, not the sole basis for an academic integrity finding.

**Why it matters for K-12:** Even for non-NSW schools, this is the clearest signal yet that a major Australian curriculum authority has concluded that AI detection is insufficiently reliable to underpin assessment integrity at scale, and has responded by restructuring the conditions of assessment rather than trying to ban or detect AI use. The practical lesson is instructive: if NSW is moving toward supervised conditions as the integrity backstop, other states are likely to follow, and independent schools in all jurisdictions should be auditing whether their current assessment programs would hold up under similar scrutiny. The detection-as-one-input guidance also reduces the risk of a false positive harming a student, which is a safeguard worth mirroring in school policy.

Sources: [NSW moves to protect AI academic integrity in HSC assessments](https://www.theeducatoronline.com/k12/news/nsw-moves-to-protect-ai-academic-integrity-in-hsc-assessments/289112) -- The Educator K-12; [HSC class of '27 on notice: NESA told to consider impact of AI on learning](https://www.themandarin.com.au/318000-hsc-class-of-27-on-notice-nesa-told-to-consider-impact-of-ai-in-schools/) -- The Mandarin; [Use of AI by students for the HSC](https://www.nsw.gov.au/education-and-training/nesa/hsc/2027-rules-and-procedures/artificial-intelligence) -- NESA, 2026.

### 3. US state survey: AI is top edtech priority but most states cannot measure if it works

**SETDA (State Educational Technology Directors Association) | 29 October 2026**

SETDA's fifth annual State EdTech Trends Report, released 29 October, found that 79% of US state technology leaders named AI a top priority -- up from 68% in 2025 -- and 83% of states now have an active AI initiative. At the same time, 52% of states said their state collects no data on the use or effectiveness of edtech tools. On procurement, 53% of states have published guidance for buying AI-enabled tools, and 79% of that guidance now includes evidence standards, up from 45% a year earlier.

**Why it matters for K-12:** Australia does not have a comparable national survey, but the US pattern -- rapid adoption combined with almost no measurement infrastructure -- mirrors what AERO reported for Australian schools in October. The finding that 52% of states collect no effectiveness data should prompt Australian school leaders to ask the same question of themselves: what data are we actually collecting on whether the AI tools we deploy improve learning? The rapid rise in evidence standards for AI procurement (79% of buying guidance, up from 45%) also signals a direction of travel: expect Australian state governments to move toward evidence requirements for edtech purchasing, which will affect how schools evaluate and justify their tool selections.

Sources: [New Report: AI Remains Top State EdTech Priority Amid Growing Debate About Technology in Schools](https://prnewswire.com/news-releases/new-report-ai-remains-top-state-edtech-priority-amid-growing-debate-about-technology-in-schools-302923698.html) -- PR Newswire / SETDA, 29 October 2026.

### 4. UNESCO updates guidance on AI competency frameworks for teachers and students

**UNESCO | 27 October 2026**

A UNESCO article updated 27 October 2026 sets out recommendations for how countries should use its two AI competency frameworks -- one for students, one for teachers -- published in 2024. The article recommends folding the frameworks into a broader AI capacity-building strategy that includes universal internet access and ethical design requirements for AI tools used in education. UNESCO cautions explicitly against using AI to paper over teacher shortages or infrastructure gaps, and notes that few countries have yet translated the competency frameworks into national training programmes.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The UNESCO frameworks are the international reference point most likely to be adopted by AITSL when it eventually updates AI-related teacher standards, in the same way that AITSL drew on UNESCO digital literacy guidance previously. The student framework's four areas -- human-centred mindset, ethics of AI, AI techniques and applications, and AI system design -- are a useful scaffold for whole-school curriculum mapping, particularly for primary teachers planning AI literacy units. The caution against using AI to compensate for structural gaps is also worth noting: it supports the case for investing in teacher professional learning rather than substituting AI tools for teacher time.

Sources: [What you need to know about UNESCO's new AI competency frameworks for students and teachers](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/what-you-need-know-about-unescos-new-ai-competency-frameworks-students-and-teachers) -- UNESCO, updated 27 October 2026; [AI and the future of education](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/ai-and-future-education-disruptions-dilemmas-and-directions) -- UNESCO.

### 5. US research: schools experimenting with AI but struggling to determine whether it helps or harms

**WBUR Here and Now | 29 October 2026**

A WBUR Here and Now segment from 29 October reports that US schools have moved rapidly into AI experimentation but have limited tools to assess whether the technology is benefiting or harming students. The segment drew on perspectives from school leaders, researchers and students, and found that the core difficulty is a lack of standardised approaches to measuring AI's effect on learning, motivation and long-term retention. This is consistent with findings from the Stanford SCALE lab's 2026 review, which examined 818 papers and identified strong causal evidence in only 20 of them, and found gains frequently fail to persist after the tool is removed.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This story is worth reading as a candid field report from practitioners facing the same problem as Australian schools. The language "hard to tell if it helps or harms" is not defeatism -- it is a reminder that the absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and that any school claiming AI is "working" without a measurement framework is really saying it feels like it is working. The Stanford figure -- 20 high-quality causal studies out of 818 -- is a useful anchor for conversations with sceptical staff or board members who ask whether there is research support for AI in schools. There is some, but it is thin, and most of it does not apply to Australian primary classrooms.

Sources: [Schools struggle to measure AI's impact on students](https://wbur.org/hereandnow/2026/10/29/ai-students-impact) -- WBUR Here and Now, 29 October 2026; [Understanding the Evidence Base on AI in K-12 Education](https://scale.stanford.edu/research-in-action/understanding-evidence-base-ai-k12-education) -- Stanford SCALE, 2026.

## Worth Watching

**1. South Australia's Royal Commission submissions -- education sector engagement**

The first submissions round is now open. Schools, sector bodies and researchers have an opportunity to put evidence on the record before the first deadline. Given that the commission covers workforce, economy, education and public services, submissions that connect classroom AI experience to workforce preparation and community capability questions are most likely to land. Monitor the [official site](https://www.royalcommissionai.sa.gov.au/) for submission deadlines and any education-specific consultation sessions. If the SA commission produces strong school-sector submissions, it may become a reference document for the federal Joint Select Committee's final report in November.

**2. Google Gemini's ongoing integration into Google Classroom**

Google turned on Gemini AI features for K-12 students in Classroom in August 2026, with admin opt-in controls. Multiple reports in October suggest schools -- including those using Google Workspace for Education -- are still working through the implications, with some districts finding teachers were not trained before the rollout. The practical risk for any school on Google Workspace: AI features may be live in student accounts unless administrators have explicitly checked and configured their settings. This is a near-term action item, not a watch item, for any school IT or curriculum lead who has not already audited Gemini permissions in Google Admin.
