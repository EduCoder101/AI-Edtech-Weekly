Free national teacher training matures, UK legislates on AI and children, and evidence emerges of bias in AI writing feedback

Issue of 21 June 2026

At a glance

ESA and Microsoft released the second module of their free, nationally aligned AI professional learning series, moving from awareness to classroom implementation. A Stanford study found AI writing feedback tools systematically give less substantive critique to students identified as belonging to marginalised groups, and the UK passed the most direct AI-and-child-safety legislation in the English-speaking world.

Key signals

  • ESA Module 2 (AI Implementation) is free, aligned to the teacher standards and national framework, and counts towards professional development evidence.
  • Stanford's 'Marked Pedagogies' study: AI feedback tools praised more and critiqued less when essays were labelled as written by Black, Hispanic, Asian, female or learning-disabled students.
  • The UK's Children's Safety Act and AI Chatbot Powers Act give regulators explicit powers over AI chatbots that engage children, a model likely to influence Australian reform.
  • The OAIC's consultation drew 337 responses including 161 from primary-aged children, who clearly articulated wanting control and trust over their data.
  • eSafety hosted a free educator webinar on AI chatbots, deepfakes and AI-facilitated cyberbullying; the Children's Online Privacy Code consultation has now closed ahead of the December deadline.

Considerations for the Executive

  • Offer the free ESA modules as the baseline layer of the staff AI professional learning programme.
  • Require an equity impact check before adopting any AI feedback or marking tool, with monitoring of outcomes by student cohort.
  • Ask edtech vendors what Children's Online Privacy Code compliance work is under way ahead of December.

1

ESA and Microsoft release Module 2 of free national AI professional learning series for Australian teachers

Education Services Australia / Microsoft | June 2026

Education Services Australia, in partnership with Microsoft, has this month released the second module in its free national AI professional learning series. Module 1 (AI Readiness) covered foundational understanding of generative AI and how to evaluate AI-generated content for bias and inaccuracy. Module 2 – AI Implementation: Strategies for guiding students in safe and effective use of GenAI – is designed for teachers of students aged 13 and above and focuses on practical classroom integration: guiding students through AI-supported activities while keeping pedagogy and critical thinking at the centre. Together, the two modules run approximately three hours and can be completed independently online. Both are free for all teachers across Australia and New Zealand and are aligned to the Australian Professional Standards for Teachers and the national AI in Schools Framework. Live online sessions complement the self-paced modules. The series was commissioned following ESA research showing teacher confidence in AI use rising significantly after structured training.

Why it mattersThis is a genuinely useful, no-cost professional learning resource that any independent school can offer as a baseline before layering school-specific content. The fact that Module 2 focuses on implementation with students – not just teacher awareness – means it moves beyond introductory framing into the harder question of how to actually use AI well in a classroom. For those leading AI integration building a whole-school professional learning programme, this provides a nationally endorsed foundation that can be supplemented with context-specific work. The alignment to the Australian Framework and APST also means participation counts toward teachers' evidence of professional development.

Sources: Education Services Australia, "Training for teachers boosts confidence in safe and effective AI use in classrooms", June 2026; Microsoft Source Asia, "Upskilling Australia's educators: Microsoft partners to deliver essential AI training for teachers", 2025; The Educator K-12, "Inside Microsoft's big AI play for Australian classrooms", 2026.

2

eSafety Commissioner's June 25 webinar on AI chatbots and deepfakes – and what's next for the Children's Online Privacy Code

eSafety Commissioner / OAIC | June 2026

Two overlapping regulatory developments are moving simultaneously this week. First, the eSafety Commissioner is hosting a free webinar on Wednesday 25 June (3:45 pm AEST) for school educators covering AI companion chatbots, deepfakes, and AI-facilitated cyberbullying. The webinar follows the Commissioner's earlier warning this year that reports of AI-generated intimate images involving people under 18 have more than doubled over the preceding 18 months; it offers practical guidance for educators navigating these incidents. Second, the public consultation period on Australia's draft Children's Online Privacy Code closed on 5 June 2026. Developed by the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC), the Code is due to come into effect on 10 December 2026 and will impose new obligations on digital services accessed by children – including school management systems, edtech platforms, and AI-powered learning tools. The exposure draft signals tougher standards around data minimisation, parental consent, and transparency, with legal commentary from Allens and MinterEllison confirming the Code will have material operational impact on services used by schools. A public consultation report is expected ahead of the final Code.

Why it mattersIndependent schools in Sydney are likely using edtech platforms that will be directly affected by the Children's Online Privacy Code, regardless of whether those platforms are marketed to children. The December 2026 deadline is close enough that schools should now be asking their edtech vendors what compliance work is under way. The June 25 eSafety webinar is the most immediately actionable item: it is free, professionally relevant for pastoral care and wellbeing staff, and addresses harms that are already arriving in Australian school communities. Forward the webinar link to relevant colleagues before it passes.

Sources: eSafety Commissioner, "AI-assisted image-based abuse: Navigating the deepfake threat (webinar, 25 June 2026)", June 2026; OAIC, "Children's Online Privacy Code", 2026; Allens, "Draft Children's Online Privacy Code: proposed protections to have material impact on online services", April 2026; MinterEllison, "Protecting Kids Online: What the Draft Children's Online Privacy Code Means for Your Business", 2026.

3

Stanford study: AI writing feedback tools give less substantive critique to students identified as Black, female, or having learning difficulties

Stanford University / Education Week | March 2026 (major coverage this week)

A Stanford study titled "Marked Pedagogies: Examining Linguistic Biases in Personalized Automated Writing Feedback" has attracted significant attention in education circles this week following coverage in Education Week. Researchers Mei Tan, Lena Phalen, and Dorottya Demszky submitted 600 persuasive essays written by Year 8 students to four AI models, including versions of ChatGPT and Llama. Each essay was submitted multiple times with different demographic descriptors attached (race, gender, motivation level, learning ability). The finding: students identified as Black, Hispanic, Asian, female, unmotivated, or as having a learning disability received more praise and less substantive critique compared to students identified as White, male, or high-achieving. White students were more likely to receive developmental feedback – critique of argumentation, suggestions for improving reasoning, prompts to extend ideas. The researchers describe the pattern as a combination of "positive feedback bias" and "feedback withholding," and conclude it mirrors patterns already documented in human teacher feedback – but with the added risk of scale and apparent objectivity. The study is pre-published on arXiv and available openly.

Why it mattersThis has direct implications for any school considering AI-powered writing feedback or automated essay marking tools. The finding is not that AI feedback tools are useless – they can provide scalable formative feedback that teachers alone cannot deliver – but that the feedback they give is not equivalent for all students, and it is inequitable in a direction that would compound existing disadvantage. For a school in which equity and inclusion are part of the stated mission, deploying a feedback tool that consistently gives less developmental feedback to certain student groups is a governance and values question, not just a technical one. The study also highlights a general principle: AI tools trained on human-generated data tend to replicate human biases at scale. Schools need a process for scrutinising this before deployment, not after.

Sources: Stanford University / arXiv, "Marked Pedagogies: Examining Linguistic Biases in Personalized Automated Writing Feedback", March 2026; Education Week, "AI Changes Its Feedback on Students' Writing When It Knows Their Race, Gender", June 2026; KQED MindShift, "Feedback Bias? How AI Adjusts Replies Based on Race and Gender, Research Finds", 2026; Hechinger Report, "AI gives more praise, less criticism to Black students", 2026.

4

OAIC consultation finds children want control and trust in how their personal data is handled

OAIC | 2026

The OAIC's public consultation on the Children's Online Privacy Code received 337 responses including 161 from primary-school-aged children (Years 3-6) and 74 from secondary students (Years 7-12). The responses from children themselves are notable: the OAIC reports that children expressed they want to feel in control when sharing personal information online and want to trust that their data is being handled responsibly. Children's responses directly informed the Code's emphasis on age-appropriate transparency, data minimisation, and clear consent processes. This bottom-up research approach – consulting children themselves rather than only parents, regulators, and providers – is consistent with the participatory frameworks being proposed in international AI governance research.

Why it mattersThe fact that primary-school-aged children are clearly articulating concerns about data control suggests student agency in data privacy conversations is higher than many schools assume. For schools operating their own digital platforms or encouraging students to use AI tools, this is a prompt to examine whether students have been genuinely educated about what happens to their data – and whether they have meaningful choice. The OAIC's findings also signal the regulator's direction: the final Code will likely be framed around children's own rights and experiences, not just parental consent or institutional risk management.

Sources: OAIC, "Children's Online Privacy Code: Public Consultation Report", 2026; DLA Piper, "Australia: Exposure draft of Children's Online Privacy Code signals tougher standards", April 2026.

5

UK passes Children's Safety Act and AI Chatbot Powers Act – international regulatory signal for student-facing AI tools

UK Parliament | June 2026

The UK Government's Children's Safety Act and a separate AI Chatbot Powers Act received Royal Assent in June 2026. The AI Chatbot Powers Act gives UK regulators explicit authority over AI chatbot services that engage children, including the power to mandate safety features, restrict certain interactions, and require age verification. The Children's Safety Act extends existing online safety obligations to apply to AI-generated content and AI-mediated interactions. Together, the two acts represent the most direct legislative intervention on AI and child safety anywhere in the English-speaking world to date. This follows the UK's earlier Online Safety Act (2023), which established the general framework, and builds specific obligations for AI into it. The eSafety Commissioner's existing regulatory approach in Australia – using directions to comply against specific harmful services – is different in character: reactive and service-specific rather than a generalist AI regulatory power.

Why it mattersAustralian regulation of AI in children's online contexts is currently distributed across the eSafety Commissioner (online safety), the OAIC (privacy), and education sector frameworks (the national AI in Schools Framework, state policies). The UK's approach – a specific legislative power over AI chatbot services used by children – is a materially different model that concentrates authority and creates enforceable obligations for providers. Australian schools and the eSafety Commissioner have been watching UK regulatory developments closely; this legislation is likely to influence the shape of Australian regulatory reform discussions in the second half of 2026. Independent schools should note that what is legally required of edtech AI providers in Australia will look different twelve months from now.

Sources: Bird & Bird, "UK Government Children's Safety and AI Chatbot Powers Two New Acts Receive Royal Assent", June 2026; Inside Privacy, "UK Government Launches Consultation on Children's Online Experiences, Including New Obligations for AI", 2026.

Where the evidence agrees

Independent sources pointing the same way

AI tools replicate and amplify existing inequities for disadvantaged students

Independent research from the US and Australia is converging on the same conclusion: without deliberate design and oversight, AI tools tend to benefit students who are already advantaged and add risk for those who are not; equity impact assessment should be a precondition for AI tool adoption, not an afterthought.

Sources: Stanford "Marked Pedagogies" study, UTS cognitive atrophy report (March 2026), Paul Ramsay Foundation digital equity research

Regulatory frameworks for AI in children's digital lives are accelerating simultaneously across privacy, online safety, and sector-specific governance

Schools are now managing at least three distinct regulatory streams – privacy law, online safety regulation, and education-sector AI frameworks – that are advancing on different timelines and from different agencies; compliance without a coordinated view of all three is likely to leave gaps.

Sources: OAIC Children's Online Privacy Code (December 2026), UK AI Chatbot Powers Act (June 2026), eSafety webinar and guidance (June 2026)

Teacher professional learning for AI is shifting from awareness to implementation practice

National and commercial providers are now publishing implementation-focused resources rather than introductory material; schools still relying on awareness-level professional learning are behind the curve, and a more specific capability framework – what can teachers actually do with AI at the end of their training? – is now worth building.

Sources: ESA Module 2 (June 2026), Microsoft Elevate for Educators (2026), AITSL professional learning resources (2026)

Where experts disagree

Live debates worth understanding before deciding

Should AI writing feedback tools be used in schools given evidence of systematic bias?

One viewThe "Marked Pedagogies" study shows AI feedback tools give systematically less developmental feedback to marginalised student groups, creating a risk schools cannot currently detect or mitigate. (Stanford/arXiv)
The other viewSeparate research on automated feedback shows medium positive effect sizes on writing outcomes overall, with AI making formative feedback scalable in ways human teachers alone cannot achieve. (Frontiers in Education meta-analysis)
The tensionBoth findings can be true: AI feedback tools may raise average writing outcomes while simultaneously widening the gap between advantaged and disadvantaged students; the right response is not necessarily to reject the tools but to use them with explicit monitoring of differential outcomes by student cohort.

Is teacher confidence with AI translating into better student outcomes?

One viewTeachers report strong benefits from AI tools: 69% say they have improved their teaching, and ESA data shows confidence rising after structured training; teachers are saving an average of 9.3 hours per week through AI tool use. (ESA; The Educator)
The other viewStanford's evidence review found no high-quality causal studies of student AI use in K-12 classrooms; performance gains observed during tool use frequently disappear when AI is removed, and most research has not measured whether teacher AI adoption translates into durable student learning. (Stanford SCALE)
The tensionTeacher self-reported confidence and time savings are real, but they are not the same as evidence of student learning improvement; schools investing in teacher AI capability need complementary evaluation frameworks that measure student outcomes, not just teacher experience.

Worth watching

Developments to keep an eye on

TRAINE study: first results from Australia's only longitudinal AI-in-schools tracker remain expected this month

The Tracking AI in Education (TRAINE) study, led by Dr Samantha-Kaye Johnston at the University of Melbourne's Assessment and Evaluation Research Centre, is a five-year longitudinal investigation of how generative AI is changing teaching, learning, and assessment in Australian secondary classrooms. The first wave of results was expected for publication in June 2026. As of this briefing, results have not yet appeared on the TRAINE project page, though a LinkedIn post from the Faculty of Education suggests a "new research launch" is imminent. Watch for this: when it arrives it will be the most substantive Australian-specific longitudinal evidence available on how AI is actually changing classroom practice – a different quality of evidence from the survey data that has dominated Australian reporting.

Philippines Department of Education formally authorises AI tool use in public schools – a different regulatory approach worth watching

The Philippines' Department of Education this month issued Department Order No. 003, Series of 2026, formally permitting teachers, non-teaching staff, and students to use AI tools including ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Grammarly, and Khanmigo in public schools. The order is notable for its positive-permission framing – it authorises and manages use rather than restricting or prohibiting it – and contrasts sharply with the more cautious "guidance" model that Australian states have adopted. The Philippines' approach essentially treats AI tool use as a managed professional practice (similar to calculator use in mathematics) rather than a risk to be contained. Whether this model produces better or worse outcomes for student learning and integrity than the Australian approach will be worth tracking over the next two to three years.
