# AI in EdTech Weekly -- 20 September 2026

*A research briefing for the AI Integration Lead, Sydney independent K-12 school. Covering 14--20 September 2026.*

## Top Stories

### 1. NESA formalises the new assessment rules: marks must not reflect AI use, and detection tools cannot be the main safeguard

**NESA / NSW Government | official notice published this week**

NESA has now published the formal changes to the ACE Rules that give legal effect to the take-home assessment limits announced at the start of September. The updated rules confirm that Preliminary and HSC assessment programs may include no more than one take-home task, worth a maximum of 15 per cent of school-based assessment, with the limit applying to HSC courses from Term 4 2026 and Preliminary courses from Term 1 2027. Courses with a major project or HSC practical exam are exempt. The ACE Rules and the assessment and reporting requirements in each syllabus will be updated before the end of Term 3, which ends in less than a fortnight.

Two requirements in the formal notice go beyond the original headlines. First, schools must design formal assessment tasks so that marks reflect the student's own knowledge, skills and understanding, and marks must not reflect a student's use of generative AI. Second, NESA states explicitly that schools should not rely on AI detection tools as the main safeguard against malpractice: if used at all, detection software is to be treated as one input into teacher professional review, never as sole proof of AI use. Each school remains responsible for its own policy on whether generative AI is permissible in assessment. The NESA Board will develop subject-specific advice and teacher guidance from next year for the 2028 HSC and beyond, and Independent Schools NSW has published a summary of the rule changes for member schools.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This is the compliance detail the September announcement lacked, and it lands two weeks before Term 4 begins. The "marks must not reflect AI use" wording has direct implications for marking rubrics, not just task design: any criterion that could reward polished AI-assisted production over demonstrated understanding now needs review. The explicit demotion of detection software also settles an argument still running in many staffrooms, and aligns NSW with the position most researchers reached some time ago. Assessment leaders should read the ACE Rules changes directly rather than working from news coverage.

### 2. Microsoft signs a legally binding AI agreement with America's teacher union, and the rest of the industry is expected to follow

**AP / Education Week / Euronews | 15 September 2026**

Microsoft and the American Federation of Teachers announced a legally binding agreement on 15 September that limits how the company can use student and educator data in every school it contracts with, effective from 1 November. The agreement bars Microsoft from using student or educator data to train AI models (with a narrow exception for student safety), prohibits selling the data or using it for advertising or product development, and requires third-party audits to verify compliance plus plain-language disclosures to families explaining how tools work. Notably, it also prohibits AI companions or any feature "designed to foster emotional attachment or dependency" or to keep students engaged longer than a learning task requires.

The AFT described the agreement as a potential industry standard, and the immediate responses suggest it may become one. OpenAI called it "an important milestone for AI safety and privacy in schools" and said it is finalising its own agreement with the AFT; Anthropic said it is working with the union to shape "a gold standard for safety and privacy". Google, the dominant provider of education technology in US schools, has not said whether it will offer similar protections, and coverage this week repeatedly singled out that silence.

**Why it matters for K-12:** A union has just extracted, by negotiation, stronger and more enforceable commitments than most governments have achieved by regulation: binding terms, third-party audits and an outright ban on engagement-optimised features. For an Australian school, the agreement is a ready-made benchmark for vendor evaluation, and the engagement-dependency clause is language worth borrowing for any local procurement standard. It also strengthens the case made in earlier briefings for a sector-negotiated Australian equivalent through ISA or AISNSW: the AFT has demonstrated that collective negotiation with vendors works.

### 3. Only 28 per cent of Australian education professionals say their institution has a clear AI strategy

**Instructure / The Educator | research covered this week**

New Australian research from Instructure, drawn from a June-July 2026 survey of 215 education professionals across primary, secondary and higher education and presented around CanvasCon ANZ in Sydney, puts a number on what many school leaders already suspect: only 28 per cent of respondents were quite or extremely confident their institution has a clear strategy for where AI fits within its learning pathways. Three in four said helping learners retrain and adapt as careers change is important, but only 44 per cent were confident their institution's current pathways match those priorities. Instructure's Chief Customer Officer Rachel Orston described the result as "a gap between ambition and execution", and the coverage in The Educator this week argued that giving students a list of what they can and cannot do is not a strategy: young people are looking to educators for guidance on using AI effectively, critically and responsibly.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The finding is a useful mirror. Most Australian schools now have an AI policy; far fewer have an AI strategy that connects classroom practice, staff capability and assessment design to a view of where the school is heading. For a school that does have a coherent position, the 28 per cent figure is also context worth sharing with the executive: it suggests the school is further ahead of the sector than internal frustrations might imply. The vendor origin of the survey warrants the usual caution, but the sample spans sectors and the finding is consistent with the TALIS capability data reported in August.

Sources: [The AI strategy gap in Australian schools](https://www.theeducatoronline.com/k12/news/the-ai-strategy-gap-in-australian-schools/289153) -- The Educator K-12, September 2026; [Australian educators back lifelong learning but lag on AI](https://channellife.com.au/story/australian-educators-back-lifelong-learning-but-lag-on-ai) -- ChannelLife, September 2026; [AI researcher Toby Walsh and neuroscientist Lila Landowski headline CanvasCon ANZ 2026](https://www.instructure.com/en-au/press-release/ai-researcher-toby-walsh-and-neuroscientist-lila-landowski-headline-canvascon-anz) -- Instructure, 2026.

### 4. Florida mandates AI policies in every district, with parental consent and a non-AI alternative required

**Florida State Board of Education | 16 September 2026**

Florida's State Board of Education unanimously approved two sets of AI rules on 16 September, one for K-12 schools and one for state colleges. Every public school district and charter school must adopt rules for AI use within its internet safety policy by 1 July 2027, covering how students and teachers may use AI, student safety, and data protection. The most consequential requirement is the parental consent regime: districts must notify parents if their child will use an AI product, obtain consent, and offer an alternative non-AI instructional tool "similar in instructional quality" if a parent objects.

**Why it matters for K-12:** Florida has taken a different route from the New York and Los Angeles moratoriums: rather than pausing student-facing AI, it has made classroom AI use conditional on parent-by-parent consent, with a guaranteed opt-out. That model will be attractive to Australian policymakers because it appears to preserve both choice and access, but it carries a heavy operational load: schools must effectively run two parallel instructional pathways wherever AI is embedded in learning materials. If parent sentiment in Australia continues to harden along the lines of the Heads Up Alliance petition, a consent-and-alternative model is a plausible ask, and it is worth thinking now about what it would cost to operate.

Sources: [Florida approves statewide AI rules for K-12 schools and colleges](https://www.cfpublic.org/education/2026-09-16/florida-approves-statewide-ai-rules-for-k-12-schools-and-colleges) -- Central Florida Public Media, 16 September 2026; [Florida approves new AI policies for K-12 schools, state colleges](https://baynews9.com/fl/tampa/news/2026/09/16/florida-approves-new-ai-policies-for-k-1-schools-state-colleges) -- Bay News 9, 16 September 2026; [State board approves AI Guidelines for K-12 schools, colleges](https://www.news4jax.com/news/local/2026/09/17/state-board-approves-ai-guidelines-for-k-12-schools-colleges/) -- News4Jax, 17 September 2026.

### 5. Experience AI arrives in Australian classrooms: free, curriculum-aligned AI literacy from Foundation up

**Adelaide University / Raspberry Pi Foundation | national coverage this week**

Coverage this week spread the news that Experience AI, the AI literacy program developed by the Raspberry Pi Foundation and Google DeepMind, is now available to Australian teachers through Adelaide University's Computer Science Education Research (CSER) Group, which announced the partnership on 2 September. The CSER team has adapted the materials to the Australian Curriculum Version 9.0 and AITSL standards, with Australian contexts and examples, and will support implementation through its long-running free online professional learning for teachers. Teachers receive complete classroom kits: lesson plans with speaker notes, slides, videos, student activities and assessments, with no prior AI knowledge required. The program is funded in Australia by Google.org, is used in more than 195 countries, and the partnership aims to reach 150,000 learners across Australia and New Zealand.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This is the most substantial free, curriculum-aligned AI literacy offering yet available to Australian primary and secondary teachers, and it arrives exactly where TALIS located the capability gap: primary teachers who say they lack the knowledge and skills to teach with or about AI. For professional learning planning, the CSER connection matters as much as the content, since the group has a decade of credibility delivering free digital technologies PL to Australian teachers. The Google funding line should be noted in any evaluation, but the materials themselves are classroom-ready and the PISA finding that structured AI literacy practice is associated with better outcomes gives the program a clear pedagogical rationale.

Sources: [Global AI teaching program arrives in Australian classrooms](https://www.theeducatoronline.com/k12/news/global-ai-teaching-program-arrives-in-australian-classrooms/289161) -- The Educator K-12, September 2026; [Delivering free AI learning resources for Australian classrooms](https://educationmattersmag.com.au/delivering-free-ai-learning-resources-for-australian-classrooms) -- Education Matters Magazine, September 2026; [Supporting AI education for 150,000 learners in Aotearoa New Zealand and Australia](https://www.raspberrypi.org/blog/supporting-ai-education-for-150000-learners-in-aotearoa-new-zealand-and-australia/) -- Raspberry Pi Foundation, 2026.

## Worth Watching

**1. SA Royal Commission commissioners start 1 October, and the terms of reference are now overdue**

South Australia's Royal Commission into AI commences on 1 October, yet as of this briefing the terms of reference, promised within four to six weeks of the 10 August announcement, have still not been published. That window has now effectively closed, which means the terms may land at the same moment the commissioners begin work, compressing the time available for schools and sector bodies to prepare submissions. Education remains confirmed in scope. The practical advice from previous briefings stands: a submission grounded in real classroom experience, routed directly or through ISA and AISNSW, will be worth preparing the moment the process opens.

Sources: [Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence](https://ai.sa.gov.au/royal-commission-into-artificial-intelligence) -- Office for AI, SA Government; [South Australia announces Royal Commission into AI](https://www.gadens.com/insights/south-australia-announces-royal-commission-into-ai) -- Gadens, August 2026.

**2. Participatory AI governance gets an Australian roadmap**

Researchers from the University of Sydney, UTS and the University of Bergen, led by Professor Kalervo Gulson, have published work under an APPI Policy Challenge Grant testing participatory methods for AI policymaking within the NSW EdTech ecosystem, working with students, teachers, policymakers and technologists. Gulson frames the goal as shifting "from policy that is done to schools, to policy that is done with them", which echoes UNESCO's ministerial commitment this month that teachers should have a say in which AI tools enter classrooms. If the approach gains traction with NSW policymakers, consultation windows on AI policy may become genuine design opportunities rather than box-ticking, and schools with documented practitioner evidence will be best placed to use them.

Sources: [Shaping the future of AI in schools: new roadmap for participatory approaches to AI in education](https://appi.org.au/shaping-the-future-of-ai-in-schools-new-roadmap-for-participatory-approaches-to-ai-in-education/) -- APPI, September 2026; [Democratising AI in schools: ensuring policy keeps pace with technology](https://www.sydney.edu.au/arts/news-and-events/news/2025/06/13/democratising-ai-in-schools-ensuring-policy-keeps-pace-with-technology.html) -- University of Sydney.
