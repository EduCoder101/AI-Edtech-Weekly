AERO draws Australia's first evidence line on classroom AI, Canberra's inquiry asks for national AI literacy, and US states take OpenAI to court over children

Issue of 18 October 2026

At a glance

The Australian Education Research Organisation released two explainers on 14 October concluding there is little robust evidence that AI improves student learning, warning of a 'performance paradox' in which AI-generated work creates an illusion of learning, and advising no AI at all in the first three years of primary school. The federal Joint Select Committee on AI wrote to the Prime Minister recommending a national approach to AI literacy spanning education, training and workforce readiness, though the letter's release was clouded by claims it was vetted by the Prime Minister's office ahead of the committee's 30 November report. Internationally, eight US state attorneys-general sued OpenAI on consumer-protection grounds over ChatGPT's alleged harms to minors, and a US coalition report found only 20 of more than 800 studies on classroom AI produce strong causal evidence, saying globally what AERO said domestically a day earlier.

Key signals

  • AERO's two explainers, released 14 October, found limited robust research on AI and student learning outcomes in school settings, and CEO Jenny Donovan said AI should be avoided completely in the first three years of primary school and used carefully, as a scaffold rather than a substitute for thinking, after that.
  • The Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence's interim letter to the Prime Minister, dated 12 October, recommends a national approach to AI literacy, technical skills and workforce readiness, with the committee's full report due 30 November and a Sydney hearing on 20 October.
  • Attorneys-general from eight US states sued OpenAI on 14 October under consumer-protection law, alleging ChatGPT was marketed as safe for teenagers while deliberately designed to be agreeable and emotionally engaging, with harms including encouragement of self-harm; a federal class action over children's data was filed a day earlier.
  • The Alliance for Learning Innovation's report, covered by Education Week on 15 October, leans on Stanford SCALE's review of more than 800 academic papers on AI in K-12 education, of which only 20 produced strong causal evidence of effects on learning.
  • Clinical psychology researchers writing in The Conversation on 16 October reported that roughly half of Australian children aged 10 to 17 who use AI do so for personal or social purposes, and set out practical guidance for parents: open conversations, screen-style boundaries, and teaching children that chatbot conversations are neither private nor impartial.

Considerations for the Executive

  • Test the school's current AI position against AERO's explainers this term, especially the 'no AI in the first three years of primary' line and the five considerations: they are now the national evidence reference point that parents, staff and the board will quote, and the primary-years programme should be able to say explicitly where it agrees and where it deliberately differs, and why.
  • Arm leadership with the week's two numbers, AERO's finding of little robust evidence and Stanford SCALE's 20 strong studies out of 800, as the standing reply to vendor learning-gain claims, and make small-scale internal evaluation a condition of any AI pilot, since the evidence schools need is not coming from anywhere else.
  • Close the parent gap on AI companions: build a newsletter piece or parent session from The Conversation's 16 October guidance, with the wellbeing team as co-owner, so families of younger students hear the chatbots-are-not-counsellors message from the school before they hear it from the news.

The stories in depth

Full analysis with linked sources
1

AERO's new guidance: use AI sparingly, avoid it entirely in the first years of primary

AERO / EducationHQ / ABC News | 14 October 2026

The Australian Education Research Organisation released two explainers on 14 October, one for school leaders and teachers and one for policymakers, concluding that there is limited robust research examining AI and student learning outcomes in school settings, with most studies short-term, conducted overseas and focused on engagement or perceptions rather than learning. AERO warns of a "performance paradox" in which AI-generated work creates what CEO Jenny Donovan called an illusion of learning, citing studies in which students who used AI could not reproduce their results without it. Donovan told the ABC that AI should be avoided completely in the first three years of primary school and used carefully thereafter, and the guidance sets out five considerations: evidence-based teaching, monitoring for risks, clear expectations, assessment design and a culturally responsive approach.

Sources: Little robust evidence to show AI's impact on student learning: AERO – EducationHQ, 14 October 2026; Education research body urges schools to use AI sparingly due to learning risks – ABC News, 14 October 2026; Schools get new guidance on the use of AI in classrooms – The Educator K-12, 14 October 2026; AI in schools: Considerations for school leaders and teachers – AERO, October 2026.

Why it mattersThis is the closest thing Australia now has to an official evidence position on classroom AI, from the body whose explainers principals and boards actually read, and it lands squarely on the side of restraint: AI should scaffold the acquisition, retrieval, practice and application of knowledge, never replace the thinking itself. For a school with a primary years AI programme, the explicit "not in the first three years" line is the sharpest age guidance any Australian authority has given and will need a considered response, since parents and staff will quote it. It is also useful ammunition: any vendor claim about learning gains can now be met with AERO's finding that few studies even measure learning, and fewer still in primary settings.
2

Federal AI inquiry asks the Prime Minister for a national approach to AI literacy, amid an independence row

Parliament of Australia / The Canberra Times | 12--13 October 2026

The Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence sent an interim letter to the Prime Minister, dated 12 October and released publicly on 13 October, ahead of its full report due 30 November. Among its recommendations: a national approach to AI literacy, technical skills and workforce readiness spanning education, training and workforce transition; consideration of changes to copyright law over creators' work being used without adequate control or payment; and ensuring critical AI infrastructure is covered by the Security of Critical Infrastructure framework. The release was overshadowed by independent senator David Pocock's accusation that a senior adviser in the Prime Minister's office vetted the draft recommendations before they were sent, which committee chair Jo Briskey denied. The committee held further hearings in Canberra on 14 and 16 October, with a Sydney hearing scheduled for 20 October.

Sources: Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence interim letter to the Prime Minister – Parliament of Australia, 12 October 2026; Anthony Albanese's staffers accused of interfering with committee – The Canberra Times, October 2026; Public hearings, Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence – Parliament of Australia.

Why it matters"National approach to AI literacy" is the phrase to watch, because if the government takes it up, it becomes the hook on which curriculum bodies, AITSL standards and funding programmes get hung, much as digital literacy frameworks followed earlier national statements. The committee's framing treats AI literacy as a workforce and community capability question, not just a classroom one, which strengthens the case the reader has been making for whole-staff professional learning rather than opt-in enthusiast sessions. The vetting controversy matters too: it signals the final report in November will be read through a political lens, so schools should anchor their own positions in AERO's evidence rather than waiting for the committee's conclusions.
3

Australian psychologists publish practical guidance as children take their problems to chatbots

The Conversation | 16 October 2026

Following last week's Adelaide University findings on children disclosing victimisation to chatbots, clinical psychology researchers writing in The Conversation set out what is known about children's advice-seeking from AI and what parents and schools can actually do. Roughly half of Australian children aged 10 to 17 who use AI use it for personal or social purposes, including advice on sensitive topics and talking about their feelings, and international studies report that a majority of teens have tried AI companions, with most who seek mental health advice from a chatbot telling no one. The piece recommends open, non-judgemental conversations about chatbot use, treating AI tools with the same boundaries as other screens, watching for withdrawal from human relationships, and teaching children that stored conversations are not private. The eSafety Commissioner is meanwhile recruiting parents of children aged 9 to 12 for research on how children use AI chatbots.

Sources: Kids and teens are going to AI chatbots for advice. How can parents make sure this is safe? – The Conversation, 16 October 2026; eSafety: AI companions information sheet for educators – eSafety Commissioner.

Why it mattersThe advice translates directly into parent communication, which is the gap most schools have not yet filled: AI guidance has gone to staff and students, but parents are the ones at home when a Year 5 student asks a chatbot about friendship problems. A school newsletter piece or parent evening built on this guidance, open questions rather than bans, chatbots are not private, agreement is not care, would be timely while the Adelaide findings are still in the news. It also reinforces the case for the wellbeing team owning part of the AI brief, since the behaviours described sit well below the secondary years where most AI policy attention goes.
4

Eight US states sue OpenAI over ChatGPT's harms to children

EducationHQ / Al Jazeera | 14--15 October 2026

Attorneys-general from eight US states, Arizona, Arkansas, Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska and West Virginia, filed coordinated lawsuits against OpenAI on 14 October, the most significant state action against an AI company to date. The suits rest on consumer-protection law, alleging OpenAI marketed ChatGPT as safe for teenagers while deliberately designing it to be agreeable and emotionally engaging, and cite alleged harms including encouragement of self-harm, dangerous medical advice and assistance with violence. The states seek civil penalties, court-ordered product changes and surrender of profits earned from young users; a separate federal class action over the collection of children's data was filed a day earlier. OpenAI said it has industry-leading protections, and no court has yet ruled.

Sources: Eight US states sue OpenAI over ChatGPT harm to young – EducationHQ, 15 October 2026; Why are eight US states suing OpenAI? – Al Jazeera, 15 October 2026; USA: Eight states sue OpenAI over ChatGPT's alleged harms to children – Business and Human Rights Resource Centre, October 2026.

Why it mattersCourts and consumer-protection regulators, not education departments, are now setting the pace on AI child safety, and the legal theory matters for schools: the claim is that sycophantic, engagement-maximising design is itself the deceptive practice, not merely a side effect. That is the same design property Australian researchers flagged with the Spot the Sycophant tool and that AERO's guidance warns about, and it will shape what vendors are willing to put in front of minors. For a school, the practical reading is that any student-facing tool's engagement-boosting features, personas, memory, flattery, now carry documented legal and safety concerns worth raising in procurement, and that Australia's own Digital Duty of Care Bill would push in the same direction with penalties attached.
5

US coalition: only 20 of 800 studies on classroom AI produce strong causal evidence, and governments should fund better research

Education Week | 15 October 2026

The Alliance for Learning Innovation, a coalition of education nonprofits, researchers and funders, published a report arguing the US federal government should fund research into the safe and effective design and use of AI in classrooms, so that states and districts stop buying on faith. The report leans on Stanford SCALE's 2026 review, which analysed more than 800 academic papers on AI in K-12 education and found only 20 produced strong causal evidence, and it lands in a policy vacuum: the US Education Department's Office of Educational Technology was shut down last year and federal guidance on AI purchasing remains thin.

Sources: We Need Research on AI in Schools. The Feds Should Fund It, Report Says – Education Week, 15 October 2026; The Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A 2026 Review – Stanford SCALE Initiative, 2026.

Why it mattersThe 20-of-800 figure is the single most quotable statistic of the week and says internationally what AERO said domestically the day before: schools everywhere are buying and deploying classroom AI on an evidence base that rounds to zero. For the reader's purposes it is a governance asset, a one-line answer to "everyone else is adopting this" in any leadership or board conversation, and a reason to make small-scale internal evaluation (baseline, intervention, check for transfer without the tool) part of any AI pilot the school runs, since nobody else is producing the evidence either.

Where the evidence agrees

Independent sources pointing the same way

Institutions on three continents now formally say the evidence for classroom AI is thin and higher-order thinking is the casualty

"There is no good evidence this helps learning, and credible reason to think unstructured use erodes thinking" is now the official institutional position, not a contrarian one, so school AI strategies should be written to survive that premise

Sources: AERO's two explainers finding limited robust research and a performance paradox (14 October); Alliance for Learning Innovation / Stanford SCALE: 20 of 800+ studies show strong causal evidence (15 October); 30-expert consensus report led from the University of Minnesota rating the disruption risk to higher-order cognitive and metacognitive processes as severe and one-sided (published 8 October, circulating widely this week)

The pressure on AI companies over children is shifting from reports to enforcement

The design features at issue, sycophancy, emotional engagement, data collection from minors, are the ones to interrogate in any student-facing tool, because regulators and courts have now named them as the harm

Sources: Eight state consumer-protection lawsuits against OpenAI (14 October); a federal class action over children's data filed a day earlier (13 October); eSafety's ongoing research and its legal notices requiring companion-chatbot providers to explain child protections

AI literacy, taught rather than assumed, is the convergence point of policy, research and parenting advice

Every serious voice this week lands on explicit teaching about AI's behaviour and limits, which is exactly the whole-staff and parent-education work an AI lead can start without waiting for any national framework

Sources: The Joint Select Committee's recommendation of a national approach to AI literacy (12 October); AERO's call for clear expectations and taught, scaffolded use (14 October); The Conversation's guidance to parents on teaching children how chatbots actually work (16 October)

Where experts disagree

Live debates worth understanding before deciding

Should Australia lead with AI capability-building or with restraint?

One viewThe Joint Select Committee urges a national push on AI literacy, skills and uptake (12 October), in the same spirit as South Australia's statewide EdChat rollout and the Premier's position that every student should learn AI
The other viewAERO advises no AI at all in the first three years of primary and sparing, scaffolded use after that (14 October), grounded in the absence of learning-outcome evidence
The tensionCanberra's economic framing says build capability now; the national evidence body says the learning science is not there yet, and schools are the ground on which both instructions land simultaneously

Is the answer better-funded classroom AI research or less classroom technology?

One viewThe Alliance for Learning Innovation wants federal R&D money to make classroom AI safe and effective (15 October), treating adoption as inevitable and evidence as the fixable gap
The other viewA Washington Times opinion argues that competing on AI requires less technology in classrooms, not more (13 October), citing declining achievement, while Newsweek maps a growing list of US states legislating to limit AI in schools
The tensionWhether the evidence vacuum is an argument for filling it with research while adoption continues, or for withdrawing the technology until the research exists, the same fork the Australian debate reached last week

Worth watching

Developments to keep an eye on

The federal AI inquiry's Sydney hearing on 20 October and its 30 November report

The Joint Select Committee sits in Sydney on 20 October and reports by 30 November. After this week's interim letter, the education questions are whether "a national approach to AI literacy" survives into the final recommendations with any funding or curriculum machinery attached, and whether the Pocock vetting row weakens the report's authority. Either way, it is the most likely vehicle for the first federal statement on AI capability that names schools directly since the national framework, and worth reading on release.

Sources: Public hearings, Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence – Parliament of Australia.

Anti-Poverty Week data puts a number on the equity cost of AI rollouts

Timed to Anti-Poverty Week (12 to 18 October), The Smith Family reported that 44 per cent of students on its Learning for Life programme lack a connected laptop or tablet at home, with national data showing the share of young people without a device rising, not falling. As schools and states roll out generative AI tools that assume home access, the digital divide quietly becomes an AI divide. Early-stage as a policy story, but it is the equity question every school AI strategy will eventually be asked, including by independent school scholarship families.

Sources: Digital gap widens as home device access fails disadvantaged students – The Educator K-12, October 2026.
