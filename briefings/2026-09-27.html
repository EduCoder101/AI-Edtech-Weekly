A quiet week at home sharpens the real question: with detection discredited and bans unenforceable, the work left is assessment design and teacher capability

Issue of 27 September 2026

At a glance

After three weeks of heavy Australian policy activity, no new releases from NESA, ACARA, AERO or the eSafety Commissioner met the significance threshold this week; the domestic pause comes just before the SA Royal Commission commences on 1 October, still without published terms of reference or named commissioners. The week's most useful Australian contribution was intellectual: researchers writing in The Conversation argued that schools and universities should stop using AI detection software altogether, going one step beyond NESA's new position that detection cannot be the main safeguard against malpractice. Internationally, a new American College of Education survey found 54 per cent of US teachers have had no AI training at all, and Education Week's reporting on the New York and Los Angeles moratoriums concluded that switching off AI district-wide may be impossible now that Gemini and its peers are embedded in the everyday tools schools already use. La Trobe University offered a fresh angle on staff-facing AI: a social robot that role-plays hostile parent conversations so teachers can rehearse before facing the real thing.

Key signals

  • Researchers writing in The Conversation on 23 September argued the education sector should abandon AI detection software entirely, citing false positives, documented bias against non-native English speakers, and OpenAI's decision to shut down its own detector over poor accuracy, and recommended the TEQSA-supported alternative of redesigning assessment instead.
  • A new American College of Education survey of 694 US teachers found 54 per cent have received no AI training whatsoever, 44 per cent of those who name AI a top classroom challenge have had none, and only 22 per cent believe AI will make teachers more valuable rather than less.
  • Education Week reported that the New York City and Los Angeles moratoriums are colliding with AI's ubiquity: Stanford's Victor Lee argues generative AI cannot be turned off like a light switch now that it sits inside Workspace accounts, search results and browsers, and parent advocates are already pushing beyond bans to screen-time limits.
  • La Trobe University's AI-driven social robot Fletch, which role-plays a disgruntled parent so preservice and practising teachers can rehearse heated conversations, drew sector coverage this week, with several Victorian schools signed on to train existing staff.
  • South Australia's Royal Commission into AI commences on 1 October with neither final terms of reference nor commissioners publicly announced as of this briefing, compressing the preparation window for schools and sector bodies intending to make submissions.

Considerations for the Executive

  • Review whether the school still subscribes to or informally relies on any AI detection product, and decide its fate deliberately: the research case for dropping detection entirely is now public and strong, and any residual use should be confined to prompting human inquiry, never to evidencing a malpractice finding.
  • Treat the quiet week as the window to finish Term 4 readiness: NESA's assessment rules take effect within weeks, so marking rubrics, task schedules and the malpractice process should be settled now rather than after the SA Royal Commission and the next policy wave land in October.
  • Audit the school's Google Workspace and Microsoft tenancy settings to confirm which embedded AI features are actually active for students, since the US moratorium experience shows the meaningful control point is tool-by-tool configuration, not a headline policy position.

1

Australian researchers call time on AI detection software: do not just demote it, stop using it

The Conversation | 23 September 2026

Days after NESA formally directed schools not to rely on AI detection tools as the main safeguard against malpractice, researchers writing in The Conversation argued the sector should go one step further and abandon the software entirely. The piece notes that most Australian universities have already moved away from AI detection in some form, and assembles the evidence for why schools should follow: the tools produce false positives that can derail an innocent student's schooling, the error burden falls unevenly (research has repeatedly found non-native English speakers are flagged at higher rates), and OpenAI shut down its own detection product because of poor accuracy. There is, the authors conclude, no reliable way to prove a student did or did not use AI in their work.

The recommended alternative is the one Australia's higher education regulator TEQSA already supports: redesign assessment rather than police it. That means building tasks where AI use is either structurally impossible (supervised, in-person, oral) or explicitly permitted and taught, so students learn to use AI well where it suits the task.

Sources: Unis and schools are moving away from AI-detection software. They should stop using it altogether – The Conversation, 23 September 2026; syndicated copy – New Space Economy, 23 September 2026; Changes to ACE Rules concerning take-home assessment tasks and the use of artificial intelligence – NESA, September 2026.

Why it mattersNESA's ACE Rules change left detection software a residual role as "one input into teacher professional review". This piece is the strongest public case yet that even that residual role is untenable, and it will circulate in staffrooms. For a school still paying for a detection product, the question is now live: what decision, exactly, is the subscription informing? The safer ground, consistent with both NESA and the research, is investment in supervised assessment design and staff moderation practice, with detection scores kept out of any malpractice finding.
2

Fletch the robot parent: La Trobe uses AI simulation to prepare teachers for their hardest conversations

EducationHQ / La Trobe University | sector coverage this week

EducationHQ this week profiled Fletch, an AI-driven "social robot" built by researchers at La Trobe University's School of Education that role-plays a disgruntled parent so teachers can rehearse heated conversations before facing them at work. The scenarios are drawn from interviews with experienced teachers about real interactions, such as a parent disputing a suspension or angry about falling grades, and the robot adapts its reactions to what the teacher says. Lead researcher Professor Therese Keane frames the project as a response to what she calls an occupational health and safety crisis: exposure to verbal aggression from adults is a documented contributor to teacher stress, burnout and attrition. Fletch is being used with La Trobe's preservice teachers, and several Victorian schools have signed on to train existing staff. The project first drew national attention through ABC coverage in late August.

Sources: The AI robot helping to prepare teachers to handle heated parent conversations – EducationHQ, September 2026; Robot 'parent' trained by AI preparing teachers for conflict amid exodus from profession – ABC News, 26 August 2026; Researchers use robot 'parent' to prepare teachers for difficult conversations – Education Matters Magazine, 2026.

Why it mattersThis is a genuinely novel professional learning use of AI: simulation-based rehearsal of high-stakes human interaction, a technique long used in medical training but rare in education. For a school thinking about where AI belongs in its own PL programme, the interesting move is that the AI plays the difficult human rather than the teacher, sidestepping most of the pedagogical and integrity concerns that attach to student-facing tools. It is also a reminder that the most defensible AI applications in schools right now may be the ones aimed at staff capability and wellbeing rather than at students.
3

More than half of US teachers have had no AI training at all, new survey finds

American College of Education / The Educator | 22 September 2026

A new survey of 694 US teachers by the American College of Education, covered in Australia this week under the pointed headline "America's AI edge stops at the classroom door", found 54 per cent have received no training whatsoever on using or managing AI at school. The gap is sharpest exactly where it hurts most: 44 per cent of teachers who name AI as a top classroom challenge have had no training on it. Meanwhile expectations keep rising, with 49 per cent expecting AI to take a bigger role in grading, lesson planning and administration over the next five to ten years and 43 per cent anticipating heavier reliance on AI tutoring tools. Only 22 per cent believe AI will make teachers more valuable rather than less.

Sources: America's AI edge stops at the classroom door, study finds – The Educator K-12, 22 September 2026; American College of Education – survey publisher, September 2026.

Why it mattersThe finding lands amid the largest teacher AI training push in US history, with the AFT's National Academy for AI Instruction aiming to train one in ten US teachers and OpenAI, Google and Anthropic all running free teacher programmes, yet the baseline is still that most teachers have had nothing. It is the same pattern TALIS found for Australian primary teachers and Instructure found for Australian institutions: adoption and expectation running well ahead of capability-building. The 22 per cent figure is the one for a staff meeting, because a workforce that mostly suspects AI will devalue it will not engage with tool training until the professional identity question is addressed.
4

Can a school district actually ban AI? The New York and Los Angeles moratoriums meet the ubiquity problem

Education Week | late September 2026

With the school year underway, Education Week this week examined how the New York City and Los Angeles moratoriums are working in practice, and the answer is: unevenly, because turning off generative AI "as if it were a light switch" may be impossible. Stanford education professor Victor Lee and others point out that AI is no longer a destination students visit but a layer inside the tools schools already use: Google's Gemini now sits inside Workspace accounts and is available free to any student aged 13 or over, AI summaries appear in search results by default, and assistants are embedded in browsers and operating systems. Districts can block a chatbot on managed devices; they cannot excise the technology from the digital environment their students live in. Parent groups that pushed for the bans say they welcome them but are already asking for the harder thing: reduced screen time and platform restrictions overall. A separate Conversation analysis this week made the complementary argument that bans alone will not remedy declining reading skills, which predate generative AI.

Sources: AI Is Working Its Way Into Everything. Can School Districts Really Ban It? – Education Week, September 2026; New York, Los Angeles Restrict Student AI Use. Will Other Districts Follow? – Education Week, September 2026; Why banning AI in the classroom will not be enough to remedy poor reading skills in a post-literary society – The Conversation, September 2026.

Why it mattersAustralian parent sentiment is tracking the same direction as New York's, and if an Australian system ever reaches for a moratorium it will hit the same wall: the meaningful policy unit is not "AI" but specific tools, specific tasks and specific ages. That is an argument for the granular work (which tools, for what, from what year level, with what teaching) over the satisfying headline. It is also a procurement point: whether Gemini is active inside a school's Workspace tenancy is a configuration decision many schools have not consciously made.

Where the evidence agrees

Independent sources pointing the same way

Policing AI use is a dead end; the work is assessment and task design

Regulators, researchers and implementation reporting all now converge on the same conclusion: neither detection software nor blanket bans can carry the integrity load, which shifts it onto supervised and redesigned assessment

Sources: The Conversation on abandoning detection software (23 September); Education Week on the unenforceability of district bans (September); NESA's ACE Rules position that detection cannot be the main safeguard (in force from Term 4)

The teacher capability gap is structural, not a lag that is closing on its own

Three years into the generative AI era, in two school systems, most teachers still report no meaningful preparation; schools cannot wait for systems to close this gap and need their own PL pipeline

Sources: American College of Education: 54 per cent of US teachers have had no AI training (22 September); Instructure: 28 per cent of Australian education professionals confident of a clear AI strategy (reported last week); TALIS 2024 on primary teachers lacking AI skills (ACER)

AI's strongest school use cases right now are staff-facing, not student-facing

While the student-facing question stays contested, tools aimed at teacher practice, workload and even conflict rehearsal are maturing with far less controversy, and are the lower-risk place to build staff AI fluency

Sources: La Trobe's Fletch robot for rehearsing parent conflict (covered this week); Anthropic's September Claude for Teachers additions and Detroit pilot (September); ACE survey: teachers expect AI's growth in grading, planning and admin (22 September)

Where experts disagree

Live debates worth understanding before deciding

What residual role should AI detection software have?

One viewThe Conversation researchers: none; false positives and bias make the tools unfit for any disciplinary purpose and schools should drop them entirely
The other viewNESA's formal position: detection must not be the main safeguard, but may remain one input into teacher professional review
The tensionWhether a tool too unreliable to be proof can still be a legitimate prompt for human inquiry, or whether keeping it in the room at all invites unfair accusations

Are moratoriums good policy or good theatre?

One viewParent advocates and the NYC and LA districts: a pause protects younger students while evidence accumulates, and other districts should follow
The other viewStanford's Victor Lee and Conversation analysts: AI is embedded in the everyday digital environment and cannot be switched off, so bans mislead communities about what is actually being controlled
The tensionBoth sides want to protect learning; they disagree on whether an unenforceable rule still has value as a signal and a default

Worth watching

Developments to keep an eye on

The SA Royal Commission commences Thursday with its terms of reference still unpublished

South Australia's Royal Commission into AI begins on 1 October, and as of this briefing neither the final terms of reference nor the commissioners have been publicly announced, despite the August commitment to name both ahead of the start date after an international search. Education remains confirmed in scope. Expect a burst of announcements within days; when the submission process opens it may open quickly, so the preparatory work suggested in earlier briefings (documented classroom evidence, a draft school position, coordination with ISA and AISNSW) is now time-sensitive.

Sources: Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence – Office for AI, SA Government; SA royal commission to test AI's limits – The Mandarin, August 2026.

Anthropic starts a formal classroom evaluation of Claude for Teachers in Detroit

Anthropic's September updates to Claude for Teachers added two classroom-specific skills (lesson preparation and check for understanding) and, more significantly, a pilot evaluation with Detroit Public Schools Community District studying the tool's impact on educator wellbeing and practice. Vendor-run evaluations warrant scepticism, but a major AI company committing to studying teacher outcomes rather than just claiming them is a shift, and the design and results will be worth reading whenever they emerge. If credible, it becomes the template to demand from every vendor pitching teacher-facing AI to the school.

Sources: Introducing Claude for Teachers – Anthropic, updated September 2026; Anthropic Launches Claude for Teachers. Why Some Critics Are Concerned – Education Week, July 2026.
