# AI in EdTech Weekly -- 9 August 2026

*A research briefing for the AI Integration Lead, Sydney independent K-12 school. Covering 3 August -- 9 August 2026.*

---

## Top Stories

### 1. First systematic benchmark: frontier chatbots barely know the Australian curriculum

**SchoolNews / CurricuLLM benchmark | c. early August 2026**

Australian edtech company CurricuLLM published the first systematic benchmark of how well frontier AI models actually recall Australian and New Zealand K-10 curricula, testing seven mainstream models (including GPT-5.2, Gemini 3 Pro and Claude Sonnet 4.5) on roughly 1,700 questions per model across five categories of curriculum knowledge. The results were poor across the board: no mainstream model exceeded a 41 per cent overall pass rate across the four curricula, most scored below 17 per cent on content recall, and two of the seven scored zero when asked to identify the meaning of specific curriculum outcome entries. The plain-language finding is that a general-purpose chatbot trained mostly on American content has serious blind spots on the Australian Curriculum and state syllabuses, and will confidently fill those gaps with plausible fabrication. One caveat: this is vendor-published research (CurricuLLM sells a curriculum-grounded AI product), so the framing serves its business case even if the method is the first of its kind for the region.

**Why it matters for K-12:** A large share of Australian teachers now use general chatbots for planning, and most will assume the model knows what NSW outcome codes or Australian Curriculum content descriptions mean. This benchmark says it mostly does not, which is a concrete, evidence-based message for professional learning: teachers must paste in the actual syllabus content they are planning against rather than trusting the model's recall, and any AI-generated planning that cites outcome codes needs checking against the source document. It is also a useful evaluation question to put to every vendor: how is your tool grounded in the curriculum we teach?

Sources: [Study reveals what AI chat bots actually know about Australian curricula](https://www.school-news.com.au/technology/study-reveals-what-ai-chat-bots-actually-know-about-australian-curricula/) -- SchoolNews Australia, c. early August 2026.

### 2. TALIS Australian report: world-leading AI adoption, with the capability gap concentrated in primary

**The Educator / ACER | c. early August 2026**

Sector coverage this week dug into the AI chapter of ACER's TALIS 2024 Australian Report (Friedman, Ainley et al.), the full national report from the world's largest teacher survey (6,040 Australian teachers across 359 schools). Around two in three lower secondary teachers used AI in the past year, ranking Australia fourth of 55 education systems and nearly double the OECD average of 36 per cent, while only about one in two primary teachers did. The primary-specific detail is the news: of primary teachers not using AI, 84 per cent said they lack the knowledge or skills to teach with it (75 per cent at lower secondary), and about two in five non-users said schools should not be using AI in teaching at all. Meanwhile 87 per cent of lower secondary teachers worry AI lets students misrepresent others' work as their own, and more than half say it can amplify biases that reinforce student misconceptions. The benefits teachers most endorse are adapting material to student ability (65 per cent lower secondary, 52 per cent primary) and improving lesson plans (64 and 52 per cent).

**Why it matters for K-12:** Primary-specific AI data is rare, and this is nationally representative. It confirms the capability gap is largest exactly where a K-6 professional learning programme operates: half of primary teachers are not using AI, and the overwhelming reason is skills, not opposition. That is an argument for foundational, low-threat capability-building in primary rather than tool rollouts, and the 87 per cent integrity concern suggests staff goodwill for assessment-design work is high. Adoption rankings make good headlines, but the same survey shows adoption without confidence.

Sources: [Teachers turn to AI, but doubts linger](https://www.theeducatoronline.com/k12/news/teachers-turn-to-ai-but-doubts-linger/288762) -- The Educator K/12, c. early August 2026; [TALIS 2024 Australian Report](https://research.acer.edu.au/talis/12/) -- ACER (Friedman, Ainley et al.); [AI in the classroom -- evidence, teacher insights and action](https://www.teachermagazine.com/au_en/articles/ai-in-the-classroom-evidence-teacher-insights-and-action) -- Teacher Magazine (ACER), August 2026.

### 3. OpenAI ships "education plugins": agentic AI arrives inside managed school deployments

**OpenAI / Forbes | 3-4 August 2026**

Timed for the northern back-to-school season, OpenAI released three education plugins for ChatGPT Work and Codex: K-12 Educator, College Educator and College Student. A plugin bundles apps, role-specific skills, instructions and pre-built workflows, so a teacher gets lesson-planning and classroom-material workflows that run against the course materials and context the school chooses, without having to construct prompts from scratch. The K-12 Educator plugin is available in ChatGPT Edu workspaces and in ChatGPT for Teachers workspaces managed through a district domain. Forbes framed the significance accurately: this moves classroom AI from answering questions to executing multi-step workflows on the school's own content.

**Why it matters for K-12:** Two signals worth separating from the product news. First, the grounding problem in Story 1 is now the competitive battleground: vendors are solving the "model doesn't know your curriculum" gap by having schools supply their materials and context, which makes data governance and copyright questions about what a school uploads more pressing than chatbot output quality. Second, agentic AI (systems that do tasks, not just answer) is now being packaged for ordinary teachers inside managed school accounts, so AI policies written around "students asking chatbots questions" are already a generation behind. Worth reviewing what any school-managed AI workspace can now do before staff discover it first.

Sources: [New ways to learn and teach with ChatGPT Work and Codex](https://openai.com/index/learn-teach-chatgpt-work-codex/) -- OpenAI, c. 3-4 August 2026; [OpenAI Education Plugins Move AI From Answers To Workflows](https://www.forbes.com/sites/rayravaglia/2026/08/04/openai-education-plugins-move-ai-from-answers-to-workflows/) -- Forbes, 4 August 2026; [OpenAI adds education plugins to ChatGPT Work and Codex](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/openai-adds-three-education-plugins-for-k12-teachers-college-faculty-and-students) -- EdTech Innovation Hub, August 2026.

### 4. The AI-first school goes mainstream in the US, faster than the evidence

**Axios / Houston Public Media / Christian Science Monitor | 2-7 August 2026**

Alpha Schools, the "two hours of AI-powered academics a day" private school chain, announced expansion from about a dozen to roughly 50 campuses across 27 US markets in the 2026 school year, at tuition of USD 45,000 to 75,000. More significant for the sector: Houston ISD, a large public district, is piloting Alpha's Timeback AI learning platform in two public elementary campuses from this term, the model's first meaningful crossover into public schooling. The same week, the Christian Science Monitor reported the US K-12 AI market reached USD 730 million in 2026 and is projected to grow 40 per cent annually to USD 18.5 billion by 2036, while researchers warn districts are buying ahead of the evidence. An education researcher quoted on the Alpha expansion called its adaptive-tutoring approach "an open experiment" not supported by critical research, and a Conversation research review found no evidence AI tutors outperform human teachers.

**Why it matters for K-12:** Australian independent schools will increasingly field parent questions shaped by this coverage, in both directions: "why isn't our school doing AI-personalised learning like Alpha?" and "is AI harming my child's thinking?". The honest evidence position is the useful one: AI tutoring shows promise in narrow, well-designed uses, there is no evidence it beats human teaching, and the schools spending most aggressively are running an experiment. Having that framing ready, with sources, is cheap insurance for the next parent forum.

Sources: [AI-powered Alpha Schools expands to 50 campuses as AI debate grows](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/02/alpha-schools-ai-expansion-50-campuses) -- Axios, 2 August 2026; [Two Houston ISD elementary campuses will pilot Alpha School's AI-powered education program this fall](https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/education/2026/08/03/558564/hisd-ai-learning-alpha-school-timeback-future-2-gregg-martinez-elementary/) -- Houston Public Media, 3 August 2026; [Education's AI 'gold rush' comes with risks for students](https://www.csmonitor.com/USA/Education/2026/0807/ai-risks-schools-students) -- Christian Science Monitor, 7 August 2026; [Despite the growth of some AI schools like Alpha, research doesn't show that AI tutors are better than human teachers](https://theconversation.com/despite-the-growth-of-some-ai-schools-like-alpha-research-doesnt-show-that-ai-tutors-are-better-than-human-teachers-281290) -- The Conversation, c. ## Worth Watching

**1. National Cabinet and the national AI standards: still no outcome, fourth week on watch**

The Prime Minister's 15 July commitment to take the legislated Australian AI standards to National Cabinet in August, with an Office of AI coordinating education among other portfolios, has still produced no reported outcome as at 9 August. The proposal on the table centres on data centre rules, but the Office of AI's remit explicitly includes education, chatbot risks to children, and a digital duty of care, all of which touch schools directly. When the meeting result lands, expect it with little warning; the education provisions are the thing to read first.

Sources: [AI in Australia's Interests](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/ai-australias-interests) -- Prime Minister of Australia, 15 July 2026; [Govt to introduce AI, data centre laws in policy pivot](https://ia.acs.org.au/article/2026/govt-to-introduce-ai--data-centre-laws-in-policy-pivot.html) -- Information Age (ACS), July 2026.

**2. eSafety's deepfake webinar for educators, 27 August**

Following its late-July advisory on school imagery, eSafety is running a professional learning webinar on 27 August covering deepfakes and synthetic media, including how young people experience fake images and videos and how professionals should respond. Flagged because it is the natural next step for any school acting on last week's imagery advisory, and a low-cost way to put trained language in front of pastoral and communications staff before an incident.

Source: [Professional learning for educators](https://www.esafety.gov.au/educators/training-for-professionals/educators-and-youth-serving-professionals) -- eSafety Commissioner, webinar scheduled 27 August 2026.
