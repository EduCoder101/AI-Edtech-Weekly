Deepfake incidents double as regulators act, and the sharpest AI literacy gap turns out to be in primary school

Issue of 3 May 2026

At a glance

The eSafety Commissioner urged schools to report AI-generated deepfake incidents after reports more than doubled in 18 months, releasing an updated Toolkit for Schools. New survey data shows primary teachers report the biggest gap in students' ability to recognise AI-generated content, just as AI tools reach primary classrooms. New York City published hard-limit AI guidance for 1.1 million students, and US students will co-write a model AI policy.

Key signals

  • eSafety deepfake reports involving under-18s more than doubled in 18 months; three 'nudify' platforms have been removed from the Australian market and criminal offences now apply in some states.
  • 61% of primary teachers say students struggle badly to distinguish AI-generated content, a far higher share than secondary. Capability is arriving before literacy.
  • NYC's guidance draws hard lines: no AI grading, no AI disciplinary decisions, no biometric collection without strict oversight. A template for enforceable policy.
  • AASA, MIT and Day of AI will have students co-draft a model AI policy, formalising student voice in governance.
  • ISNSW's ICT leadership conference put AI strategy at the top of the independent sector's agenda.

Considerations for the Executive

  • Establish a deepfake incident protocol distinct from general cyberbullying policy, using the eSafety toolkit.
  • Prioritise concrete AI-content literacy in the primary years rather than scaled-down secondary ethics content.
  • Consider structured student input into the school's next AI policy revision.

ISNSW engagED ICT Conference concludes – AI strategy dominates the agenda

Independent Schools NSW | 29 April -- 1 May 2026

The Independent Schools NSW ICT Management and Leadership Conference, held this week at the Gold Coast Convention and Exhibition Centre under the theme "engagED – Where innovation meets education," brought together ICT leaders from across the NSW independent school sector. Keynote speakers included Dr Sandra Peter and Dr Kai Riemer (University of Sydney Business School), both of whom work extensively on AI disruption and the future of work. The conference featured over 100 technology vendors and sessions on AI strategy, digital transformation, and school-level implementation. ISNSW offered scholarships to increase participation from a diverse range of member schools.

Why it mattersThis is the most significant gathering of independent school technology leaders in NSW, and its timing – mid-semester, mid-year planning cycle – means the conversations happening in those rooms will shape infrastructure decisions, platform choices, and PL priorities for the rest of 2026. The presence of Peter and Riemer as keynotes signals that ISNSW is framing AI as a leadership and strategic question, not purely a technology one. If your school's ICT leadership attended, it is worth debriefing on what commitments or conversations emerged.

Sources: Independent Schools NSW, ISNSW ICT Management and Leadership Conference 2026, 29 April – 1 May 2026; Cambium Networks, Independent Schools NSW | April 29 – May 1, 2026, 2026.

2

eSafety urges schools to report AI-generated deepfakes as cases double

eSafety Commissioner | ongoing, updated 2026

Australia's eSafety Commissioner has issued an urgent call for schools to report AI-generated deepfake incidents, after reports of digitally altered intimate images involving young people under 18 more than doubled in 18 months – exceeding the total number received in the seven preceding years. eSafety has released an updated Toolkit for Schools with step-by-step guidance for managing deepfake incidents. Four out of five reports involve female targets. Following eSafety enforcement action against "nudify" services used in Australian schools, three platforms have been removed from the Australian market. In some states, creating or sharing AI-generated explicit content is now a criminal offence.

Why it mattersThis is no longer a rare or emerging problem – it is a pattern, and it is in Australian schools now. The doubling of reports in under two years suggests that awareness among students is not a deterrent. Independent schools need a specific protocol for deepfake incidents that is distinct from existing cyberbullying policies: the legal context is different, eSafety has specific reporting pathways, and victim support needs are different from general online harm. The updated toolkit is a practical starting point and is worth sharing with your wellbeing and student services teams.

Sources: eSafety Commissioner, "eSafety urges schools to report deepfakes as numbers double", 2026; eSafety Commissioner, "Deepfake damage in schools", 2026; eSafety Commissioner, "eSafety moves against nudify services", 2026.

Primary teachers report the sharpest AI-content literacy gap – new EdWeek survey

Education Week Research Center | April 2026

A new survey by the Education Week Research Center, published in late April, found that 61% of primary school teachers say their students struggle "a lot" to distinguish AI-generated content from non-AI content – a significantly higher proportion than middle school (44%) or secondary teachers (38%). The survey was conducted across February and March 2026 and complements an April EdWeek analysis arguing that schools are playing "catch-up" on media literacy at the same time AI content is accelerating in volume and sophistication. At least half of US states have enacted media literacy laws since 2024; Australia has no equivalent national requirement.

Why it mattersThe primary years gap is the most directly relevant finding here. If your Year 5 students are already interacting with AI tools through NSWEduChat, they are encountering AI-generated text and images as normal – but this survey suggests they lack the critical lens to interrogate that content. AI literacy at the primary level cannot simply be a scaled-down version of secondary AI ethics conversations; it needs to build from concrete skills (how do I check whether this is AI-generated?) toward more abstract critical thinking. This is a curriculum design question, not just a values one.

Sources: Education Week, "Schools Play Game of Media Literacy Catch-Up as AI Use Rises", April 2026; Media Literacy Now, New U.S. Media Literacy Report, 2026.

4

New York City releases preliminary AI guidance for 1.1 million students

NYC Department of Education | active through 8 May 2026

The New York City Department of Education has published preliminary AI guidance for its 1.1 million-student school system and is accepting public feedback until 8 May 2026, with a comprehensive playbook planned for June. Under the guidance, teachers may use AI for brainstorming, drafting communications, and lesson planning. Hard limits include: AI may not be used to assign grades, make disciplinary decisions, or collect biometric and behavioural data without strict oversight. The guidance also addresses student use in classrooms, data privacy, and vendor procurement.

Why it mattersNYC's framework is significant not because Australian schools need to follow it, but because of the distinctions it draws. The explicit prohibition on AI grading and AI-driven discipline is a governance signal worth noting: these are exactly the uses that are most tempting under workload pressure and most risk-laden for students. Australian frameworks – including the national AI in Schools Framework – have been less explicit about hard limits. NYC's approach offers a template for schools wanting to move beyond aspirational principles toward enforceable policy.

Sources: NYC Department of Education, Preliminary AI Guidance, April 2026; Chalkbeat, "What NYC's new AI school rules say, and what still remains unclear", 2026; AI for Education, AI + Education Weekly Update, 27 April 2026.

5

Students to co-write model AI policy for US schools – a new governance model

Education Week | April 2026

AASA (The School Superintendents Association), Day of AI, and MIT RAISE have announced a July 2026 workshop in Boston at which one superintendent and two student representatives from each US state will work together to draft a model AI policy for K-12 schools. The initiative directly addresses what district leaders cite as a primary barrier to responsible AI adoption: the absence of clear, student-endorsed policy frameworks. Day of AI – a free, curriculum-aligned AI literacy program from MIT – is also active in Australia through Day of AI Australia, which has reached over 100,000 students and educators.

Why it mattersThe idea of students co-designing the policy they will be governed by is both pedagogically sound and practically useful – students often identify edge cases and values tensions that adults miss. The resulting policy will be shared publicly and is likely to circulate quickly in the Australian independent school context. Worth flagging this to your principal as a conversation-starter: should your school seek student input into its AI policy revision? Day of AI Australia's existing presence in Australian schools means the underlying curriculum infrastructure is already here.

Sources: Education Week, "Students Will Take the Lead on Crafting a Model AI Policy for Schools", April 2026; Day of AI Australia, For Teachers, 2026.

Where the evidence agrees

Independent sources pointing the same way

Formal governance limits on AI are tightening – especially around assessment and harm

Regulators and large jurisdictions are moving from aspirational frameworks to hard limits; schools that have only adopted principles-based policies may soon find them inadequate against regulatory expectations.

Sources: NYC DoE preliminary AI guidance, eSafety deepfake enforcement, eSafety AI companion findings

AI literacy in the primary years is under-resourced and urgently needed

Students in Years 5 and 6 are being given AI tools at the same time data shows they are least equipped to critically evaluate AI content; the rollout of capability without the corresponding literacy creates a dependency gap.

Sources: EdWeek Research Center survey, NSWEduChat rollout to Year 5, OECD TALIS data on lower secondary AI use

Student voice is becoming a structural part of AI policy design

Multiple US initiatives are formalising student input into AI governance; this is a model that aligns with existing Australian frameworks around student agency and is directly applicable to school-level policy review processes.

Sources: AASA/MIT RAISE student policy workshop, NYC public feedback period, Percy Julian student faculty panel, April 8

Where experts disagree

Live debates worth understanding before deciding

How explicit should school AI policy be?

One viewNYC's preliminary guidance draws hard limits – no AI for grading, discipline, or biometric data – treating governance as a series of prohibitions that protect students. (NYC DoE)
The other viewISNSW's AI portal and the Australian national framework emphasise principles-based guidance, leaving schools to determine their own implementation, on the grounds that no single pathway is right for every context. (ISNSW AI portal, Dept of Education Framework)
The tensionRules-based governance protects students but may lag behind rapidly changing tools; principles-based governance is flexible but creates uneven protections across schools, particularly for students in less well-resourced settings.

Should AI tools for students be introduced in the primary years at all?

One viewNSW DoE has rolled out NSWEduChat to all students from Year 5, arguing early access to a safe, scaffolded AI builds capability and normalises guided use. (NSW DoE)
The other viewParent survey data and primary teacher reports suggest strong hesitancy: 42% of parents of primary-age children do not support AI tool use at that developmental stage, and 61% of primary teachers report their students cannot distinguish AI-generated from non-AI content. (EdWeek Research Center, KidsAITools 2026 Parent Survey)
The tensionRolling out AI access before students have the literacy to evaluate AI content may embed dependency rather than competence; the question for primary schools is whether capability or literacy should come first – and what "safe" AI access actually means at Year 5.

AI deepfake harm: education response or criminal justice response?

One vieweSafety and school wellbeing advocates emphasise the school's role in prevention, early detection, survivor support, and toolkit-based incident management – treating it primarily as a community and wellbeing issue. (eSafety Commissioner)
The other viewSouth Australia and other jurisdictions are criminalising creation and sharing of AI-generated intimate images, including by students as young as 16, framing the problem as one requiring legal deterrence rather than pastoral response. (SBS News)
The tensionSchools are caught between a welfare response (support the victim, educate the perpetrator) and a legal response (report to police, face criminal proceedings); without clear national guidance on when and how to escalate, schools face significant liability and inconsistency.

Worth watching

Developments to keep an eye on

LAK26 conference now concluded – research outputs incoming

The 16th Annual Learning Analytics and Knowledge Conference (Bergen, Norway, 27 April – 1 May 2026) concluded this week. Flagged in last week's briefing as a high-quality venue for causal research on AI in classrooms, the conference presented the NC State "sticky teacher attention" paper among others. Proceedings and papers will be published over the next 4--6 weeks. Worth monitoring for findings relevant to AI tutoring system design and teacher circulation in primary classrooms.

Character.AI's regulatory response to eSafety: a model for platform accountability?

Following eSafety's transparency notice process, Character.AI introduced age assurance measures for Australian users in early 2026 and removed chat functionality from its under-18s experience. Chub.ai geo-blocked Australia entirely. This is the first significant instance of an AI companion platform materially changing its product for the Australian market in response to regulatory pressure – and it happened without new legislation, using existing Online Safety Act powers. If this pattern holds, it suggests eSafety's transparency notice mechanism is more powerful than its formal enforcement profile suggests. Worth tracking as an emerging model for platform accountability in the Australian context.
