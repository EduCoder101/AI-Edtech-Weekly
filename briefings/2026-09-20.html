Weekly briefing · 14–20 September 2026

NESA writes the AI assessment rules into law as America's teacher union extracts binding guardrails from Microsoft, and Florida makes classroom AI conditional on parental consent

Issue of 20 September 2026

At a glance

The week confirmed that AI governance is moving from advice to enforceable instruments. NESA published the formal ACE Rules changes behind this month's take-home assessment cap, adding two requirements with real bite: marks must not reflect a student's use of generative AI, and detection software must not be a school's main safeguard against malpractice. In the United States, Microsoft signed a legally binding agreement with the American Federation of Teachers barring the use of student data to train AI and banning engagement-optimised features, with OpenAI and Anthropic finalising their own agreements and Google conspicuously silent, while Florida mandated district AI policies with a parent consent and opt-out regime. At home, new Instructure research found only 28 per cent of Australian education professionals are confident their institution has a clear AI strategy, and the Raspberry Pi Foundation's Experience AI program arrived in Australia, free and aligned to the Australian Curriculum.

Key signals

  • NESA's formal ACE Rules changes confirm one take-home task per Stage 6 course at a maximum 15 per cent weighting (HSC courses from Term 4 2026, Preliminary from Term 1 2027), require that marks reflect the student's own knowledge and not their use of generative AI, and direct schools not to rely on AI detection tools as the main safeguard against malpractice.
  • Microsoft and the American Federation of Teachers announced a legally binding agreement on 15 September covering every school Microsoft contracts with from 1 November: no training AI on student or educator data, no sale or advertising use, no features designed to foster emotional attachment or extend engagement beyond the learning task, verified by third-party audits.
  • Florida's State Board of Education unanimously approved rules on 16 September requiring every district and charter school to adopt AI policies by 1 July 2027, notify parents and obtain consent before students use AI products, and offer a non-AI instructional alternative of similar quality where parents object.
  • Instructure's survey of 215 Australian education professionals across primary, secondary and higher education found only 28 per cent are quite or extremely confident their institution has a clear strategy for where AI fits, despite three in four rating career-long learner adaptability as important.
  • Experience AI, the Raspberry Pi Foundation and Google DeepMind literacy program used in more than 195 countries, is now available to Australian teachers through Adelaide University's CSER Group, adapted to Australian Curriculum Version 9.0 with free professional learning and a target of 150,000 learners across Australia and New Zealand.

Considerations for the Executive

  • Have Stage 6 leaders check marking rubrics, not just task schedules, against the formal ACE Rules wording before Term 4: any criterion that could reward AI-polished production needs rewriting, and the school's malpractice process should match NESA's position that detection scores are one input into teacher professional review, never proof.
  • Adopt the AFT agreement as the school's vendor benchmark: its terms (no training on student data, no engagement-optimised features, audit rights, plain-language disclosure to families) make a ready procurement checklist, and with three major vendors now accepting union-negotiated terms, the case for a sector-negotiated Australian equivalent through ISA or AISNSW is stronger than ever.
  • Evaluate Experience AI for the K-6 professional learning programme, where TALIS located the capability gap, and quietly cost what a Florida-style consent-and-alternative model would take to operate, so the school has an informed answer ready if Australian parent sentiment pushes policy in that direction.

1

NESA formalises the new assessment rules: marks must not reflect AI use, and detection tools cannot be the main safeguard

NESA / NSW Government | official notice published this week

NESA has now published the formal changes to the ACE Rules that give legal effect to the take-home assessment limits announced at the start of September. The updated rules confirm that Preliminary and HSC assessment programs may include no more than one take-home task, worth a maximum of 15 per cent of school-based assessment, with the limit applying to HSC courses from Term 4 2026 and Preliminary courses from Term 1 2027. Courses with a major project or HSC practical exam are exempt. The ACE Rules and the assessment and reporting requirements in each syllabus will be updated before the end of Term 3, which ends in less than a fortnight.

Two requirements in the formal notice go beyond the original headlines. First, schools must design formal assessment tasks so that marks reflect the student's own knowledge, skills and understanding, and marks must not reflect a student's use of generative AI. Second, NESA states explicitly that schools should not rely on AI detection tools as the main safeguard against malpractice: if used at all, detection software is to be treated as one input into teacher professional review, never as sole proof of AI use. Each school remains responsible for its own policy on whether generative AI is permissible in assessment. The NESA Board will develop subject-specific advice and teacher guidance from next year for the 2028 HSC and beyond, and Independent Schools NSW has published a summary of the rule changes for member schools.

Sources: Changes to ACE Rules concerning take-home assessment tasks and the use of artificial intelligence – NESA, September 2026; New limit on take-home assessment tasks from Term 4 2026 – NESA, September 2026; ACE rule changes – Independent Schools NSW, September 2026; NSW schools face new take-home assessment rules next term – EduChoice, September 2026.

Why it mattersThis is the compliance detail the September announcement lacked, and it lands two weeks before Term 4 begins. The "marks must not reflect AI use" wording has direct implications for marking rubrics, not just task design: any criterion that could reward polished AI-assisted production over demonstrated understanding now needs review. The explicit demotion of detection software also settles an argument still running in many staffrooms, and aligns NSW with the position most researchers reached some time ago. Assessment leaders should read the ACE Rules changes directly rather than working from news coverage.
2

Microsoft signs a legally binding AI agreement with America's teacher union, and the rest of the industry is expected to follow

AP / Education Week / Euronews | 15 September 2026

Microsoft and the American Federation of Teachers announced a legally binding agreement on 15 September that limits how the company can use student and educator data in every school it contracts with, effective from 1 November. The agreement bars Microsoft from using student or educator data to train AI models (with a narrow exception for student safety), prohibits selling the data or using it for advertising or product development, and requires third-party audits to verify compliance plus plain-language disclosures to families explaining how tools work. Notably, it also prohibits AI companions or any feature "designed to foster emotional attachment or dependency" or to keep students engaged longer than a learning task requires.

The AFT described the agreement as a potential industry standard, and the immediate responses suggest it may become one. OpenAI called it "an important milestone for AI safety and privacy in schools" and said it is finalising its own agreement with the AFT; Anthropic said it is working with the union to shape "a gold standard for safety and privacy". Google, the dominant provider of education technology in US schools, has not said whether it will offer similar protections, and coverage this week repeatedly singled out that silence.

Sources: Microsoft commits to sweeping AI privacy rules for students. Will other tech giants follow? – AP via News4Jax, 15 September 2026; Microsoft Commits to AI Guardrails for Schools. Will Other Tech Giants Follow? – Education Week, September 2026; Microsoft introduces AI guardrails for US schools amid growing backlash – Euronews, 15 September 2026; Microsoft, AFT Agree Student AI Privacy Rules, Require Audits – CloudLink, September 2026.

Why it mattersA union has just extracted, by negotiation, stronger and more enforceable commitments than most governments have achieved by regulation: binding terms, third-party audits and an outright ban on engagement-optimised features. For an Australian school, the agreement is a ready-made benchmark for vendor evaluation, and the engagement-dependency clause is language worth borrowing for any local procurement standard. It also strengthens the case made in earlier briefings for a sector-negotiated Australian equivalent through ISA or AISNSW: the AFT has demonstrated that collective negotiation with vendors works.
3

Only 28 per cent of Australian education professionals say their institution has a clear AI strategy

Instructure / The Educator | research covered this week

New Australian research from Instructure, drawn from a June-July 2026 survey of 215 education professionals across primary, secondary and higher education and presented around CanvasCon ANZ in Sydney, puts a number on what many school leaders already suspect: only 28 per cent of respondents were quite or extremely confident their institution has a clear strategy for where AI fits within its learning pathways. Three in four said helping learners retrain and adapt as careers change is important, but only 44 per cent were confident their institution's current pathways match those priorities. Instructure's Chief Customer Officer Rachel Orston described the result as "a gap between ambition and execution", and the coverage in The Educator this week argued that giving students a list of what they can and cannot do is not a strategy: young people are looking to educators for guidance on using AI effectively, critically and responsibly.

Sources: The AI strategy gap in Australian schools – The Educator K-12, September 2026; Australian educators back lifelong learning but lag on AI – ChannelLife, September 2026; AI researcher Toby Walsh and neuroscientist Lila Landowski headline CanvasCon ANZ 2026 – Instructure, 2026.

Why it mattersThe finding is a useful mirror. Most Australian schools now have an AI policy; far fewer have an AI strategy that connects classroom practice, staff capability and assessment design to a view of where the school is heading. For a school that does have a coherent position, the 28 per cent figure is also context worth sharing with the executive: it suggests the school is further ahead of the sector than internal frustrations might imply. The vendor origin of the survey warrants the usual caution, but the sample spans sectors and the finding is consistent with the TALIS capability data reported in August.
4

Florida mandates AI policies in every district, with parental consent and a non-AI alternative required

Florida State Board of Education | 16 September 2026

Florida's State Board of Education unanimously approved two sets of AI rules on 16 September, one for K-12 schools and one for state colleges. Every public school district and charter school must adopt rules for AI use within its internet safety policy by 1 July 2027, covering how students and teachers may use AI, student safety, and data protection. The most consequential requirement is the parental consent regime: districts must notify parents if their child will use an AI product, obtain consent, and offer an alternative non-AI instructional tool "similar in instructional quality" if a parent objects.

Sources: Florida approves statewide AI rules for K-12 schools and colleges – Central Florida Public Media, 16 September 2026; Florida approves new AI policies for K-12 schools, state colleges – Bay News 9, 16 September 2026; State board approves AI Guidelines for K-12 schools, colleges – News4Jax, 17 September 2026.

Why it mattersFlorida has taken a different route from the New York and Los Angeles moratoriums: rather than pausing student-facing AI, it has made classroom AI use conditional on parent-by-parent consent, with a guaranteed opt-out. That model will be attractive to Australian policymakers because it appears to preserve both choice and access, but it carries a heavy operational load: schools must effectively run two parallel instructional pathways wherever AI is embedded in learning materials. If parent sentiment in Australia continues to harden along the lines of the Heads Up Alliance petition, a consent-and-alternative model is a plausible ask, and it is worth thinking now about what it would cost to operate.
5

Experience AI arrives in Australian classrooms: free, curriculum-aligned AI literacy from Foundation up

Adelaide University / Raspberry Pi Foundation | national coverage this week

Coverage this week spread the news that Experience AI, the AI literacy program developed by the Raspberry Pi Foundation and Google DeepMind, is now available to Australian teachers through Adelaide University's Computer Science Education Research (CSER) Group, which announced the partnership on 2 September. The CSER team has adapted the materials to the Australian Curriculum Version 9.0 and AITSL standards, with Australian contexts and examples, and will support implementation through its long-running free online professional learning for teachers. Teachers receive complete classroom kits: lesson plans with speaker notes, slides, videos, student activities and assessments, with no prior AI knowledge required. The program is funded in Australia by Google.org, is used in more than 195 countries, and the partnership aims to reach 150,000 learners across Australia and New Zealand.

Sources: Global AI teaching program arrives in Australian classrooms – The Educator K-12, September 2026; Delivering free AI learning resources for Australian classrooms – Education Matters Magazine, September 2026; Supporting AI education for 150,000 learners in Aotearoa New Zealand and Australia – Raspberry Pi Foundation, 2026.

Why it mattersThis is the most substantial free, curriculum-aligned AI literacy offering yet available to Australian primary and secondary teachers, and it arrives exactly where TALIS located the capability gap: primary teachers who say they lack the knowledge and skills to teach with or about AI. For professional learning planning, the CSER connection matters as much as the content, since the group has a decade of credibility delivering free digital technologies PL to Australian teachers. The Google funding line should be noted in any evaluation, but the materials themselves are classroom-ready and the PISA finding that structured AI literacy practice is associated with better outcomes gives the program a clear pedagogical rationale.

Where experts disagree

Live debates worth understanding before deciding

How bad are Australia's PISA 2025 results really?

One viewJohn Hattie at ACER's PISA event: the doom narrative is harmful and false, Australia sits near the top of the world once statistically comparable countries are grouped, and teachers deserve thanks rather than blame
The other viewCritics including the Bad Mathematics blog and earlier EducationHQ analysis: Australia ranked 17th, reading and maths are at record lows, and reframing that as a top-ten result spins away a two-decade decline
The tensionBoth readings use the same data; the disagreement is about whether relative international standing or absolute decline is the right lens, and which story schools should tell their communities

Do vendor guardrails answer the AI-in-schools question?

One viewAFT, Microsoft and OpenAI: binding privacy and safety agreements remove the main risks and clear the way for responsible classroom AI
The other viewThe restriction movement in practice: NYC and LAUSD moratoriums remain in force and parent backlash continues, because the core concern is what AI does to learning, not what vendors do with data
The tensionPrivacy guardrails and learning outcomes are different problems; a school can satisfy every data protection standard and still face the harder question of whether, when and how students should use AI at all

Worth watching

Developments to keep an eye on

SA Royal Commission commissioners start 1 October, and the terms of reference are now overdue

South Australia's Royal Commission into AI commences on 1 October, yet as of this briefing the terms of reference, promised within four to six weeks of the 10 August announcement, have still not been published. That window has now effectively closed, which means the terms may land at the same moment the commissioners begin work, compressing the time available for schools and sector bodies to prepare submissions. Education remains confirmed in scope. The practical advice from previous briefings stands: a submission grounded in real classroom experience, routed directly or through ISA and AISNSW, will be worth preparing the moment the process opens.

Sources: Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence – Office for AI, SA Government; South Australia announces Royal Commission into AI – Gadens, August 2026.

Participatory AI governance gets an Australian roadmap

Researchers from the University of Sydney, UTS and the University of Bergen, led by Professor Kalervo Gulson, have published work under an APPI Policy Challenge Grant testing participatory methods for AI policymaking within the NSW EdTech ecosystem, working with students, teachers, policymakers and technologists. Gulson frames the goal as shifting "from policy that is done to schools, to policy that is done with them", which echoes UNESCO's ministerial commitment this month that teachers should have a say in which AI tools enter classrooms. If the approach gains traction with NSW policymakers, consultation windows on AI policy may become genuine design opportunities rather than box-ticking, and schools with documented practitioner evidence will be best placed to use them.

Sources: Shaping the future of AI in schools: new roadmap for participatory approaches to AI in education – APPI, September 2026; Democratising AI in schools: ensuring policy keeps pace with technology – University of Sydney.
