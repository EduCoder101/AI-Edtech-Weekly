# AI in EdTech Weekly -- 6 September 2026

*A research briefing for the AI Integration Lead, Sydney independent K-12 school. Covering 31 August -- 6 September 2026.*

---

## Top Stories

### 1. NSW locks in the HSC take-home cap: one task per course, maximum 15%, from Term 4

**NSW Government / NESA | 1--2 September 2026**

The Minns Government's NESA directive, flagged in last week's Worth Watching, has been confirmed as binding rules. From the start of Term 4 2026, NSW schools are limited to a maximum of one take-home assessment task per HSC course, worth no more than 15% of school-based assessment (7.5% of the total HSC mark). Preliminary courses follow the same limit from Term 1 2027. Major works in creative arts, Design and Technology, and English Extension 2 are exempt. NESA has also been commissioned to develop a common approach for detecting and responding to inappropriate AI use across school-based tasks that remain in place.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This is a binding rule, not guidance, and it applies to NSW independent schools through NESA's authority over HSC registration. Any school running more than one take-home task per HSC course needs to revise its assessment schedule before Term 4 opens on 13 October. AISNSW has confirmed it will issue implementation guidance shortly. The key practical implication goes beyond counting tasks: schools should review whether their academic integrity procedures describe a process (professional conversation and re-demonstration under supervision) rather than a product (detection software), since that aligns with the direction NESA has signalled for its forthcoming detection guidance.

### 2. NSWEduChat rolls out to all public school students from Year 5 -- and independent schools are not included

**NSW Department of Education | Term 4 2026**

Following a Term 3 trial across 50 schools, the NSW Department of Education is rolling out NSWEduChat to all public school students from Year 5 to Year 12 at the start of Term 4. The tool uses Socratic questioning rather than giving direct answers, is aligned to the NSW curriculum, stores no data on external servers, and sits behind departmental security infrastructure. A teacher-facing version has been available to all NSW public school staff since early 2026.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This creates a visible divide in how AI is provisioned for students across NSW. Public school students now have a free, state-funded, privacy-safe AI tool designed to support learning -- starting in Year 5, the year level this briefing's reader teaches. Independent school students have access to whatever their school has arranged, or nothing. Parents will start comparing notes across the two sectors; schools should be able to explain their AI tool strategy clearly and, if they have not yet formed one, should treat this as a prompt to do so before Term 4 parent conversations begin. The tool's design -- Socratic mode, no external data storage, curriculum-aligned -- is also a useful benchmark for evaluating any third-party AI product under consideration.

Sources: [Roll out of purpose-built AI education tool to all NSW public school students](https://education.nsw.gov.au/news/latest-news/roll-out-of-purpose-built-ai-education-tool-to-all-public-school) -- Education NSW; [NSW to roll out purpose-built AI education tool to all public school students from Year 5](https://www.nsw.gov.au/ministerial-releases/nsw-to-roll-out-purpose-built-ai-education-tool-to-all-public-school-students-from-year-5) -- NSW Government; [State-wide rollout of purpose-built AI learning tool](https://educationmattersmag.com.au/state-wide-roll-out-of-purpose-built-ai-learning-tool/) -- Education Matters Magazine; [NSW rolls out EduChat AI tool to public school students](https://www.innovationaus.com/nsw-rolls-out-educhat-ai-tool-to-all-public-school-students/) -- InnovationAus.

### 3. New York City bans generative AI for 600,000 K-8 students -- reversing its own 2023 position

**NYC Mayor's Office / Chalkbeat / CNN | 2 September 2026**

Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Schools Chancellor Kamar Samuels announced a one-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI across New York City public schools, effective from 10 September. The ban covers approximately 600,000 students across around 1,600 schools in grades 2K through 8. AI functions will be disabled on 38 district-wide edtech products. Companion chatbots are banned at all year levels, including high school. High school students retain access to five approved tools in career and technical education programmes. The policy package includes twice-yearly AI critical thinking modules for high school students. This is a complete reversal of the district's 2023 position, when NYC Schools described AI restrictions as "knee-jerk fear."

**Why it matters for K-12:** This is the world's most watched school system pulling an emergency brake three years after leading the push to embrace AI. The arguments cited -- developmental appropriateness for primary-age children, the absence of evidence that AI access improves durable learning (citing the OECD crutch effect data), and companion chatbot risks -- are all live in Australian state education debates. Note that the NYC ban and the NSW assessment cap are, in an important sense, addressing different problems: NSW is constraining assessment design to protect integrity; NYC is restricting AI access to protect cognitive development. An independent school needs a position on both. The companion chatbot dimension is also distinct from academic AI: regardless of what a school decides about AI for schoolwork, the pastoral implications of AI companion products marketed directly to children deserve a separate conversation with senior leadership and parents.

Sources: [Mayor Mamdani and Chancellor Samuels: students first with national AI policy](https://www.nyc.gov/mayors-office/news/2026/09/mayor-mamdani-and-chancellor-samuels-put-students-first-with-nat) -- NYC Mayor's Office, 2 September 2026; [NYC bans classroom AI through 8th grade](https://www.cnn.com/2026/09/02/tech/new-york-city-classroom-ai-ban) -- CNN, 2 September 2026; [What to know about NYC's generative AI ban and screen time limits](https://www.chalkbeat.org/newyork/2026/09/02/what-to-know-about-nyc-public-schools-generative-ai-ban-screen-time-limits/) -- Chalkbeat, 2 September 2026; [NYC reverses its own AI embrace: generative tools banned for 600,000 K-8 students](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/326422/20260903/nyc-reverses-its-own-ai-embrace-generative-tools-banned-600000-k-8-students.htm) -- TechTimes, 3 September 2026.

### 4. The OECD crutch effect is now driving live policy decisions

**OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026 | Widely cited from 31 August**

The OECD's biennial Digital Education Outlook, published in January 2026, has moved from a research document to an active policy instrument this week. NYC's moratorium announcement explicitly cited its findings, as did statements accompanying NESA's assessment cap. The key statistics now shaping school policy decisions: students using AI were 48% more successful completing tasks, but their performance dropped 17% when the AI was removed. In a separate study cited by the OECD, 80% of students who wrote AI-assisted essays could not afterwards recall what they had written about. The OECD distinguishes between "shortcut use" (common among disadvantaged students; worsens learning outcomes over time) and "tutor use" (structured, human-supervised; modestly positive). Without explicit pedagogical guidance, AI tends toward shortcut use. Disadvantaged students are disproportionately likely to shortcut -- which, the report argues, risks widening rather than closing the achievement gap.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This data is now moving policy decisions in real time and your school's board and leadership team are likely to encounter it. The 80% amnesia figure and the 17% performance drop are accurate, memorable, and are appearing in mainstream newspaper coverage. For staff professional learning, the most important nuance is what the data does not say: structured AI use, explicitly taught and supervised, consistently produces different results from unstructured access. Schools that frame their AI approach around this distinction -- rather than treating AI as uniformly beneficial or uniformly harmful -- will be better positioned for the next wave of parent and media attention.

Sources: [OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026: Exploring Effective Uses of Generative AI in Education](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html) -- OECD; [Summary of OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026](https://ciddl.org/summary-of-oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026/) -- CIDDL.

### 5. Washington DC prohibits AI from grading, disciplinary, and IEP decisions

**DC Office of the State Superintendent of Education | September 2026**

Washington DC's Office of the State Superintendent of Education released AI policy for 2026-27 explicitly prohibiting AI from three categories of decision: grading, disciplinary action, and Individualised Education Program (IEP) determinations for students with disabilities. Any decision affecting an individual student's rights, welfare, or educational standing must remain with a human educator. The policy allows classroom and planning uses of AI but requires that human accountability cannot be delegated to an automated system for high-stakes decisions. In the same week, the Los Angeles Unified School District (the second-largest in the US) restricted student access to generative AI platforms for 2026-27 and convened a new Generative AI Ad Hoc Committee to develop formal policy recommendations by end of year.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The DC prohibition on AI in IEP and disciplinary decisions has clear Australian crossover. Schools have corresponding obligations under the Disability Discrimination Act and student welfare policies; the logic is identical -- AI cannot bear accountability for decisions that affect individual students' rights. Any Australian school policy that permits AI to generate draft IEP goals, draft disciplinary recommendations, or individual student reports should explicitly require human review and documented sign-off. A school that cannot point to that safeguard in its policy is exposed if a decision is later challenged. This is worth a specific line in your AI governance policy if it is not already there.

Sources: [DC schools get new AI rules: grading and IEP decisions stay off-limits](https://hoodline.com/2026/09/dc-schools-get-new-ai-rules-grading-and-iep-decisions-stay-off-limits/) -- Hoodline, September 2026; [LA school district restricts generative AI access for all students](https://americanbazaaronline.com/2026/09/04/la-school-district-restricts-generative-ai-access-for-all-students-487618/) -- American Bazaar Online, 4 September 2026.

## Worth Watching

**1. UNESCO Digital Learning Week opens 8 September: expect communiques responding to the NYC ban and OECD data**

UNESCO's annual flagship education technology convening -- "Facts | Frictions | Frontiers" -- runs 8 to 11 September in Paris. The theme directly signals intent to address the backlash visible this week in the NYC moratorium, the OECD crutch effect data, and the growing academic integrity gap across US states. UNESCO communiques and position papers from this event typically inform Australian federal and state education departments within weeks. Watch for any shift in the international consensus framing, particularly around the evidence threshold for AI access in primary classrooms and the use of AI in high-stakes assessment -- both directly relevant to the decisions NESA and other state authorities are currently making.

Sources: [UNESCO Digital Learning Week 2026: Education in the Age of AI -- Facts, Frictions, Frontiers](https://www.unesco.org/en/weeks/digital-learning) -- UNESCO.

**2. SA Royal Commission terms of reference due mid-September -- submission window opening**

The terms of reference for South Australia's Royal Commission into AI remain on schedule for mid-September, with commissioners commencing hearings on 1 October 2026 and a final report due 1 July 2027. Education is confirmed as a core focus. When terms are published, the submission window opens. This is the moment for school sector evidence -- including from ISA and NCEC -- to reach the formal public record. Schools that have gathered data on student AI use patterns, teacher workload impacts, or assessment integrity incidents should consider whether to contribute through sector bodies. A Royal Commission finding about evidence standards for AI in schools would carry weight beyond South Australia.

Sources: [Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence](https://ai.sa.gov.au/royal-commission-into-artificial-intelligence) -- Office for AI, SA Government; [Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence announced](https://www.dpc.sa.gov.au/news/royal-commission-into-artificial-intelligence-announced) -- SA Department of the Premier and Cabinet, August 2026.
