# AI in EdTech Weekly -- 5 July 2026

*A research briefing for the AI Integration Lead, Sydney independent K-12 school. Covering 29 June -- 5 July 2026.*

## Top Stories

### 1. "Faster Completion, Less Learning": landmark study of 3.2 million interactions finds AI is shrinking what students retain

**arXiv preprint | May 2026 | Rismanchian, Uzun, Matayoshi, Cosyn, Kurd-Misto et al.**

A large-scale study drawing on 3.2 million ALEKS learning interactions across more than a decade has produced the most rigorous population-level evidence yet that generative AI is altering not just how students work but how much they retain. Researchers exploited variation between AI-susceptible tasks (text-based word problems) and AI-resistant tasks (interactive graph-based problems) as a natural quasi-experiment. Key findings: study time on AI-susceptible problems declined 31.3% for high school students over eleven quarters since ChatGPT's release; proctored retention assessments show a 25% cumulative decline in odds of correct response on non-proctored assessments. Primary school students (Grade 5 equivalent) showed no detectable effect; middle schoolers showed a 9% decline; high schoolers 31.3%. The authors introduce the term "cognitive surrender" to describe the pattern -- not selective offloading of discrete tasks, but wholesale relinquishment of active learning, where students adopt AI-generated outputs with minimal scrutiny.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The Grade 5 finding is directly relevant for primary teachers: there is no detectable cognitive surrender effect at that year level, which suggests the risk accumulates as students encounter more text-heavy, AI-susceptible tasks in secondary school. For a Year 5 teacher, the implication is about building AI-resistant learning habits now -- procedural fluency, oral explanation, hands-on problem-solving -- before students reach year levels where the effect is measurable. The "cognitive surrender" term also gives educators precise, non-alarmist language for student conversations: the question is not whether AI is used but whether the student remains cognitively in control. This study also provides the strongest empirical support to date for pedagogical approaches that require students to explain, reconstruct, or apply their work -- rather than just submit it.

Sources: arXiv, ["Faster Completion, Less Learning: Generative AI Reduced Study Time on Math Problems and the Knowledge They Build"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21629), May 2026; Dr Philippa Hardman, ["From AI Tutors to AI Study Mates"](https://drphilippahardman.substack.com/p/from-ai-tutors-to-ai-study-mates), June 2026.

### 2. WEF: the AI divide that matters is literacy, not access

**World Economic Forum | 1 July 2026**

The World Economic Forum published an article on 1 July drawing on the joint European Commission and OECD AI Literacy (AILit) Framework to argue that the defining educational equity question of the coming decade is not who has access to AI but who understands how to question, evaluate, and direct AI systems -- and who simply consumes outputs. The framework establishes a three-dimension model for AI literacy: understanding how AI works, using AI purposefully and critically, and shaping AI systems through ethical engagement. The article highlights a stark teacher preparation gap: 1 in 3 teachers currently uses AI in their work, while 3 in 4 report lacking the knowledge and skills needed to teach with AI. A 2025 European Commission survey found that 63% of respondents agreed everyone will need to be AI-literate by 2030.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The EC/OECD AILit Framework gives Australian educators a credible international reference point for designing AI literacy scope and sequence -- one that is not vendor-generated. The teacher gap finding (1 in 3 using, 3 in 4 unprepared to teach with AI) is consistent with Australian data and provides external validation for investing professional learning time in AI literacy rather than just AI tool adoption. The three-dimension model is a useful curriculum planning lens: most school AI programs currently address only the middle dimension (using AI). Schools that limit AI learning to tool proficiency are missing the understanding and shaping dimensions that the framework treats as equally important. The WEF article is useful for staff PL framing because it reframes AI literacy as an equity issue, not an efficiency one.

Sources: World Economic Forum, ["Beyond Access: Why AI Literacy Will Define the Future"](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2026/07/beyond-access-why-ai-literacy-will-define-the-future/), 1 July 2026; World Economic Forum, ["Shaping the Future of Learning: Education Readiness for the Age of AI"](https://reports.weforum.org/docs/WEF_Shaping_the_Future_of_Learning_2026.pdf), June 2026.

### 3. OAIC Children's Online Privacy Code: final code due December 2026 -- schools need a tool review now

**Office of the Australian Information Commissioner | 2026**

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner has released an exposure draft of Australia's first dedicated Children's Online Privacy Code, with public consultation closing 5 June 2026 and the final code required to be registered by 10 December 2026. Key provisions: organisations whose apps, websites, or games are "likely to be accessed by children" must obtain explicit consent before using children's personal information for targeted advertising; must enable children to request deletion of their data; and must notify children when a parent or carer is consenting on their behalf, or when geolocation is being tracked by another user. The scope is deliberately broad -- the "likely accessed by children" threshold captures general-purpose services that children use, not only services designed specifically for them.

**Why it matters for K-12:** Schools that have approved third-party AI tools for classroom use need to assess which of those tools would fall under the code's scope. The threshold that matters is "likely accessed by children" -- which is broad enough to capture AI writing assistants, adaptive learning platforms, and chatbot tools that also have free consumer versions. The December 10 deadline means schools have approximately five months to complete that review. This is a governance action, not a policy watch item: if approved tools are out of scope with the final code, schools may need to revisit procurement decisions before the code comes into force. The code sits alongside the eSafety Commissioner's online safety codes (in effect since December 2025) and should be read together when reviewing the school's AI tool inventory.

Sources: OAIC, ["Children's Online Privacy Code"](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-registers/privacy-codes/childrens-online-privacy-code), 2026; OAIC, ["OAIC releases Exposure Draft of the Children's Online Privacy Code"](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/oaic-releases-exposure-draft-of-the-childrens-online-privacy-code), 2026; MinterEllison, ["OAIC's Children's Online Privacy Code: what to expect"](https://www.minterellison.com/articles/oaics-childrens-online-privacy-code-what-to-expect), 2026; Corrs Chambers Westgarth, ["OAIC's Children's Online Privacy exposure draft: from consultation to code"](https://www.corrs.com.au/insights/oaics-childrens-online-privacy-exposure-draft-from-consultation-to-code), 2026.

### 4. NSWEduChat: what Australia's largest school AI deployment tells independent schools about implementation

**NSW Department of Education | Term 4 2025 rollout, operational throughout 2026**

Following a trial in 50 schools, the NSW Department of Education has rolled out NSWEduChat to all Year 5 to Year 12 students across NSW public schools -- the most significant government-led AI deployment in Australian schools to date. Three design decisions define the tool: data sovereignty (all data stays in Australia and is accessible only to the department); curriculum alignment (content is filtered and connected to NSW syllabus requirements); and pedagogical guardrails (the student-facing mode asks open-ended questions and prompts reasoning rather than supplying direct answers). The tool is free for staff and students. Alongside NSWEduChat, the department has also launched Lesson Library, an online platform providing curriculum resources written by NSW teachers for NSW teachers, designed to reduce the AI-related shortcut risk by giving teachers high-quality starting points.

**Why it matters for K-12:** For independent school leaders, NSWEduChat is the most directly comparable reference point available for evaluating their own AI tool choices -- particularly the three design decisions NSW made. The data sovereignty requirement, the pedagogical guardrail design, and the Year 5 entry point are each defensible with reference to research (the "Faster Completion, Less Learning" study found no cognitive surrender effect at Grade 5, which is consistent with the Year 5 threshold). Independent schools using commercial AI tools without equivalent data controls or pedagogical guardrails are in a materially different risk position, which is worth making explicit in your AI strategy documentation. The Lesson Library initiative is also worth tracking: it addresses a less-discussed AI risk, which is that teachers under time pressure use AI to generate generic lesson content rather than curriculum-aligned material.

Sources: NSW Department of Education, ["NSWEduChat"](https://education.nsw.gov.au/teaching-and-learning/education-for-a-changing-world/nsweduchat), 2026; NSW Government, ["NSW to roll out purpose-built AI education tool to all public school students from Year 5"](https://www.nsw.gov.au/ministerial-releases/nsw-to-roll-out-purpose-built-ai-education-tool-to-all-public-school-students-from-year-5), 2025; Education Matters Magazine, ["State-wide rollout of purpose-built AI learning tool"](https://educationmattersmag.com.au/state-wide-roll-out-of-purpose-built-ai-learning-tool/), 2025; NSW Department of Education, ["Roll out of purpose-built AI education tool to NSW public school students"](https://education.nsw.gov.au/news/latest-news/roll-out-of-purpose-built-ai-education-tool-to-all-public-school), 2025.

### 5. Brookings Global Task Force: risks of AI for students currently outweigh benefits

**Brookings Institution | January 2026**

The Brookings Institution's Global Task Force on AI and Education published "A New Direction for Students in an AI World: Prosper, Prepare, Protect" following a year-long study involving students, teachers, parents, education leaders, and technologists in over 50 countries. The central finding: given current patterns of use, the risks of generative AI for students outweigh its potential benefits. Identified risks include erosion of student privacy, weakened institutional trust, growing cognitive dependence, and risk of widening existing inequalities. The report explicitly does not recommend banning AI. Instead, it offers a three-pillar framework -- Prosper (build AI that helps students thrive), Prepare (equip every learner to navigate AI critically), Protect (establish meaningful guardrails) -- with specific recommendations for governments, technology companies, education system leaders, and families.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The Brookings report is significant for two reasons. First, it is the broadest multi-country qualitative study published this year and its "risks outweigh benefits under current conditions" finding cannot be dismissed as outlier pessimism -- it is a synthesis across 50 countries. Second, the "current patterns of use" qualifier matters: the risk-benefit calculus is not inherent to AI but is a function of implementation conditions. The Prosper-Prepare-Protect framework is useful for evaluating whether your school's current AI governance is balanced across all three pillars -- many schools have invested heavily in Protect (policy, acceptable use, detection) without equivalent investment in Prosper (ensuring AI actively helps students) or Prepare (building critical AI literacy). A governance audit against these three pillars would be a practical output from this briefing.

Sources: Brookings Institution, ["A new direction for students in an AI world: Prosper, prepare, protect"](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/a-new-direction-for-students-in-an-ai-world-prosper-prepare-protect/), January 2026; Brookings Institution, ["Brookings Global Task Force on AI in Education"](https://www.brookings.edu/projects/brookings-global-task-force-on-ai-in-education/), 2026; Day of AI, ["Preparing Students for an AI World: Key Takeaways from Brookings' New Report"](https://dayofai.org/news/preparing-students-for-an-ai-world-key-takeaways-from-brookings-new-report), 2026.

## Worth Watching

**1. OAIC Children's Online Privacy Code -- final code due 10 December 2026**

The consultation has closed and the OAIC is now drafting the final code. Watch for the final code's release in the second half of 2026, and specifically whether it clarifies the scope of "likely accessed by children" in relation to general-purpose AI tools used in school contexts. The [OAIC's code page](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-registers/privacy-codes/childrens-online-privacy-code) will publish updates. This is likely to generate legal guidance from sector bodies including ISNSW over the second half of the year -- worth checking the [ISNSW newsroom](https://www.isnsw.edu.au/newsroom) and [AISNSW](https://www.aisnsw.edu.au/) for sector-specific interpretation.

**2. TRAINE longitudinal study -- University of Melbourne**

The AERC Tracking AI in Education five-year study of how generative AI is changing teaching, learning, and assessment in Australian secondary schools has signalled new research activity but has not yet released findings publicly. When published, this will be the most substantive longitudinal Australian-specific evidence available. Check the [TRAINE project page](https://education.unimelb.edu.au/aerc/research/projects/Tracking-AI-in-Education-TRAINE) regularly.
