Weekly briefing · 24 May – 31 May 2026

Half of NSW secondary teachers cannot prevent AI cheating, a children's privacy code nears, and teacher unions enter the debate

Issue of 31 May 2026

At a glance

The largest Australian survey of educator attitudes to student AI use finds restrictions widely in place and widely ignored, and recommends restricting student access until durable learning benefits are demonstrated. The OAIC's Children's Online Privacy Code closes for consultation this week, with material consequences for how schools procure edtech, while America's largest teaching union calls for an end to student-facing AI in elementary schools and a levy on AI companies to fund teacher training.

Key signals

  • Learning First's survey of more than 4,000 NSW educators: around three-quarters of teachers whose students use AI report use in assessment tasks, despite over 80% saying their school has restrictions; about half say they cannot prevent AI-related plagiarism.
  • The report's central recommendation is the most restrictive position yet from a credible Australian body: restrict student AI access for schoolwork until research shows durable learning gains.
  • The OAIC Children's Online Privacy Code consultation closes 5 June; the final code, due by December, would tighten consent obligations for AI and edtech tools used with younger students.
  • The American Federation of Teachers' 10-point plan calls for no student-facing AI in elementary schools, no AI companions for under-16s, and a levy on AI companies to fund teacher training.
  • The EU Council adopted conclusions positioning teachers as designers and evaluators of classroom AI rather than passive users, a framing Australian policy has historically followed.

Considerations for the Executive

  • Audit the gap between the school's written AI policy and actual student practice rather than assuming restrictions are being observed.
  • Confirm whether AISNSW is making a submission on the Children's Online Privacy Code, and review consent processes for AI-enabled tools used with younger students.
  • Prepare an evidence-based position on the restrict-versus-teach debate that the school can defend to parents on both sides of it.

The stories in depth

Full analysis with linked sources
1

NSW survey of 4,000+ educators finds half of secondary teachers cannot prevent AI cheating – and calls for restricted access

Learning First / EducationHQ / Information Age | 26--29 May 2026

Consulting group Learning First released "AI use in schools: Taking action now," the largest Australian survey of educator attitudes toward student AI use to date, drawing on responses from more than 4,000 NSW teachers and school leaders across government, Catholic, and independent schools. The headline findings: about half of NSW secondary teachers report students are using AI for schoolwork; of those, approximately three-quarters say students use AI for assessment tasks – even though more than 80 per cent of those same teachers say their school has restrictions in place. About half of all teachers say they do not know how to prevent AI-related plagiarism or cheating. Eight in ten teachers and school leaders whose students use AI express worry about its future impact on education. The report's central recommendation is blunt: restrict student access to general-purpose AI tools for schoolwork until robust research demonstrates that such use measurably strengthens durable learning – not just task completion. The most urgent actions, it argues, concern senior secondary assessment linked to qualifications and post-secondary pathways.

Sources: Learning First, "AI use in schools: Taking action now", 26 May 2026; Medianet / News Hub, "New data shows school systems need to act on student AI use", May 2026; EducationHQ, "'Get AI out of schools': study reveals clear and present danger to student learning", 29 May 2026; Information Age (ACS), "Australian students using AI to complete assessments", 2026.

Why it mattersThis is the most substantial piece of new Australian empirical data on school AI use published this year, and its central finding – that restrictions are widely in place but widely ignored – should prompt every school to audit the gap between its written policy and what is actually happening in classrooms and at home. The recommendation to restrict access until evidence of durable learning exists is the most restrictive position any credible Australian education body has yet taken publicly, and it will sharpen debate with those who argue that building AI literacy requires students to use the tools. For a primary school context, the implication is also practical: if secondary teachers cannot enforce restrictions, primary schools assuming their students are not using AI for homework are likely wrong. The report is freely available and worth sharing with your principal team.

OAIC Children's Online Privacy Code: consultation closes this Friday

OAIC | Deadline 5 June 2026

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner's exposure draft of the Children's Online Privacy Code closes for public submissions at close of business on Friday 5 June 2026. The final Code must be registered by 10 December 2026. The draft Code, if enacted as written, would require entities providing online services to children to take steps consistent with the best interests of the child – a standard that goes beyond current Privacy Act obligations. Legal analysis from Baker McKenzie, Corrs, and MinterEllison confirms the Code would materially affect how schools procure and deploy AI and edtech tools, particularly for students aged 15 and under, where parental consent obligations are likely to be most stringent. Sector bodies including AISNSW and NCEC are best placed to make submissions on behalf of independent and Catholic schools if they have not already done so.

Sources: OAIC, "Children's Online Privacy Code", 2026; Baker McKenzie, "Australia: Children's Online Privacy Code Exposure Draft", May 2026; Corrs Chambers Westgarth, "OAIC's Children's Online Privacy exposure draft: from consultation to code", 2026.

Why it mattersThis is the most consequential pending privacy development for Australian schools in 2026. If the Code's final form requires parental consent rather than school-level approval for edtech tools used with younger students, schools will need to redesign their procurement and consent processes. Any AI-enabled tool – writing assistants, tutoring platforms, learning management data analytics – that processes personal data of children would be in scope. The window to shape the final instrument closes in four days. If your school has not yet considered a submission through a sector body, this is the last opportunity. The OAIC submission portal is at oaic.gov.au.
3

American Federation of Teachers calls for AI ban in elementary schools and a "Big Tech tax" to fund teacher training

AFT / Education Week / Local News Matters | 27--28 May 2026

Randi Weingarten, president of the 1.8-million-member American Federation of Teachers, released a 10-point AI plan on 27 May 2026 that is the first formal position paper on K-12 AI from a major US teaching union. The plan calls for: an end to student-facing AI tools in all elementary schools; a ban on screens and online assessment for pre-K through Grade 2; prohibition on "social companion" AI chatbots for anyone under 16; a levy on AI companies to fund a national teacher training fund; and binding vendor contracts at every K-12 school requiring demonstrated evidence of safety and learning benefit before deployment. Weingarten explicitly stated she is not calling for a blanket AI ban but rather for "getting the balance right." The plan comes a week after a separate Gallup/Walton Family Foundation survey found that 82 per cent of US teachers have received no formal guidance on how to apply AI to their work, and 44 per cent of teachers, principals, and district leaders have received no AI professional development of any kind.

Sources: Education Week, "Teachers' Union's AI Plan Seeks 'Big Tech Tax,' Elementary Screen Bans", May 2026; Local News Matters, "AFT AI school screen restrictions plan", 28 May 2026; Education Week, "Teachers Say Lack of AI Guidance Is a Major Problem", May 2026.

Why it mattersThe AFT plan matters in the Australian context less as a direct policy model and more as a leading indicator of where teacher union thinking is heading globally. Australia's unions – the AEU in particular – have so far been measured in their AI positions, but the pressures driving the AFT plan (evidence of learning harm, teacher workload, lack of guidance, safeguarding concerns) are identical in Australian schools. The elementary school ban proposal will intensify debate about whether primary-age students should have access to AI tools at all – a question directly relevant to a Year 5 teacher or a school that has rolled out writing assistants to primary students. The Big Tech levy proposal also signals a new political framing: that the cost of educator readiness should fall partly on the platforms that are disrupting classrooms.
4

EU Council passes conclusions calling for human-centred, teacher-led approach to AI in education

Council of the European Union | 11 May 2026

The Council of the European Union adopted formal conclusions on 11 May 2026 on "the role of teachers in the era of artificial intelligence." The conclusions call on EU member states to embed AI literacy into initial teacher training and professional development; encourage the development of education-specific AI tools (as distinct from general-purpose consumer products) with clear data protection and accountability requirements; address the impact of AI on teacher workload and well-being as a distinct policy obligation; and reduce inequalities in AI access between students. Notably, the conclusions place teachers not just as users of AI but as "guides, mentors and critical thinkers" who should contribute to the design and evaluation of AI tools used in their classrooms – a participatory governance model rather than a top-down deployment model. The resolution carries no binding legal force on member states but sets the direction for EU education policy and is consistent with the phased implementation of the EU AI Act, which classifies some educational AI systems as high-risk.

Sources: Council of the European Union, "AI in education: Council calls for human-centred approach", 11 May 2026; Digital Watch Observatory, "Council of the EU pushes for human-centred AI in education systems", 2026.

Why it mattersThe EU is now the leading international body producing specific, actionable governance language for AI in schools. While Australia is not bound by EU conclusions, Australian policy frameworks have historically tracked European approaches to digital governance – the Privacy Act's influence on the OAIC Children's Code is a direct example. Two elements of this resolution deserve attention in an Australian independent school context. First, the obligation to address AI's impact on teacher workload as a distinct policy matter (not just a professional development question) is a framing Australian schools could apply internally. Second, the call for education-specific AI tools – rather than general-purpose products – aligns with what NSW has done with NSWEduChat and raises a question for independent schools: are the AI tools your staff and students are using designed for educational contexts, or are they general consumer products that happen to be used in school?

APPI research proposes participatory governance model for AI in Australian schools

Australian Public Policy Institute / University of Sydney / UTS | May 2026

A research team led by Professor Kalervo Gulson (University of Sydney and University of Technology Sydney), supported by an APPI Policy Challenge Grant and in collaboration with the University of Bergen, published a new roadmap outlining participatory approaches to AI governance in Australian school education. The research was conducted within the NSW EdTech ecosystem and tested two participatory methods – involving students, teachers, policymakers, and technology companies – to inform school AI policy. The core argument is that AI governance in schools should not be designed by technology vendors or government agencies in isolation, but through structured processes that include the groups most directly affected. The report identifies equity as a central consideration: participatory processes, it argues, surface concerns about AI's effects on different student populations that top-down policy frameworks tend to miss.

Sources: Australian Public Policy Institute, "Shaping the future of AI in schools: new roadmap for participatory approaches to AI in education", May 2026; APPI, "Governing AI in education: Policy opportunities for building participatory and equitable futures", 2026.

Why it mattersMost of the national AI frameworks produced to date – including the Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools – have been designed by government and agency bodies with limited direct input from students and classroom teachers. This research offers a practical alternative model, and the methods tested are small-scale enough to be adapted at a school level. For an AI integration lead designing professional learning or reviewing AI policy, the participatory framing is also useful as a change management approach: staff who help shape AI policy are more likely to understand and apply it than those who receive it as a document. The full report is available at the APPI website.

Where the evidence agrees

Independent sources pointing the same way

Student AI use is already pervasive and largely ungoverned, despite restrictions being in place

Schools across Australia and the US have written policies that students are routinely circumventing, and teachers lack the tools or training to enforce them; the response cannot be more rules – it requires a combination of assessment redesign, explicit student AI literacy instruction, and realistic policy calibration.

Sources: Learning First NSW survey, EducationHQ "Get AI out of schools", AFT 10-point plan, Gallup/Walton survey via EdWeek

International governance bodies are coalescing around "human-centred AI" framing that embeds equity and teacher agency as primary obligations

The dominant international policy frame is no longer "how do we accelerate AI adoption" but "how do we ensure AI serves human learning rather than substituting for it"; schools that have framed their AI strategy primarily around adoption and efficiency are out of step with where both the evidence and governance frameworks are heading.

Sources: EU Council conclusions, May 11, APPI participatory governance roadmap, OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026

Where experts disagree

Live debates worth understanding before deciding

Should student access to AI be restricted until durable learning benefits are demonstrated?

One viewThe Learning First report argues that AI access for student schoolwork should be "significantly restricted" until research shows measurable durable learning gains – a precautionary position that treats the absence of evidence of harm as insufficient. (Learning First)
The other viewOthers, including the ISNSW and technology-forward practitioners, argue that restricting access prevents students from building AI literacy skills they will need in work and life, and that a blanket restriction privileges students with home access over those dependent on school access. (ISNSW)
The tensionThe disagreement is partly empirical (does using AI in school harm durable learning?) and partly about values (is developing AI competency itself an educational outcome worth pursuing?); neither side yet has the long-run outcome data required to settle the empirical question, which means the policy choice is currently a values judgement.

Do high AI adoption rates in Australian schools indicate successful integration?

One viewOECD TALIS data shows Australian teachers are among the world's highest frequency AI users – a fact frequently cited as evidence that Australian schools are ahead of the curve on integration. (EducationHQ / OECD)
The other viewThe Learning First data and the OECD Digital Education Outlook's "performance-learning gap" finding both suggest that frequency of AI use does not predict whether learning is being deepened or merely assisted; and EducationHQ's earlier reporting found many Australian teachers are using AI primarily as a workload relief strategy rather than a pedagogical one. (Learning First; OECD DEO 2026)
The tensionSchools with high staff AI adoption rates may be counting the wrong metric; the more useful question is whether AI use is producing better learning conditions, stronger student outcomes, and more sustainable teacher practice – none of which is captured by frequency data alone.

Worth watching

Developments to keep an eye on

AITSL teacher standards review – consultation now open, closes February 2027

The AITSL review of the Australian Professional Standards for Teachers, launched last month, is now in open consultation. Submissions can be made at aitsl.edu.au. This review is the mechanism through which AI competency could be embedded into what Australia formally expects of all registered teachers. The consultation window runs until February 2027, so there is no immediate deadline – but getting in early ensures your input shapes the discussion rather than reacting to a draft. Schools, sector bodies, and individual teachers can all submit. Given the scale of AI adoption documented in this week's Learning First data, a strong case exists for the Standards to explicitly address AI-enabled professional practice.

University admissions bodies beginning to track student AI use patterns – a new source of senior school intelligence

The Universities Admissions Centre's Student Lifestyle and Learning Report 2026 found that AI use among school leavers is uneven, with one in five respondents reporting no AI tool use in the prior month and about a third reporting only occasional use. A separate EducationHQ report noted that Year 12 students appear "cautious but curious" about AI, with boys more confident than girls. As university admissions bodies begin systematically tracking how HSC and ATAR cohorts are engaging with AI, this data will increasingly inform both university readiness programs and secondary school design conversations. Worth monitoring as it matures into a longitudinal dataset.

Sources: UAC, "Student Lifestyle and Learning Report 2025", 2025; EducationHQ, "Year 12s cautious but curious about AI, while boys more confident in schools", 2026.
