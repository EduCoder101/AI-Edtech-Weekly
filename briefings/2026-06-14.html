Queensland puts a state-built AI in students' hands, free national teacher training arrives, and the metacognitive risk evidence hardens

Issue of 14 June 2026

At a glance

Queensland's government-built Corella platform is now in every state high school and open to Years 7 to 10 students with parental consent, the most explicit government endorsement of student-facing AI in Australian schooling so far. Microsoft's free Elevate for Educators programme offers a nationally aligned AI credential for teachers, while new RAND survey data and a peer-reviewed scoping review sharpen the evidence that AI use can improve task output while weakening the thinking behind it.

Key signals

  • Queensland's Corella, a government-owned AI platform hosted on state infrastructure, has rolled out to all state high schools, with Year 7 to 10 student access available under parental consent.
  • Microsoft's Elevate for Educators gives all Australian teachers free AI training and a credential formally aligned with the national AI framework and the professional standards for teachers.
  • Iceland's national AI pilot concluded: teachers saved meaningful planning time, but the union-side review flagged dependency, data sovereignty and language-culture risks for other governments.
  • RAND: 63% of US high schoolers now use AI for homework, up 14 points in seven months, and 67% believe it harms their critical thinking; only one in three reports a school-wide rule.
  • A peer-reviewed scoping review finds offloading tasks to AI fosters 'metacognitive passivity' even where task performance improves, converging with OECD and Stanford findings.

Considerations for the Executive

  • Audit the data governance arrangements of the commercial AI tools in use at the school against the benchmark Queensland has set with a government-hosted platform.
  • Evaluate Microsoft's free AI Literacy for Educators credential as a shared baseline for staff professional learning, while weighing its vendor perspective.
  • Treat metacognitive passivity as a working assumption in task design: any AI-assisted activity should preserve the cognitive effort that produces the learning.

Queensland's Corella AI platform now available across all state high schools and to Year 7-10 students

Queensland Department of Education / The Indian Sun / EducationDaily | 23 May 2026

Queensland's government-owned AI platform, Corella, has rolled out to all state high schools, with access available to teachers, teacher aides, and school leaders – and, with parental consent, to students in Years 7 to 10. The platform is hosted within secure Queensland Government infrastructure and was purpose-built for classroom use rather than adapted from a commercial general-purpose tool. Teachers can use it to draft lesson materials, streamline administrative tasks, and develop curriculum resources. Students can brainstorm, draft, research, summarise, and revise. The rollout follows trials at selected schools, including Woree State High School, where teachers reported reduced time on administration and students used the platform in digital technologies classes for coding and game development projects. The government frames the rollout as part of a commitment to cut teacher red tape by 25 per cent over four years.

Sources: The Indian Sun, "Queensland schools to expand use of AI platform Corella", 23 May 2026; EducationDaily, "Queensland Government to Roll Out AI Teaching Tool Corella to All State High Schools", 2026; OpenGov Asia, "Queensland Expands AI Learning Platform Across State Schools", 2026; Queensland Government, "Ministerial statement: Award-winning education program rolls out across Queensland state schools", 2026.

Why it mattersCorella is now the most significant state-level AI deployment in Australian schools, and its design choices carry a policy signal. By building a government-owned tool rather than endorsing commercial platforms, Queensland is making an implicit argument about data sovereignty, student safety, and the limits of relying on tools built for general consumer markets. The decision to extend access to Years 7-10 students – with parental consent – is the most explicit government-level endorsement of student-facing AI in Australian schooling so far. Independent schools watching this development should be asking two questions: what equivalent data governance arrangements apply to the commercial AI tools their own students are already using, and whether a school or consortium-built environment would better meet their needs than individual tool sign-ups.

Microsoft Elevate for Educators: free AI training and a globally recognised credential now available to all Australian teachers

Microsoft / The Educator K-12 | 23 April 2026

Microsoft announced the Elevate for Educators program in Sydney on 23 April, alongside Prime Minister Anthony Albanese, as part of a broader A$25 billion Australian investment. The free program gives teachers and school leaders access to the AI Literacy for Educators credential – described as a globally recognised standard for understanding, applying, and leading responsible AI in schools – alongside community connection, practical training, implementation guidance, and classroom-ready resources. The program is aligned with the national AI in Schools Framework and the Australian Professional Standards for Teachers. The Educator K-12 reported that the initiative is specifically designed to move Australian schools "from experimentation to everyday use" and to build the practical AI capability needed to improve outcomes over time.

Sources: Microsoft, "Microsoft announces Australia's largest AI skilling commitment: three million people by 2028", 23 April 2026; The Educator K-12, "Inside Microsoft's big AI play for Australian classrooms", 2026; Microsoft, "Elevate for Educators", 2026.

Why it mattersThis is the most substantial free, nationally accessible AI professional learning resource currently available to Australian educators, and it has the unusual advantage of formal alignment with national standards. For an independent school weighing how to build staff AI capability – whether through internal programmes, external providers, or platform subscriptions – this is worth evaluating as a baseline layer. The AI Literacy for Educators credential could provide a shared vocabulary and common floor for staff capacity before more specialised school-specific training is layered on top. The caveat: as a vendor-provided programme, it will reflect Microsoft's own AI ecosystem; schools should evaluate the independence of its content accordingly.

Iceland-Anthropic national AI education pilot concludes: teachers save time, but cultural and dependency risks flag for other governments

Anthropic / European Trade Union Committee for Education / EdTech Innovation Hub | Concluded April 2026

One of the world's first government-level AI education pilots has concluded in Iceland. Running from October 2025 to April 2026, the initiative gave roughly 260 upper secondary and vocational teachers access to Claude.edu (Anthropic) and around 300 primary and lower secondary teachers access to Google Gemini, managed by Iceland's Educational and School Services Centre (MMS) in collaboration with the Icelandic Teachers' Union. The explicit focus was on teacher-facing use – lesson planning, differentiation, and feedback – rather than direct student instruction. Teachers who used Claude.edu reported meaningful time savings on planning and more tailored support for individual learners. However, the European Trade Union Committee for Education, in its analysis of the pilot, raised three concerns: teacher dependency on AI tools displacing professional judgement; data sovereignty questions arising from student and teacher data flowing to US-based providers; and the risk that AI tools trained on English-dominant data would underperform for Icelandic language contexts. Iceland's government is now planning its next stage on the basis of these findings.

Sources: Anthropic, "Anthropic and Iceland announce one of the world's first national AI education pilots", 2025; EdTech Innovation Hub, "Anthropic announces partnership with Iceland's Ministry of Education", 2026; European Trade Union Committee for Education, "Iceland's AI Pilot in Education – What it really means for teachers?", 2026; Iceland Directorate of Education and School Services, "AI Pilot Project", 2026.

Why it mattersIceland's pilot is the closest available model of what a deliberate, government-managed AI integration programme looks like at national scale. Several features are directly relevant to the Australian context. The teacher-first, student-later approach – using AI to improve what teachers prepare rather than what students submit – sidesteps the immediate assessment integrity problem while still building capacity. The cultural and linguistic risk finding has direct Australian resonance: schools serving Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students, or students from non-English speaking backgrounds, need to scrutinise whether AI tools trained overwhelmingly on English-language content will serve these students well or poorly. The data sovereignty concern is a live issue for any Australian school using US-hosted AI platforms.

RAND: 63% of high schoolers now use AI for homework, and two-thirds believe it is harming their critical thinking

RAND Corporation / EdWeek / K-12 Dive | March 2026

A RAND Corporation survey of more than 1,200 US students aged 12 to 29, conducted in December 2025, found that 63 per cent of high schoolers used AI for homework – up from 49 per cent in May 2025, a 14 percentage-point increase in seven months. Middle school use rose from 30 per cent to 46 per cent over the same period. Students most commonly used AI to get better explanations of assignments (38 per cent), brainstorm ideas (35 per cent), look up facts (33 per cent), and draft or revise writing (33 per cent). Crucially, 67 per cent of student respondents said that using AI for schoolwork harmed critical thinking – up from 54 per cent earlier in the year. Only one in three students reported that their school had a school-wide rule about AI use. RAND's Heather Schwartz noted that students are "clearly embracing AI as a learning tool" while simultaneously being "conflicted about what it means for their own learning."

Sources: RAND, "More Students Use AI for Homework, and More Believe It Harms Critical Thinking: Selected Findings from the American Youth Panel", March 2026; EdWeek, "Students Are Worried That AI Will Hurt Their Critical Thinking Skills", March 2026; K-12 Dive, "More middle and high schoolers are leaning on AI for homework", 2026.

Why it mattersThis is the most detailed longitudinal picture of student AI behaviour currently available, and while the sample is American, the usage trajectory almost certainly describes Australian secondary students' behaviour at a comparable pace – a finding consistent with the Learning First NSW data reported last week. Three points are worth flagging for school leaders. First, the rate of change: 14 percentage points in seven months is not a gradual drift but a rapid normalisation, and any school that last surveyed student AI use more than six months ago is working from outdated data. Second, the critical thinking self-concern: students are not passive or uncritical users – they are themselves worried about what AI use is doing to their thinking, which creates an opening for genuine classroom conversations about metacognitive habits rather than adversarial policing. Third, the policy gap: if only one in three US students reports a school-wide rule, and the Learning First data shows half of NSW secondary teachers do not know how to prevent AI-related plagiarism, the shared problem is governance, not intent.

Peer-reviewed scoping review: GenAI in K-12 risks "metacognitive passivity" even when task performance improves

Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence (ScienceDirect) | 25 February 2026

A peer-reviewed scoping review published in Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence has systematically examined the literature on potential risks of generative AI integration in K-12 education. Its central finding: offloading cognitive tasks to AI chatbots fosters metacognitive passivity and disengagement, potentially impeding long-term skill acquisition – even when immediate task performance appears to improve. The review draws on studies published through January 2026 and follows PRISMA-ScR systematic review methodology. It also documents risks relating to equity (uneven access to AI tools widening existing achievement gaps), privacy (inadequate data protections in many commercial EdTech AI products), and accuracy (students who rely on AI outputs without critical evaluation may internalise factual errors or biased perspectives).

Sources: ScienceDirect / Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence, "Potential risks of generative artificial intelligence integration into K-12 education: A scoping review", 25 February 2026.

Why it mattersThis review is significant not primarily for its individual findings – which broadly align with what the OECD and Stanford SCALE have already reported – but for what they collectively signal. Three methodologically distinct research traditions now converge on the same mechanism: the OECD's multi-country synthesis calls it "metacognitive laziness"; Stanford's causal review finds AI performance gains that disappear without AI support; and this scoping review identifies "metacognitive passivity." When independent teams using different methods arrive at the same finding, the confidence level rises substantially. For a primary teacher designing a unit where students will use AI tools, this is the most important disciplining question: does my task design require students to think, or does it allow them to hand thinking to the AI? If the latter, the activity may produce output but not learning.

Where the evidence agrees

Independent sources pointing the same way

Students are rapidly normalising AI homework use while simultaneously doubting its effect on their learning

Students are not naive about the risks – they are worried and using AI anyway; this opens a more productive entry point for school conversation than detection and punishment, focused on helping students understand when AI use serves their learning and when it substitutes for it.

Sources: RAND American Youth Panel (March 2026), Learning First NSW survey (May 2026)

Three independent research traditions converge on metacognitive risk as AI's core learning threat

The metacognitive passivity finding is now robust enough to treat as a working assumption for curriculum design: any AI-assisted task that does not actively preserve the cognitive effort of learning is unlikely to produce durable learning gains, regardless of output quality.

Sources: ScienceDirect scoping review (Feb 2026), OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026, Stanford SCALE K-12 evidence review (2026)

Government-provided AI tools for schools are moving from pilot to deployment

State and national governments are no longer just publishing frameworks – they are deploying or endorsing specific tools; independent schools that have been waiting for a settled landscape before committing to AI infrastructure are running out of runway for deferral.

Sources: Queensland Corella rollout (May 2026), Iceland-Anthropic pilot concludes (April 2026), Microsoft Elevate for Educators Australia (April 2026)

Where experts disagree

Live debates worth understanding before deciding

Should student-facing AI tools be government-built or commercially sourced?

One viewQueensland's Corella model: government-owned, purpose-built within secure state infrastructure, extended to Year 7-10 students with consent. (QLD Dept of Education)
The other viewMost independent schools are currently using or evaluating commercial tools (Microsoft Copilot, Google Gemini, sector-specific EdTech products) built by global vendors and hosted offshore. (ISNSW)
The tensionThe data sovereignty and privacy trade-offs between government-built (more control, less capability) and commercial tools (more capability, less control) are not resolved; Queensland's choice to build its own is a legitimate policy stance, not the only one, but it raises questions independent schools have not yet formally answered about where their students' data goes.

Should AI pilots prioritise teacher use or student use?

One viewIceland's national pilot was explicitly teacher-facing: AI for lesson planning, differentiation, and feedback, deliberately kept away from direct student instruction. (Anthropic / Iceland MMS)
The other viewQueensland's Corella rollout extends directly to Year 7-10 students (with consent), framing student AI use as a digital literacy and productivity development opportunity. (QLD Government)
The tensionThese represent genuinely different policy judgements about readiness and risk: teacher-first protects against integrity and dependency risks but delays student AI literacy; student-facing accelerates literacy but increases exposure to the metacognitive passivity risks documented in the current research literature.

Worth watching

Developments to keep an eye on

Ohio's July 1 AI policy deadline: first legally mandated district AI policies in the US – will Australian states follow?

Ohio has set 1 July 2026 as the deadline for every public school district in the state to adopt a formal AI use policy covering student use, staff use, privacy, ethical use, vendor agreements, third-party tools, and student assessments. This makes Ohio the first US state to legally mandate AI governance at district level. Nearly 100 US state bills on K-12 AI are currently tracked by the PIE Network. The question for Australian education ministers and curriculum authorities is whether legislated AI governance – rather than voluntary frameworks and guidelines – is the next step. The national AI in Schools Framework is non-binding. Watch whether any Australian state moves from guidance to mandate in the second half of 2026, particularly given the Learning First data showing half of NSW secondary teachers cannot prevent AI-related academic integrity breaches.

Sources: ExcelInEd, "K-12 AI Policy in Education (2026 Trends)", May 2026; FutureEd, "Legislative Tracker: 2026 State AI in Education Bills", 2026.

eSafety Commissioner webinar on AI-facilitated harm: 25 June 2026 (11 days away)

The eSafety Commissioner is hosting a free professional learning webinar on 25 June 2026 (3:45 pm AEST) for school educators covering AI companion chatbots, sexual and non-sexual deepfakes, AI-facilitated cyberbullying, and how these technologies affect young people's safety, privacy, and development. Given that reports of deepfake incidents involving students under 18 have doubled in the past 18 months, this is a directly relevant event for wellbeing, pastoral care, and safeguarding staff.
