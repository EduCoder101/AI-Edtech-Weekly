# AI in EdTech Weekly -- 18 October 2026

*A research briefing for the AI Integration Lead, Sydney independent K-12 school. Covering 12--18 October 2026.*

## Top Stories

### 1. AERO warns of learning risk as it issues its first AI-in-schools guidance

**ABC News / EducationHQ / The Educator K-12 | 14 October 2026**

Australia's national education evidence body, the Australian Education Research Organisation (AERO), released its first substantive guidance on AI in schools on 14 October, urging leaders and teachers to judge AI not by how efficiently it produces outputs but by whether it is building student learning. AERO CEO Jenny Donovan named the core risk as students reaching for AI as a crutch: they may score well on a task completed with AI, then be unable to replicate that performance without it. AERO notes that most research into AI in schools is short-term, conducted overseas, focused on engagement gains rather than learning, and almost never based in primary settings. The guidance identifies five areas for school consideration: evidence-based teaching, monitoring learning risks, setting clear expectations on AI use, rethinking assessment and feedback, and applying a culturally responsive lens that respects First Nations knowledges.

**Why it matters for K-12:** AERO is the authoritative voice on Australian education evidence, and it is saying clearly that the evidentiary base for classroom AI is thin, that primary schools have almost no local research to draw on, and that gains in task performance do not reliably transfer to gains in actual learning. That is the intellectual backdrop to every AI procurement and classroom policy decision right now. The five considerations are also a usable self-assessment checklist for a school AI lead reviewing its own position: not a ban, but a structured prompt to test whether current practice is benefiting students or substituting for them.

### 2. Federal AI inquiry turns contentious; education among final-report priorities

**ABC News | 13 October 2026**

The Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence, established on 20 August 2026 and due to report by 30 November, has begun releasing interim findings. An ABC News report on 13 October says the committee has recommended that AI companies be required to submit to independent safety checks before deployment, that computing capacity be reserved for Australian use, and that security agencies receive early access to new models. Independent senator David Pocock raised concerns that the major parties were shaping the inquiry to avoid the independent regulatory body he and others have proposed. OpenAI's representatives appeared before the committee on 6 October; CEO Sam Altman and Anthropic's Dario Amodei declined to attend, according to The Next Web.

**Why it matters for K-12:** Education is within the committee's terms of reference as an adequacy-of-the-education-system question, meaning the November report may carry national recommendations affecting how schools prepare students for an AI-affected workforce. The Pocock controversy is also relevant beyond politics: it reflects a genuine unresolved argument about whether AI in Australian public life needs an independent standing regulator, rather than industry self-governance and episodic parliamentary inquiries. A future regulatory body with education in scope would look very different from the current patchwork of state guidance and school-level policy.

### 3. South Australia's Royal Commission into AI opens, with schools in its remit

**Capital Brief / Australian Cyber Security Magazine | commencing 1 October 2026**

South Australia's Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence, chaired by former Federal Court judge and economist Dr Iain Ross AO, with commissioners Kate Pounder and Professor Christopher Manning, formally opened on 1 October 2026. Its $3 million budget and broad terms of reference include impacts and opportunities for education at school, tertiary, and vocational levels. Premier Malinauskas has publicly framed schools as one motivation for the inquiry. The commission will report to government no later than 1 July 2027.

**Why it matters for K-12:** Australia now has a federal parliamentary inquiry and a state-level royal commission both actively examining AI in education simultaneously, with the federal body reporting in November and the SA commission in mid-2027. Independent schools in South Australia are in direct scope; independent schools in other states should track both, since royal commission findings frequently inform national policy conversations and ministerial working groups. The concurrent timing, with NESA's assessment restrictions already in place and AERO's new guidance just published, means a significant body of Australian-sourced evidence and recommendation will accumulate over the next eight to twelve months.

### 4. NESA's cap on take-home tasks bites at the start of Term 4

**NSW Government / The Educator K-12 | effective Term 4 2026**

NESA's changes to the ACE Rules, published under reference NESA 34/26 on 16 September, came into practical effect at the start of Term 4. From this term, NSW Preliminary and HSC courses are limited to one take-home assessment task per year, worth no more than 15 per cent of the school-based assessment mark (7.5 per cent of the total HSC mark). The stated driver is AI: the Minns Government commissioned the review in August and the minister asked NESA to consider whether unsupervised home tasks should continue at all while the broader integrity review is under way. NESA has indicated that subject-specific teacher guidance and changes for the 2028 HSC are in development. Major projects and creative works are expected to be treated differently.

**Why it matters for K-12:** While the rule is binding only on NSW public and registered non-government schools, independent schools in NSW need to confirm with their system or NESA whether they are subject to the cap and on what timeline. For schools already using rubrics and task designs that assumed take-home work, this is an immediate curriculum-planning matter, not a future consideration. The 2028 HSC horizon also signals that more significant assessment redesign is coming, and subject faculties that wait until the guidance is published will be at a disadvantage.

### 5. UNESCO calls on vendors to prove educational value before AI enters classrooms

**Dev Discourse / UNESCO | September 2026**

UNESCO's ministerial statement from its Digital Learning Week in Paris (8 September 2026) argued that AI's role in education should be decided through public deliberation rather than by vendors, and specifically called on AI providers to present evidence of educational benefits before implementation, alongside auditing and accountability mechanisms. A UNESCO survey of UNESCO Chairs found 93 per cent of academics believe students are using generative AI to produce assignments, while 63 per cent perceive a decline in students' cognitive abilities. Most respondents favoured regulated use with disclosure over either a ban or open access.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The evidence-before-implementation call aligns directly with AERO's new Australian guidance and adds international institutional weight to it. For a school AI lead, this framing provides a useful lever in procurement conversations: the question is not whether the vendor's tool is impressive but whether it can demonstrate learning gains under controlled conditions. The 63 per cent cognitive-decline perception from academics also matters as a data point, even though it is a perception survey rather than measured outcomes research; it signals where the international expert community's instincts currently sit.

Sources:
- [UNESCO Calls for Human-Centred Rules as AI Reshapes Education](https://www.devdiscourse.com/article/education/3975197-unesco-calls-for-human-centred-rules-as-ai-reshapes-education) -- Dev Discourse, covering UNESCO Digital Learning Week, September 2026

## Worth Watching

**1. AITSL Teacher Standards review enters consultation phase in October 2026**

AITSL is currently reviewing the Australian Professional Standards for Teachers (May 2026 -- February 2027), with a second consultation round due this month. The existing standards predate generative AI by fifteen years. A commentary piece from Australian researchers argues that Standard 1 (knowing students) matters more, not less, in a world where AI is personalising and tracking learning, and that the review is an opportunity to test whether the standards prepare teachers to support human flourishing in an AI-affected classroom. There is no confirmation yet that AI will appear as an explicit standard or descriptor; that is precisely what is worth watching. If the revised standards name AI competencies directly, professional learning requirements for teachers across Australia will shift accordingly.

Source: [Review of the Teacher Standards](https://www.aitsl.edu.au/standards/review-of-the-teacher-standards) -- AITSL, 2026; [A profile is not a relationship: these standards must support human flourishing](https://blog.aare.edu.au/?p=30823) -- AARE Blog, August 2026.

**2. The AI tutoring evidence base is accumulating, but the effect sizes are small and study durations short**

A 2026 systematic review of 28 studies (4,597 students) on intelligent tutoring systems found generally positive outcomes but found those effects were mitigated when compared to non-intelligent tutoring systems. A separate arXiv preprint reports a 0.15 standard deviation gain from personalised problem sequencing over fixed sequencing, with no added instruction time. These are meaningful signals, but most studies last fewer than eight weeks, and the IES cautions that effects measured on AI-assisted tasks often do not hold when students are tested without the tool. A large cluster-randomised trial across 18 US middle schools using a major AI tutoring platform is reportedly two years in; its findings, when published, will be the most credible evidence yet. Schools considering AI tutoring procurement should hold out for that data before committing.

Sources: [A systematic review of AI-driven intelligent tutoring systems (ITS) in K-12 education](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12078640/) -- NCBI / PMC, 2026; [AI in K-12 Education: The Good, the Bad, and the Guardrails to Consider](https://ies.ed.gov/learn/blog/ai-k-12-education-good-bad-and-guardrails-consider) -- IES / EDC, 2026.
