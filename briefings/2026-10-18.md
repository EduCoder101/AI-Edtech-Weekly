# AI in EdTech Weekly -- 18 October 2026

*A research briefing for the AI Integration Lead, Sydney independent K-12 school. Covering 12--18 October 2026.*

---

## Top Stories

### 1. AERO's new guidance: use AI sparingly, avoid it entirely in the first years of primary

**AERO / EducationHQ / ABC News | 14 October 2026**

The Australian Education Research Organisation released two explainers on 14 October, one for school leaders and teachers and one for policymakers, concluding that there is limited robust research examining AI and student learning outcomes in school settings, with most studies short-term, conducted overseas and focused on engagement or perceptions rather than learning. AERO warns of a "performance paradox" in which AI-generated work creates what CEO Jenny Donovan called an illusion of learning, citing studies in which students who used AI could not reproduce their results without it. Donovan told the ABC that AI should be avoided completely in the first three years of primary school and used carefully thereafter, and the guidance sets out five considerations: evidence-based teaching, monitoring for risks, clear expectations, assessment design and a culturally responsive approach.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This is the closest thing Australia now has to an official evidence position on classroom AI, from the body whose explainers principals and boards actually read, and it lands squarely on the side of restraint: AI should scaffold the acquisition, retrieval, practice and application of knowledge, never replace the thinking itself. For a school with a primary years AI programme, the explicit "not in the first three years" line is the sharpest age guidance any Australian authority has given and will need a considered response, since parents and staff will quote it. It is also useful ammunition: any vendor claim about learning gains can now be met with AERO's finding that few studies even measure learning, and fewer still in primary settings.

### 2. Federal AI inquiry asks the Prime Minister for a national approach to AI literacy, amid an independence row

**Parliament of Australia / The Canberra Times | 12--13 October 2026**

The Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence sent an interim letter to the Prime Minister, dated 12 October and released publicly on 13 October, ahead of its full report due 30 November. Among its recommendations: a national approach to AI literacy, technical skills and workforce readiness spanning education, training and workforce transition; consideration of changes to copyright law over creators' work being used without adequate control or payment; and ensuring critical AI infrastructure is covered by the Security of Critical Infrastructure framework. The release was overshadowed by independent senator David Pocock's accusation that a senior adviser in the Prime Minister's office vetted the draft recommendations before they were sent, which committee chair Jo Briskey denied. The committee held further hearings in Canberra on 14 and 16 October, with a Sydney hearing scheduled for 20 October.

**Why it matters for K-12:** "National approach to AI literacy" is the phrase to watch, because if the government takes it up, it becomes the hook on which curriculum bodies, AITSL standards and funding programmes get hung, much as digital literacy frameworks followed earlier national statements. The committee's framing treats AI literacy as a workforce and community capability question, not just a classroom one, which strengthens the case the reader has been making for whole-staff professional learning rather than opt-in enthusiast sessions. The vetting controversy matters too: it signals the final report in November will be read through a political lens, so schools should anchor their own positions in AERO's evidence rather than waiting for the committee's conclusions.

Sources: [Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence interim letter to the Prime Minister](https://www.aph.gov.au/DocumentStore.ashx?id=624cc99a-6e83-4e7a-aabb-15828aeac37d) -- Parliament of Australia, 12 October 2026; [Anthony Albanese's staffers accused of interfering with committee](https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/9367508/anthony-albaneses-staffers-accused-of-interfering-with-committee/) -- The Canberra Times, October 2026; [Public hearings, Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Public_Hearings) -- Parliament of Australia.

### 3. Australian psychologists publish practical guidance as children take their problems to chatbots

**The Conversation | 16 October 2026**

Following last week's Adelaide University findings on children disclosing victimisation to chatbots, clinical psychology researchers writing in The Conversation set out what is known about children's advice-seeking from AI and what parents and schools can actually do. Roughly half of Australian children aged 10 to 17 who use AI use it for personal or social purposes, including advice on sensitive topics and talking about their feelings, and international studies report that a majority of teens have tried AI companions, with most who seek mental health advice from a chatbot telling no one. The piece recommends open, non-judgemental conversations about chatbot use, treating AI tools with the same boundaries as other screens, watching for withdrawal from human relationships, and teaching children that stored conversations are not private. The eSafety Commissioner is meanwhile recruiting parents of children aged 9 to 12 for research on how children use AI chatbots.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The advice translates directly into parent communication, which is the gap most schools have not yet filled: AI guidance has gone to staff and students, but parents are the ones at home when a Year 5 student asks a chatbot about friendship problems. A school newsletter piece or parent evening built on this guidance, open questions rather than bans, chatbots are not private, agreement is not care, would be timely while the Adelaide findings are still in the news. It also reinforces the case for the wellbeing team owning part of the AI brief, since the behaviours described sit well below the secondary years where most AI policy attention goes.

Sources: [Kids and teens are going to AI chatbots for advice. How can parents make sure this is safe?](https://theconversation.com/kids-and-teens-are-going-to-ai-chatbots-for-advice-how-can-parents-make-sure-this-is-safe-294145) -- The Conversation, 16 October 2026; [eSafety: AI companions information sheet for educators](https://www.esafety.gov.au/educators/training-for-professionals/professional-learning-program-teachers/ai-companions-information-sheet) -- eSafety Commissioner.

### 4. Eight US states sue OpenAI over ChatGPT's harms to children

**EducationHQ / Al Jazeera | 14--15 October 2026**

Attorneys-general from eight US states, Arizona, Arkansas, Idaho, Iowa, Kansas, Montana, Nebraska and West Virginia, filed coordinated lawsuits against OpenAI on 14 October, the most significant state action against an AI company to date. The suits rest on consumer-protection law, alleging OpenAI marketed ChatGPT as safe for teenagers while deliberately designing it to be agreeable and emotionally engaging, and cite alleged harms including encouragement of self-harm, dangerous medical advice and assistance with violence. The states seek civil penalties, court-ordered product changes and surrender of profits earned from young users; a separate federal class action over the collection of children's data was filed a day earlier. OpenAI said it has industry-leading protections, and no court has yet ruled.

**Why it matters for K-12:** Courts and consumer-protection regulators, not education departments, are now setting the pace on AI child safety, and the legal theory matters for schools: the claim is that sycophantic, engagement-maximising design is itself the deceptive practice, not merely a side effect. That is the same design property Australian researchers flagged with the Spot the Sycophant tool and that AERO's guidance warns about, and it will shape what vendors are willing to put in front of minors. For a school, the practical reading is that any student-facing tool's engagement-boosting features, personas, memory, flattery, now carry documented legal and safety concerns worth raising in procurement, and that Australia's own Digital Duty of Care Bill would push in the same direction with penalties attached.

Sources: [Eight US states sue OpenAI over ChatGPT harm to young](https://educationhq.com/news/eight-us-states-sue-openai-over-chatgpt-harm-to-young-216073/) -- EducationHQ, 15 October 2026; [Why are eight US states suing OpenAI?](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/15/why-are-eight-us-states-suing-openai) -- Al Jazeera, 15 October 2026; [USA: Eight states sue OpenAI over ChatGPT's alleged harms to children](https://www.business-humanrights.org/en/latest-news/usa-eight-states-sue-openai-alleging-chatgpt-deceives-parents-and-harms-children/) -- Business and Human Rights Resource Centre, October 2026.

### 5. US coalition: only 20 of 800 studies on classroom AI produce strong causal evidence, and governments should fund better research

**Education Week | 15 October 2026**

The Alliance for Learning Innovation, a coalition of education nonprofits, researchers and funders, published a report arguing the US federal government should fund research into the safe and effective design and use of AI in classrooms, so that states and districts stop buying on faith. The report leans on Stanford SCALE's 2026 review, which analysed more than 800 academic papers on AI in K-12 education and found only 20 produced strong causal evidence, and it lands in a policy vacuum: the US Education Department's Office of Educational Technology was shut down last year and federal guidance on AI purchasing remains thin.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The 20-of-800 figure is the single most quotable statistic of the week and says internationally what AERO said domestically the day before: schools everywhere are buying and deploying classroom AI on an evidence base that rounds to zero. For the reader's purposes it is a governance asset, a one-line answer to "everyone else is adopting this" in any leadership or board conversation, and a reason to make small-scale internal evaluation (baseline, intervention, check for transfer without the tool) part of any AI pilot the school runs, since nobody else is producing the evidence either.

Sources: [We Need Research on AI in Schools. The Feds Should Fund It, Report Says](https://www.edweek.org/technology/we-need-research-on-ai-in-schools-the-feds-should-fund-it-report-says/2026/10) -- Education Week, 15 October 2026; [The Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A 2026 Review](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20Report.pdf) -- Stanford SCALE Initiative, 2026.

## Worth Watching

**1. The federal AI inquiry's Sydney hearing on 20 October and its 30 November report**

The Joint Select Committee sits in Sydney on 20 October and reports by 30 November. After this week's interim letter, the education questions are whether "a national approach to AI literacy" survives into the final recommendations with any funding or curriculum machinery attached, and whether the Pocock vetting row weakens the report's authority. Either way, it is the most likely vehicle for the first federal statement on AI capability that names schools directly since the national framework, and worth reading on release.

Source: [Public hearings, Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence](https://www.aph.gov.au/Parliamentary_Business/Committees/Joint/Artificial_Intelligence/ArtificialIntelligence/Public_Hearings) -- Parliament of Australia.

**2. Anti-Poverty Week data puts a number on the equity cost of AI rollouts**

Timed to Anti-Poverty Week (12 to 18 October), The Smith Family reported that 44 per cent of students on its Learning for Life programme lack a connected laptop or tablet at home, with national data showing the share of young people without a device rising, not falling. As schools and states roll out generative AI tools that assume home access, the digital divide quietly becomes an AI divide. Early-stage as a policy story, but it is the equity question every school AI strategy will eventually be asked, including by independent school scholarship families.

Source: [Digital gap widens as home device access fails disadvantaged students](https://www.theeducatoronline.com/k12/news/digital-gap-widens-as-home-device-access-fails-disadvantaged-students/289257) -- The Educator K-12, October 2026.
