# AI in EdTech Weekly -- 23 August 2026

*A research briefing for the AI Integration Lead, Sydney independent K-12 school. Covering 17 August -- 23 August 2026.*

## Top Stories

### 1. AI is splitting Australia's education system along pedagogical lines

**ABC News | 17 August 2026**

The week after NSW and SA made their landmark announcements, ABC News published a feature examining how generative AI is dividing Australian schools not just by resources but by values. While state governments in NSW, Queensland and South Australia have built or commissioned government AI platforms for students (EduChat, Corella, and EdChat respectively), the head of Steiner Education Australia has ruled out creating any equivalent tool for its schools and teachers. CEO Andrew Hill said the decision reflects the sector's view that anything capable of "compromising healthy brain growth" warrants caution, and that deep engagement with ideas -- not outsourcing to AI -- is central to the Steiner pedagogical model. The piece also quoted early research cautioning against over-reliance on AI reducing independent problem-solving and critical thinking skills.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The Steiner position is the sharpest articulation yet of a case parents and board members may bring to independent schools: what is AI doing to how students learn to think, not just what they produce? This is a different question from academic integrity and deserves a different answer. An independent school cannot assume its community shares the government school rationale for adopting AI tools, and it may need to explain its own position in terms of its educational philosophy, not just its IT policy. Worth having a board-ready statement that distinguishes "tool we use for teacher productivity" from "tool students use to complete work."

Sources: [How AI is splitting Australia's education system](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-08-17/qld-alternative-schools-not-rushing-to-bring-ai-into-classrooms/107016124) -- ABC News, 17 August 2026; [Alternative schools weigh AI's role in learning](https://ozarab.media/alternative-schools-weigh-ais-role-in-learning/) -- Oz Arab Media, August 2026.

### 2. eSafety Commissioner: school photos are being harvested and weaponised by AI

**eSafety Commissioner and Human Rights Watch | advisory c. 28 July 2026; HRW report 30 July 2026**

Australia's eSafety Commissioner issued an advisory urging schools to review how they share imagery online, following a spike in reports of AI-generated abuse targeting school communities. Between January and March 2026, eSafety received more than 100 reports of anonymous accounts using photos harvested from school websites and social media accounts to produce AI-generated deepfakes, face-swaps, sexualised imagery of students and staff, and fabricated stories. The content was shared predominantly on TikTok and Instagram. A complementary Human Rights Watch report, published 30 July, documented how Australian school photos are being scraped into AI training datasets without consent, compounding the problem beyond targeted harassment to systemic data exploitation. Independent Schools NSW flagged the eSafety advisory as a key 2026 update for member schools this week.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This has moved from theoretical risk to a documented, recurring threat in Australian schools. The practical actions are clear and immediate: audit what imagery your school publishes online (website, social media, newsletters), ensure consent and privacy-settings policies cover AI misuse risk, and have a response plan for when a deepfake report arrives, because the eSafety data suggests one eventually will. The eSafety Commissioner has published a new Online Safety Advisory and a deepfake-specific blog post that are ready to attach to a parent or staff communication.

Sources: [School photo posts vulnerable to emerging AI threat](https://www.esafety.gov.au/newsroom/media-releases/school-photo-posts-vulnerable-to-emerging-ai-threat) -- eSafety Commissioner, c. 28 July 2026; [Sharing school imagery in the age of AI: risks, deepfakes, and how to protect students and staff](https://www.esafety.gov.au/newsroom/blogs/sharing-school-imagery-in-the-age-of-ai-risks-deepfakes-and-how-to-protect-students-and-staff) -- eSafety Commissioner; [Deepfake damage in schools](https://www.esafety.gov.au/newsroom/blogs/deepfake-damage-in-schools-how-ai-generated-abuse-is-disrupting-students-families-and-school-communities) -- eSafety Commissioner; [Australia School Photos Scraped for AI-Fueled Image Misuse](https://www.hrw.org/news/2026/07/30/australia-school-photos-scraped-for-ai-fueled-image-misuse) -- Human Rights Watch, 30 July 2026; [Key 2026 updates for schools from the eSafety Commissioner](https://www.isnsw.edu.au/newsroom/key-2026-updates-for-schools-from-esafety-commissioner) -- Independent Schools NSW.

### 3. WA expands ClassmAIte to more than 100 schools as Australian teacher AI adoption ranks fourth globally

**WA Government / OECD TALIS | expansion under 2026-27 State Budget**

The Western Australian Government's $4.6 million investment in the ClassmAIte AI platform -- announced in the May 2026 State Budget -- is now actively expanding, with the pilot due to grow from 60 schools to more than 100 by the end of 2026. ClassmAIte is designed for teachers rather than students: it generates lesson sequences, lesson plans, classroom resources and assessment items, and includes a secure chat function with editable outputs. The expansion sits within a broader federal $30 million Workload Reduction Fund supporting 27 AI pilots across Australian state systems, including expanding NSW's EduChat to staff in around 700 schools and a generative-AI marking and feedback pilot in independent schools. Context from the OECD's Teaching and Learning International Survey (TALIS), released in late 2025: approximately two-thirds of Australian lower-secondary teachers reported using AI in their work in 2024, the fourth highest proportion among OECD's 38 member countries and well above the 36 per cent global average.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The workload angle is now the strongest argument for AI adoption in Australian schools, and the federal fund means government is actively backing it. For an independent school the practical question is whether to use a government platform (EduChat or equivalent) or an equivalent commercial tool for teacher productivity -- the evaluation criteria should include data sovereignty (government tools store data in Australia), curriculum alignment, and the distinction between AI that helps teachers prepare better lessons versus AI that generates work students submit. TALIS data also suggest Australian teachers are more exposed to AI's effects -- positive and negative -- than their international peers, which raises the stakes for professional learning.

Sources: [$4.6 million boost extends AI pilot to reduce teacher workload](https://educationmattersmag.com.au/4-6-million-boost-extends-ai-pilot-to-reduce-teacher-workload/) -- Education Matters Magazine; [$4.6 million for AI platform designed to reduce teacher's workloads](https://www.wa.gov.au/government/media-statements/Cook%20Labor%20Government/$4.6-million-for-AI-platform-designed-to-reduce-teacher's-workloads-20260509) -- WA Government, May 2026; [AI pilot to help tackle WA teacher workloads](https://ministers.education.gov.au/clare/ai-pilot-help-tackle-wa-teacher-workloads/) -- Ministers' Media Centre; [High-Quality Professional Learning for Australian Teachers and School Leaders](https://www.aitsl.edu.au/research/spotlights/high-quality-professional-learning-for-australian-teachers-and-school-leaders) -- AITSL.

### 4. OECD: AI can hollow out learning unless teaching principles come first

**OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026 | widely discussed this week**

The OECD's Digital Education Outlook 2026 (released January, now widely cited in the context of Australia's policy debates) has gained renewed attention this week and deserves a standalone entry. The headline finding, now known informally as the "crutch effect": students who practised maths with a generic AI chatbot performed 48 per cent better on practice tasks but scored 17 per cent worse on closed-book exams than peers who studied without AI. The mechanism is cognitive offloading -- students skip the productive struggle (diagnosis, testing reasoning, revising) that builds durable understanding, and develop an illusion of competence instead. Critically, the report found this effect is not inevitable: students using a well-designed AI tutor with proper pedagogical safeguards showed no exam performance decline despite significant practice gains. The report also found 37 per cent of lower-secondary teachers globally used AI in their work in 2024, and 72 per cent of lower-secondary teachers believe AI harms academic integrity by letting students pass off work as their own.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The crutch effect is the international evidence base behind what NSW is proposing domestically. For a school thinking through AI tool selection, the OECD finding is the clearest available guide: general-purpose chatbots (ChatGPT, Gemini without modification) are the highest-risk category; AI tutors that scaffold reasoning without supplying answers are where positive evidence concentrates. This distinction should sit at the centre of any staff professional learning on AI tool choice, and it is a ready-made answer to the "surely students just need to learn how to use AI" argument.

Sources: [OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html) -- OECD, January 2026; [OECD Report Warns AI Education Gains Vanish in Exams](https://www.aicerts.ai/news/oecd-report-warns-ai-education-gains-vanish-in-exams/) -- AI CERTs News; [The Impact of Generative AI on Student Learning: Why the OECD Warns Against "Fast AI"](https://www.thesify.ai/blog/impact-generative-ai-student-learning-oecd) -- Thesify.

### 5. Stanford: only 20 rigorous causal studies underpin the entire K-12 AI evidence base

**Stanford SCALE Initiative | March 2026, renewed attention this week**

Stanford's SCALE Initiative published "The Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A 2026 Review" in March 2026, analysing more than 800 academic papers. The striking finding: of all those papers, only 20 contained rigorous causal evidence about AI's actual impact on student or educator outcomes. The report concluded that K-12 AI integration is at a "preliminary" stage of evidence -- AI can function as a powerful assistant for students and teachers, but its benefit to long-term learning "is not yet guaranteed." The report also flagged equity risks (under-resourced schools without access to high-quality education-specific tools) and largely unexamined social-emotional risks from students using AI "companions" outside school hours.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This report is the best single source for grounding the current policy turbulence in evidence. When a parent or board member asks "but does AI actually improve learning?", the honest answer informed by this report is: the evidence for specific, well-designed tools in specific contexts is promising but thin; the evidence for general-purpose AI use in classrooms is mixed to negative. A school that has built its AI approach on that honest foundation is in a stronger position than one that has overpromised on outcomes it cannot yet demonstrate.

Sources: [The Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A 2026 Review](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20Report.pdf) -- Stanford SCALE Initiative, March 2026; [Stanford report questions AI impact in K-12 education](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/stanford-report-finds-limited-evidence-behind-ai-impact-in-k-12-classrooms) -- EdTech Innovation Hub; [Understanding the Evidence Base on AI in K-12 Education](https://scale.stanford.edu/research-in-action/understanding-evidence-base-ai-k12-education) -- Stanford SCALE.

## Worth Watching

**1. National Cabinet AI standards -- no public outcome as at 23 August**

Prime Minister Albanese committed in July to seeking National Cabinet agreement on the proposed Australian Standards for AI in August, with legislation to follow in early 2027. As at this briefing, no public outcome has been reported. The standards as proposed focus primarily on data centres and AI training infrastructure; the education sector's interest is in whether any provisions on AI use in schools emerge from National Cabinet or in subsequent legislation. If this lands in the next week, the education provisions -- if any -- are the thing to read first.

Sources: [AI in Australia's Interests](https://www.pm.gov.au/media/ai-australias-interests) -- Prime Minister of Australia, 15 July 2026; [Anthony Albanese maps out AI future with new national framework](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-07-14/albanese-maps-out-ai-future-introducing-national-framework/106915094) -- ABC News, 14 July 2026.

**2. SA Royal Commission terms of reference -- due in early September**

The SA Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence was announced on 10 August with terms of reference "to be released in the next four to six weeks." That puts the education terms of reference arriving in mid-September, ahead of hearings commencing 1 October. The final report is due 1 July 2027. Once the terms are published, the sector bodies (ISA, AISNSW, NCEC) will almost certainly make submissions, and a practising school with real classroom evidence on AI's effect on student learning would carry weight at a royal commission. Worth flagging now that submissions will open in October.

Sources: [Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence announced](https://www.dpc.sa.gov.au/news/royal-commission-into-artificial-intelligence-announced) -- SA Department of the Premier and Cabinet, 10 August 2026; [Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence](https://ai.sa.gov.au/royal-commission-into-artificial-intelligence) -- Office for AI, SA Government.
