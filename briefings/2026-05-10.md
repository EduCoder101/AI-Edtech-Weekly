# AI in EdTech Weekly -- 10 May 2026

*A research briefing for the AI Integration Lead, Sydney independent K-12 school. Covering 3 May -- 10 May 2026.*

## Top Stories

### 1. Nature retracts landmark ChatGPT-in-education meta-analysis

**Springer Nature / Humanities and Social Sciences Communications | Retraction notice: 22 April 2026. Widely reported: 5 May 2026.**

Springer Nature has retracted a 2025 paper by Jin Wang and Wenxiang Fan (Hangzhou Normal University) that claimed ChatGPT produced a "large positive impact" on student learning performance (effect size g = 0.867), based on a meta-analysis of 51 studies. Before retraction, the paper had accumulated approximately 504 citations, close to 500,000 readers, and ranked in the 99th percentile for online attention among all journal articles. The retraction notice states the editor identified "discrepancies in the meta-analysis" that "ultimately undermine confidence in the validity of the analysis and resulting conclusions." Critics had argued the paper synthesised studies of incompatible methods, populations, and quality levels. A separate meta-analysis published in the same journal this week, drawing on 35 studies (n = 4,193), found a more moderate positive effect (g = 0.670), predominantly in higher education contexts.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This retracted paper has been widely cited in policy discussions, vendor presentations, and school-level AI rationales worldwide. If your school's AI strategy or any external pitch to your leadership has referenced this study -- directly or through secondary sources -- the evidence underpinning that claim no longer stands. The Stanford SCALE Initiative's 2026 review of AI in K-12, published in early 2026, reached a sobering complementary conclusion: there are no high-quality causal studies examining how AI tools affect student learning outcomes in US K-12 classrooms. The lesson is not that AI is ineffective -- it is that the evidence base is far thinner than confident advocates have claimed, and schools should treat vendor-cited research with heightened scrutiny. The moderate positive effects in the new 35-study meta-analysis are from higher education, not primary or secondary settings.

Sources: Springer Nature, [Retraction Note](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-07310-z), *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*, 22 April 2026; Technology.org, ["Widely cited ChatGPT-in-education study retracted nearly a year after publication"](https://www.technology.org/2026/05/05/widely-cited-chatgpt-in-education-study-retracted-nearly-a-year-after-publication/), 5 May 2026; GovTech, ["Nature Retracts Oft-Cited Paper on Positive Impact of ChatGPT"](https://www.govtech.com/education/nature-retracts-oft-cited-paper-on-positive-impact-of-chatgpt), May 2026; Futurism, ["A Major Paper Claiming AI Is Good for Students Just Got Retracted"](https://futurism.com/artificial-intelligence/study-ai-good-for-students-retracted), May 2026; Springer Nature, ["ChatGPT's impact on student learning outcomes: a meta-analysis of 35 experimental studies"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-026-07019-z), *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*, 2026.

### 2. Australian teachers using AI to cope with marking workload -- but integrity questions mount

**EducationHQ | May 2026**

A new EducationHQ report reveals that Australian teachers are turning to AI tools to manage unsustainable marking loads, with some using AI to generate marking guides, assess assignments, and draft feedback. Australian teachers average 52-hour working weeks, with some reaching 60 hours; many schools require student feedback within a two-week turnaround. Teachers report taking personal leave to stay home and mark because school hours are too fragmented. The report does not describe a hidden or fringe practice -- it frames AI-assisted marking as an emerging norm driven by structural workload conditions, not individual choice.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This is arguably the most ethically live issue in Australian schools right now that is not yet being discussed at a policy level. The tension is threefold. First, if teachers are using AI to assess work that students are also using AI to produce, the feedback loop becomes one in which two AI systems interact with a student's learning largely removed from it. Second, if schools have policies prohibiting student AI use in assessment but no equivalent guidance for teachers, the inconsistency is both ethically and legally exposed. Third, the underlying driver -- teacher workload -- is a systemic problem that AI adoption can either ease or worsen depending on how it is deployed. This story warrants a specific conversation at a leadership level: does your school have a position on AI-assisted teacher assessment, and is that position clearly communicated?

Sources: EducationHQ, ["'Without it we can't cope': teachers turn to AI to mark student work"](https://educationhq.com/news/without-it-we-cant-cope-teachers-turn-to-ai-to-mark-student-work-208295/), May 2026.

### 3. IEU NSW/ACT: AI tools risk students' cognitive development

**Independent Education Union NSW/ACT | May 2026**

The Independent Education Union's May 2026 publication argues that over-reliance on AI tools in classrooms is placing students' cognitive abilities at risk. The IEU position, aligned with recent cognitive load theory research, warns that young people need to build strong knowledge foundations and complex reasoning skills through effortful practice -- and that tools which short-circuit that effort undermine the development of the very capabilities schools are meant to cultivate. The union calls for AI tools to be deployed in ways that promote deeper thinking, not replace it, and flags that this requires clear pedagogical intent rather than simply providing access.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This is a rare instance of an Australian industrial body making a substantive pedagogical argument about AI rather than focusing solely on employment or workload concerns. The IEU's position aligns with OECD data published earlier this year showing that students using AI were 48% more successful in completing tasks but 17% worse when the AI was removed -- a pattern the OECD labels a "performance-learning gap." For a Year 5 teacher designing learning tasks that incorporate AI tools, the question is whether the tool is being used to scaffold thinking (appropriate) or to replace it (problematic). The IEU piece is useful as professional reading for staff PL, particularly for teachers who are sceptical of AI use in their classrooms and need a research-grounded language for that scepticism.

Sources: Independent Education Union NSW/ACT, ["Artificial intelligence: Students' cognitive abilities at risk"](https://publications.ieu.asn.au/newsmonthmay2026/news4/artificial-intelligence-students-cognitive-abilities-risk/), *NewsMonth*, May 2026; OECD, ["Digital Education Outlook 2026"](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html), January 2026.

### 4. University of Washington researcher: the AI challenge for teachers is about identity, not just tools

**UW News | 5 May 2026**

A Q&A with a University of Washington researcher published on 5 May explores how teachers are personally reckoning with AI in their professional practice. The central finding is that the hardest problem is not how to use AI tools -- it is where to draw the line between AI as a thought partner that augments professional practice and AI as a substitute that replaces it. Teachers report anxiety about students and parents perceiving AI-assisted work as a form of professional dishonesty. They also describe a deeper worry: that relying on AI erodes their own authentic voice and professional identity. The researcher argues that professional learning programmes that frame AI adoption purely as a skill or efficiency problem will miss the more significant psychological and ethical dimension.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This has direct implications for how you design and facilitate professional learning at your school. Teachers who are resistant to AI may not simply need more training -- they may be responding to a genuine values tension that deserves to be taken seriously in the PL space. The most effective PL around AI tends to begin with those values tensions rather than with tool demonstrations. If teachers feel that using AI makes them less of a teacher, that belief will shape their classroom practice regardless of how confident they are with the technology. This piece is worth distributing to middle leaders and PL designers before any future staff AI workshop.

Sources: UW News, ["Q&A: How are teachers reckoning with AI in schools?"](https://www.washington.edu/news/2026/05/05/qa-how-are-teachers-reckoning-with-ai-in-schools/), 5 May 2026.

### 5. US National Science Foundation invests USD 11 million in K-12 teacher AI professional development

**National Science Foundation | March 2026 (coverage ongoing this week)**

The US National Science Foundation has awarded USD 11 million to the Computer Science Teachers Association (CSTA) to launch AI Professional Development Weeks: CS Foundations for Creating with AI, a multistate programme targeting 2,500--3,000 K-12 teachers over two years. The programme combines intensive summer training with sustained state and local network support, covering foundational computer science concepts, pedagogical strategies for AI, and ethical considerations. It will roll out initially across six states before expanding, with projected downstream reach of up to 600,000 students. The initiative includes a research component examining how sustained professional learning affects how teachers embed AI into classroom instruction.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The scale and specificity of this investment is a signal worth noting. The NSF model -- intensive training plus ongoing network support -- is what research consistently finds more effective than one-off PD workshops. Australia's National AI Literacy Program for students is ambitious, but there is no equivalent investment in sustained, research-informed teacher professional development. For an AI Integration Lead designing PL at school level, the NSF programme's structure (summer intensive + local network) offers a practical model, even if the funding context differs. It is also worth noting that the programme targets computer science teachers specifically, and that less than half of computer science specialist educators globally currently feel prepared to bring AI topics into their teaching.

Sources: National Science Foundation, ["NSF invests $11M to expand AI professional development for K-12 teachers nationwide"](https://www.nsf.gov/news/nsf-invests-11m-expand-ai-professional-development-k-12), 2026; GovTech, ["NSF Awards $11M to Expand AI Training for K-12 Teachers"](https://www.govtech.com/education/k-12/nsf-awards-11m-to-expand-ai-training-for-k-12-teachers), 2026.

## Worth Watching

**1. UK Parliament's AI and EdTech inquiry**

The UK Parliament's Education Committee launched a formal inquiry into AI and educational technology this year, inviting evidence from researchers, practitioners, and the public. The inquiry's scope includes whether AI tools are improving pupil outcomes, what the risks to assessment integrity are, and how teachers should be supported. The UK context is directly relevant to Australia: both systems share curriculum assessment traditions and similar debates about academic integrity. Submissions and interim findings are expected over the coming months and could provide a useful comparative reference point for how regulatory bodies are framing AI governance in schools. Worth monitoring for any conclusions about hard limits on AI use in assessment -- a question that remains open in Australian frameworks.

Source: UK Parliament, [AI and EdTech: MPs launch new inquiry examining technology's role in education](https://committees.parliament.uk/committee/203/education-committee/news/212112/ai-and-edtech-mps-launch-new-inquiry-examining-technologys-role-in-education/), 2026.

**2. Boston Public Schools AI literacy requirement: implementation begins September 2026**

In March 2026, Boston's Mayor announced that Boston Public Schools would become the first major US city district to require AI literacy for graduation, with programming rolling out across all BPS high schools from September. The initiative pairs a USD 1 million public-private seed grant with a curriculum co-developed with UMass Boston, covering productive, ethical, and critical AI use. With implementation now approximately three months away, the programme will produce real-world data on what district-wide AI literacy looks like at scale. This is the most concrete test case globally of what it means to make AI fluency a graduate attribute, and findings will be relevant to any Australian school developing its own AI curriculum scope and sequence.

Sources: WBUR, ["With new program, Boston to ensure AI literacy in public high schools"](https://www.wbur.org/news/2026/03/26/boston-public-schools-ai-literacy), 26 March 2026; UMass Boston, ["Boston Public Schools, UMass Boston Partner on Student AI Literacy Initiative"](https://www.umb.edu/news/recent-news/boston-public-schools-umass-boston-partner--on-student-ai-literacy-initiative/), 2026.
