Adoption is outrunning evidence: covert AI marking, thin research foundations, and a call to start with graduate capabilities

Issue of 21 April 2026

At a glance

The inaugural briefing lands on a consistent picture: Australian teachers under workload pressure are adopting AI faster than policy or evidence can follow, with some using it covertly to mark. Stanford's review found only 20 rigorous causal studies among more than 800, and the leading advice from the CoSN conference was to anchor AI strategy in what graduates should be able to do, not in tools.

Key signals

  • Teachers averaging 52-hour weeks are using AI to manage marking, sometimes in secret, because school policies are silent on staff use.
  • Stanford SCALE: of 800+ studies on AI in K-12, only 20 support causal claims; tools with pedagogical guardrails outperform answer-generators.
  • NC State research: AI tutoring does not automatically redistribute teacher attention; help patterns stay 'sticky' without deliberate strategy.
  • The US has made AI literacy a formal federal grant priority, including detecting AI-generated disinformation.
  • CoSN 2026: define graduate capabilities first, then work backwards to activities and tools.

Considerations for the Executive

  • Make sure the school AI policy explicitly addresses teacher use for assessment, not just student use.
  • Ask vendors for causal evidence, not correlational claims, in any procurement conversation.
  • Anchor the AI strategy to graduate capabilities rather than platforms.

The stories in depth

Full analysis with linked sources
1

Australian teachers turning to AI to mark student work – and doing it covertly

EducationHQ | 14 April 2026

Australian secondary teachers, averaging 52-hour working weeks, are using AI tools to help manage their marking loads. Reporting from EducationHQ includes a Melbourne teacher describing colleagues using AI tools "in secret" due to unclear or absent school policies. The story reflects a broader pattern: AI adoption driven by workload pressure rather than strategic intent, ahead of governance frameworks that would make that use visible and safe.

Why it mattersIf teachers in your school are already using AI to mark and provide feedback – without clear policy guidance – there is a meaningful risk to assessment validity, academic integrity, and data privacy. The immediate practical question is whether your school's AI policy explicitly addresses teacher use for assessment tasks, not just student use. The absence of guidance does not mean the absence of behaviour.

Sources: EducationHQ, "'Without it we can't cope': teachers turn to AI to mark student work", 14 April 2026.

2

Stanford SCALE: only 20 causal studies support AI in K-12 after reviewing 800+

Stanford SCALE Initiative | 2026

Stanford's SCALE Initiative reviewed more than 800 academic studies on AI in K-12 education and identified only 20 with sufficiently rigorous causal designs to support claims about learning outcomes. Key finding: AI tools designed with pedagogical guardrails – those that provide hints, guide reasoning, or scaffold inquiry – show more promising outcomes than tools that simply generate answers, which may reduce the cognitive effort required for durable learning. No high-quality causal studies have been conducted in US K-12 classrooms. Early evidence on educator-facing AI tools is more encouraging: time savings on lesson preparation while maintaining instructional quality.

Why it mattersThis report should be the first reference point whenever a vendor makes learning-outcomes claims. The adoption-evidence gap is now formally documented at scale. Schools making procurement decisions in 2026 should ask vendors to point to causal evidence, not correlational data, and should favour tools that are designed to scaffold student thinking rather than replace it.

Sources: Stanford SCALE Initiative, "The Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A 2026 Review", 2026.

3

NC State research: teacher attention in AI tutoring is "sticky" – and equity implications are real

NC State University | 9 April 2026

Researchers at NC State published a study analysing 1.4 million student-system interactions from 339 students using the MATHia intelligent tutoring system across 14 middle-school maths classes. The finding: when teachers circulate to assist students using AI tutoring tools, they systematically return to help the same students they have helped before. Teacher help is "sticky" – recurrence for previously supported students is a stronger predictor of who gets help than a student's current engagement state. Learning benefits from these interventions are largely session-bound and do not compound. The paper will be presented at the 16th Annual Learning Analytics and Knowledge Conference (LAK26) in Bergen, Norway, 27 April – 1 May.

Why it mattersThis research challenges the assumption that AI tutoring tools automatically redistribute teacher attention more equitably. Without deliberate intervention strategies – for example, using the system's dashboards to identify students the teacher has not yet visited – AI tools may reinforce existing patterns of attention rather than correct them. For primary school classrooms, where teacher relationships are central, this is a practical design question as much as a research one.

Sources: NC State News, "Teachers Tend to Help the Same Kids Repeatedly When Using AI-Powered Tutoring Tools", 9 April 2026.

4

US Department of Education finalises AI literacy as a federal grant priority

US Federal Register | 13 April 2026

The US Department of Education published a final rule on 13 April establishing AI literacy as a supplemental priority in discretionary grant applications. Projects that expand the understanding of AI, integrate AI literacy skills into teaching and learning, and help students detect AI-generated disinformation will receive preference when competing for federal education funding. The rule takes effect 13 May 2026. A parallel priority on Career Pathways and Workforce Readiness was finalised on the same day.

Why it mattersWhile this is a US federal mechanism, it signals a policy direction that may influence Australian funding frameworks: AI literacy is becoming a formal criterion for education investment, not merely a curriculum aspiration. The explicit inclusion of detecting AI-generated disinformation as a funded capability is notable – this framing is likely to appear in Australian state and federal policy documents over the next 12-18 months.

Sources: Federal Register, "Final Priority: Advancing Artificial Intelligence in Education", 13 April 2026; K-12 Dive, "How the Education Department will prioritize AI in awarding grants", April 2026.

5

CoSN 2026: AI is now a "foundational expectation" – but strategy should start with graduate capabilities, not tools

GovTech / EdTech Magazine | 13-15 April 2026

The CoSN Annual Conference in Chicago (13-15 April) gathered district technology leaders who agreed that AI is no longer an emerging or experimental topic in K-12 – it is now a baseline expectation. The most quoted voice from the event was Chris Agnew, Managing Director of Stanford's AI Hub for Education, who argued that schools should resist a tools-first mindset. His recommendation: start by defining what capacities students should have when they graduate, then work backward to identify learning activities and tools that support those capacities. CoSN's 2026 Driving K-12 Innovation report identified staffing shortages, cybersecurity threats, and critical media literacy as the field's biggest obstacles, with generative AI, data visualisation, and privacy tools as the most important technologies to leverage.

Why it mattersThe "graduate capabilities first, tools second" framing is directly applicable to school-level AI strategy. If your school's AI integration planning is currently anchored to specific platforms or features, this is a practical prompt to reframe it around what you want students to be able to do – and whether current tools reliably support that goal.

Sources: GovTech, "CoSN 2026: AI Strategy Should Start With Goals for Student Skills", April 2026; EdTech Magazine, "CoSN2026: Ed Tech Leaders to Collaborate on Future-Ready Learning Environments", April 2026.

Where the evidence agrees

Independent sources pointing the same way

AI adoption is outpacing the evidence base and institutional policy

Schools are integrating AI at scale while the evidence base remains thin and most policy frameworks are trailing actual teacher and student behaviour; this gap is now well-documented, not just anecdotal.

Sources: Stanford SCALE 2026, Stanford AI Index 2026, OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026

AI tools risk reinforcing existing inequities without deliberate design

AI in classrooms can systematically disadvantage already-marginalised students through sticky teacher attention patterns, algorithmic bias, and unequal access to quality tools; equity needs to be designed in, not assumed.

Sources: NC State study, Frontiers: AI and the digital divide, Paul Ramsay Foundation

Purposeful, pedagogically-grounded AI use is the distinguishing factor

Grant bodies, conference keynotes, and international research reports are converging on the same message: AI only produces learning gains when it is embedded in clear pedagogical purpose; access without design produces performance without learning.

Sources: OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026, Stanford SCALE 2026, CoSN 2026 / GovTech

Where experts disagree

Live debates worth understanding before deciding

Does AI improve student learning or create dependency?

One viewOECD (2026): students are 48% more successful completing tasks with AI support – a clear productivity and engagement gain. (OECD)
The other viewOECD (same report) and Stanford SCALE: performance drops 17% when AI is removed; Stanford causal evidence is mixed on whether gains persist on unassisted assessments. (Stanford SCALE)
The tensionThe same evidence base simultaneously supports optimism and caution – AI may boost task completion and motivation without building the durable, transferable skills that assessments and adult life require.

AI detection tools: deploy them or abandon them?

One view68% of schools are using AI detection tools as part of their academic integrity response, treating detection as a necessary layer of accountability. (AI CERTs Global Report)
The other viewVanderbilt University disabled Turnitin's AI detection entirely, citing high false-positive rates – particularly for multilingual writers – and concerns about equity, transparency, and accuracy. (AI CERTs News)
The tensionSchools face genuine uncertainty about whether AI detection is a sound practice: leading institutions are abandoning it while the majority continue to deploy it, and there is no settled consensus on the equity implications.

AI for teacher admin: necessary relief or governance gap?

One viewAustralian teachers describe AI tools for marking and correspondence as a practical necessity given 52-hour working weeks – some using them covertly rather than not at all. (EducationHQ)
The other viewPaul Ramsay Foundation: without national quality assurance frameworks, AI tools adopted for teacher admin risk embedding bias and inequity into student assessment and feedback at scale. (Paul Ramsay Foundation)
The tensionTeachers are adopting AI tools as a workload response while the quality assurance and governance frameworks that would make that use safe and equitable remain absent.

Worth watching

Developments to keep an eye on

LAK26 Conference, Bergen, Norway (27 April – 1 May 2026)

The 16th Annual Learning Analytics and Knowledge Conference will present the full NC State "sticky help" paper alongside a broader program of peer-reviewed research on AI and learning analytics. This is one of the field's highest-quality venues for causal research on AI in classrooms. Worth monitoring for findings that can support evidence-based decisions at the school level – especially as vendor marketing fills the space that rigorous evidence should occupy.

UAE mandatory AI curriculum: the world's most ambitious K-12 AI mandate enters its second year

The UAE made AI education mandatory across all school levels from the 2025-26 academic year as part of its National AI Strategy 2031. No comparable mandate exists in any other country. As the programme moves into its second year, outcome data should begin to emerge. This matters for Australian schools not as a model to copy but as a leading indicator: if large-scale, mandated AI education produces measurable learning gains, it will accelerate pressure on other governments – including Australia's – to formalise AI in the curriculum beyond Digital Technologies.
