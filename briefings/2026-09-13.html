Weekly briefing · 7–13 September 2026

PISA 2025 splits AI shortcuts from AI literacy with the first global dataset, as both of America's largest districts open the year under moratoriums and Australia discovers it has almost no evidence of its own

Issue of 13 September 2026

At a glance

PISA 2025 landed with Australia's worst-ever reading and mathematics results, but the finding that belongs in a staff meeting is the AI one: students who use AI to complete schoolwork tasks score about 20 points lower in science, while daily users whose classes require them to critique AI output score 13 points higher. In the same week, a University of Sydney and Barker Institute review of 271 studies worldwide found only four were Australian, UNESCO's education ministers adopted an eight-goal statement on AI as a public good, and the US school year began with New York City's moratorium in force and Los Angeles confirming its own. The Heads Up Alliance petition passed 28,000 signatures, previewing the parent conversation heading for Australian schools.

Key signals

  • OECD data from PISA 2025 show students using AI for schoolwork tasks such as summarising and drafting score roughly 20 points lower in science than non-users, while students who use AI daily and are consistently required to critique AI-produced content score 13 points higher, a 33-point spread between task-substitution and structured AI literacy.
  • Australian 15-year-olds recorded their worst-ever reading (491) and mathematics (478) results while remaining above OECD averages, and scored 532 against an OECD average of 500 on the new computational problem-solving domain.
  • A University of Sydney and Barker Institute review of 271 studies on generative AI and school-age learners across 45-plus countries found only four Australian studies, with researchers calling a local evidence base an urgent national priority.
  • More than 25 education ministers at UNESCO's Digital Learning Week adopted a joint statement with eight commitments, including public review of AI systems offered to schools, teacher say over classroom tools, and age-appropriate safeguards.
  • New York City's one-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI through Grade 8 took effect on 10 September for 600,000 students, Los Angeles Unified confirmed a districtwide moratorium on district devices, and the Heads Up Alliance petition to remove generative AI from Australian classrooms passed 28,000 signatures.

Considerations for the Executive

  • Rebuild the school's AI guidance around the distinction PISA has now quantified: the question is not whether students use AI but whether they use it to think or to avoid thinking, and professional learning should shift from tool training toward teaching staff and students to interrogate AI output.
  • Start documenting the school's own classroom evidence on AI use and learning, positive and negative: with four Australian studies in a field of 271, a school that systematically records what it sees is contributing to the national evidence base, and will have material ready for the SA Royal Commission's submission window.
  • Check the school's current AI position against UNESCO's eight ministerial commitments, and prepare the principal's answer to the question the US moratoriums and the 28,000-signature petition are making inevitable: not 'should we allow AI?' but 'why haven't you restricted it?'.

1

PISA 2025 lands with a direct finding on AI use: task-based AI is linked to lower science scores, but AI literacy flips the result

OECD / ACER | 8 September 2026

The first volume of PISA 2025 results was released on 8 September, covering science, reading, mathematics and computational problem-solving across 91 countries and economies. The headline for Australia is grim on the familiar measures: Australian 15-year-olds recorded their worst-ever reading and mathematics scores, extending a decline that has continued since 2003, though Australia remains above the OECD average in all three domains (reading 491 vs OECD 461; maths 478 vs OECD 463). The counterweight, reported less prominently, is that Australian students scored 532 on the new computational problem-solving domain, well above the OECD average of 500.

The AI finding is the part that belongs in a meeting. OECD data show that students who use AI for specific schoolwork tasks – summarising texts, drafting or conducting research – score approximately 20 points lower in science than students who do not use AI for those purposes. But for students who use AI every day and whose classes consistently require them to critique AI-produced content, science scores are 13 points higher than non-users. The implication is not "AI is bad"; it is that AI used as a task-completion shortcut is associated with weaker outcomes, while structured AI literacy practice reverses that association. Federal Education Minister Jason Clare responded on 10 September, citing the results as justification for ongoing curriculum reforms and flagging screen time in schools as an issue he would raise with state Education Ministers.

Sources: PISA 2025: Students' reading and mathematics performance declined sharply across the OECD – OECD, 8 September 2026; Australian students record worst-ever reading and maths results in international PISA test – ABC News, 8 September 2026; Australia's reading and maths results are at an 'all-time low' – but there's more to the story – The Conversation, 8 September 2026; PISA findings on artificial intelligence use, reading skills and learning – OECD blog, September 2026; PISA results show why the reforms we are rolling out are so important – Minister Jason Clare, September 2026; PISA 2025: Reporting Australia's results. Volume I: Student performance – ACER, September 2026.

Why it mattersThis is the first internationally scaled dataset to separate task-substitution AI use from AI literacy instruction, and the 33-point gap between those two groups is large enough to matter. For a school deciding how to frame its AI guidance for students and staff, the PISA finding offers a clear organising principle: the question is not whether students use AI, but whether they use it to think or to avoid thinking. The result should also reshape how professional learning on AI is designed – moving from "how to use tools" toward "how to evaluate and interrogate AI output". The full PISA "Learning in the Digital World" report is not due until May 2027, but the data released this week are already sufficient to take to a staff meeting.
2

University of Sydney and Barker Institute review finds Australia is nearly absent from its own AI evidence base

University of Sydney / Barker Institute | covered 8 September 2026

Researchers from the University of Sydney and the Barker Institute published what they describe as one of the largest evidence reviews of its kind worldwide: 271 studies across more than 45 countries examining the impact of generative AI on learners from pre-kindergarten to Year 12. Of those 271 studies, only four were Australian. The review found growing evidence that GenAI can increase student engagement, motivation and confidence, and can help students complete learning tasks and improve the surface quality of their work. It also found major gaps in understanding how GenAI affects deeper learning, reasoning, self-regulation, and longer-term development – the outcomes that schooling is principally organised to produce. Researchers called for a major expansion of Australian school-based research as an urgent priority, noting that Australian schools are being asked to make consequential decisions without a local evidence base to draw on.

Sources: Australia lacks evidence on GenAI in schools, landmark global review finds – University of Sydney, 3 September 2026; More Australian data needed on GenAI impacts, say researchers – Education Matters Magazine, 8 September 2026.

Why it mattersThis finding directly contextualises every Australian AI policy announced this year, including the NESA take-home assessment cap and the SA Royal Commission. All of them are being designed without robust Australian data on what GenAI actually does to student learning in Australian classrooms. The research gap is not simply an academic problem: it means Australian schools have no local evidence to test whether a vendor's claims, a government restriction, or a school-level policy is well-calibrated to Australian students and teachers. The gap between Barker College as a practitioner and the formal research record is larger than it should be, and a school that systematically documents and shares its own findings is contributing to the field, not just consuming it.

UNESCO's 25 education ministers adopt joint statement on AI as a public good – with eight specific commitments

UNESCO | 8--11 September 2026

At UNESCO's Digital Learning Week in Paris (8--11 September), more than 25 education ministers and designated representatives from around the world adopted a joint ministerial statement calling for education to be sustained as a human right and a common good in the age of AI. The statement lists eight goals: AI systems offered to schools, even for free, should be open to public review; students should build critical thinking skills rather than rely on AI to do their work; teachers should have a say in which AI tools enter their classrooms; AI should support teachers rather than replace them; governments should promote public-interest AI and digital sovereignty; analogue and community-led approaches to learning retain a place; age-appropriate safeguards should govern student AI access; and equity considerations must inform every decision. UNESCO also released a set of policy briefs covering AI safety governance, sovereignty, procurement, cost models and age-appropriateness.

Sources: Education in the age of AI: Ministerial statement to be adopted at UNESCO's Digital Learning Week – UNESCO, September 2026; UNESCO Ministers Sign Joint Statement on AI in Education – AgentLocker, September 2026; UNESCO Digital Learning Week tackles AI in education – WINS Solutions, September 2026.

Why it mattersThis is the international policy layer that typically shapes Australian frameworks within one to two years – the way UNESCO's 2023 generative AI guidance seeded age limits and human-oversight language in national policies worldwide, including Australia's own national framework reviewed in June 2025. The eight specific commitments give Australian school leaders something concrete to check their current position against: the "teachers should have a say" goal, in particular, is a useful prompt for any school designing AI governance. The statement's emphasis on public review of "free" AI tools is directly relevant to any school evaluating whether to take up the vendor offers currently on the table (Anthropic, Google, OpenAI). Worth reading in full when the final text is published.
4

AI restrictions take hold across US school districts as the new school year begins

Multiple sources | 10--11 September 2026

Thursday 10 September marked the first day of the 2026-27 school year for New York City Public Schools, and with it the start of the one-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI for the 600,000 students in pre-K through Grade 8 announced on 2 September. Reports from the opening week noted that many parents were relieved and supportive. Meanwhile, Los Angeles Unified School District – the second-largest in the United States, with approximately 378,000 students – confirmed its own districtwide moratorium on generative AI tools on district-issued devices for all grade levels, also for the 2026-27 school year. Chicago school board candidates began lining up to sign a pledge demanding a three-year moratorium. An AP wire summary published on 10 September noted that states and districts across the country are tightening rules, with education leaders citing concerns over student learning, screen time and the risks of rapidly advancing technology. Axios reported on 7 September that parents, students and educators are revolting against AI in classrooms, framing the moment as a convergence of previously separate concerns: generative AI, device overuse, screen time, student wellbeing and distrust of technology companies.

Sources: Back to school means new AI and screen time limits in NYC classrooms. Many parents are relieved. – Chalkbeat New York, 10 September 2026; AI restrictions spread as K-12 school year begins – yournews.com, 10 September 2026; AI Restrictions Continue to Widen As New School Year Begins – American Greatness, 11 September 2026; A growing movement wants AI out of schools – Axios, 7 September 2026; LAUSD restricts all students from using AI tools – K-12 Dive, September 2026; America's Two Largest School Districts Impose AI Moratoriums – Tech Policy Press, September 2026.

Why it mattersThe US situation is now a clear data point rather than a scattered anecdote: both of the country's two largest school districts have imposed restrictions in the same week, and a third major city is considering following. The direction is coherent with what NSW did earlier in September and what NYC announced the previous week. The Axios framing – that this is a convergence of AI, screen time, wellbeing and Big Tech distrust – is a useful lens for understanding what is driving parent pressure in Australia as well. A school leader preparing a community communication about the school's AI position will be asked about these international examples; this week they are all pointing the same way.

Heads Up Alliance petition tops 28,000 signatures in Australia

Heads Up Alliance / St George and Sutherland Shire Leader | this week

An Australian parent-and-teacher campaign calling for the removal of generative AI from classrooms has gathered more than 28,000 signatures on Change.org. The Heads Up Alliance – a community of families who have opted to delay social media and smartphones for children – launched the petition in early August 2026 with five nationwide demands: device-free primary schools, restoration of literacy and handwriting as core priorities, a return to print and paper as the high school default, strict vetting of edtech tools, and the removal of generative AI from classrooms until it has been proven effective. The co-founder described the campaign as driven by teachers within the network who are seeing firsthand the impact of AI and edtech on student learning. The petition has grown rapidly alongside the international moratorium announcements from NYC and LAUSD.

Sources: Heads Up Alliance: Petition for device-free classrooms tops 28,000 – St George and Sutherland Shire Leader, this week; Suspend the use of AI and 1:1 devices in schools: teachers, parents launch new petition – EducationHQ, August 2026.

Why it mattersTwenty-eight thousand signatures is a signal of community sentiment that is approaching the point where politicians and sector bodies will feel obligated to respond formally. The five demands mirror, in stronger form, what regulators in the US have been moving towards. For a Sydney independent school, the petition is a preview of the parent conversation that is coming: not "should we allow AI?" but "why haven't you restricted it?". The school's answer to that question needs to be principled, evidence-based and specific about what it does and does not permit for each year group – the kind of position the PISA findings this week can help inform.

Where experts disagree

Live debates worth understanding before deciding

What do AI use and student outcomes tell us?

One viewPISA 2025 and Stanford SCALE: students using AI for tasks score significantly lower in science, and the rigorous causal evidence base remains thin
The other viewMDPI systematic review and AI literacy advocates: adoption driven by institutional support and professional development produces better outcomes; the PISA finding is correlational, not causal
The tensionWhether AI use is a symptom of weaker engagement or a cause of it is unresolved; schools enforcing restrictions without building AI literacy may be addressing the symptom rather than the root cause

Should schools restrict or integrate?

One viewHeads Up Alliance, NYC and LAUSD: restrict student AI use until there is adequate evidence of benefit, given the current associations with lower outcomes
The other viewUNESCO statement and most vendor-aligned voices: the answer is guided integration with literacy instruction, not restriction; banning use leaves students less prepared for a world saturated with AI
The tensionThe fracture point is how much evidence is enough to justify continued student-facing AI use; neither side currently has the causal evidence it would need to win the argument conclusively

Is Australia's strong digital world PISA score good news?

One viewKarmactive and some commentators: Australia's score of 532 vs OECD average of 500 on computational problem-solving is a genuine bright spot and signals that digital learning approaches have some traction
The other viewThe Conversation and critics of the digital pivot: the record-low reading and maths scores during the same period as heavy device adoption undermines the case that the digital turn has helped foundational learning
The tensionThe same cohort that scored well on digital problem-solving recorded its worst-ever reading result; whether those two facts are connected, and in which direction, is the central question for Australian education technology policy

Worth watching

Developments to keep an eye on

SA Royal Commission: commissioners start 1 October, terms of reference still pending

The commissioners for South Australia's Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence are scheduled to begin work on 1 October 2026, with a final report due 1 July 2027. Education is confirmed in scope. The terms of reference had not been officially released as of this briefing; they were originally promised four to six weeks after the 10 August announcement. Once the terms land and a submission process opens, there will be a narrow window for schools and sector bodies to contribute practitioner evidence directly to the public record. A submission from an independent school describing real classroom experience with AI – positive and negative – would be among the more useful contributions the sector could make. ISA and AISNSW are the obvious channels to watch for a coordinated sector response.

Sources: Royal Commission into Artificial Intelligence – Office for AI, SA Government; SA royal commission to test AI's limits – The Mandarin, August 2026.

PISA 2025 "Learning in the Digital World" detailed report, due May 2027

The September 8 release covered science, reading, mathematics and computational problem-solving. The full analysis of the "Learning in the Digital World" innovative domain, including detailed findings on how students learn in digital environments and what classroom practices are associated with better outcomes, is due in May 2027. The AI-use correlation data released this week is a preview of a richer dataset. When the full report lands it will be the most significant international evidence base available for Australian digital learning policy, and schools that have been collecting their own data on AI use and student outcomes in the intervening period will be better placed to interpret and respond to it.

Sources: PISA 2025 Learning in the Digital World – OECD; PISA 2025 Results (Volume I) – OECD, 8 September 2026.
