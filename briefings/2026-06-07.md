# AI in EdTech Weekly -- 7 June 2026

*A research briefing for the AI Integration Lead, Sydney independent K-12 school. Covering 1 June -- 7 June 2026.*

## Top Stories

### 1. EDUtech Australia 2026: AI and assessment dominate Australia's largest education technology event

**Teacher Magazine (ACER) / EDUtech / ICC Sydney | 3-4 June 2026**

Australia's largest education technology conference ran this week at the International Convention Centre in Sydney, drawing more than 10,000 attendees, 350+ speakers, and 300+ exhibitors across two days. AI in the classroom and the ethics of generative technology was the headline theme, alongside assessment reform, digital infrastructure, and cybersecurity. Teacher Magazine's coverage headlined with "AI, assessment, and keeping humans in the loop," capturing the dominant thread across sessions: that AI's role in student work -- and in marking that work -- is now the central policy and pedagogy challenge for Australian schools. Keynote speakers included Professor Andy Hargreaves (leadership), Professor Rose Luckin (human-AI collaboration), and neuroscientist Sarah McKay. A recurring finding across sessions was that AI detection tools cannot reliably identify AI-generated student work, making technological solutions to integrity problems structurally inadequate.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This is the clearest signal yet that the Australian education sector has moved past the "is AI coming?" question and into the harder work of assessment design, teacher capacity, and governance. The explicit framing of "keeping humans in the loop" -- across both marking and curriculum decisions -- reflects emerging professional consensus that AI tools need deliberate pedagogical scaffolding, not passive permission. For an AI integration lead, the conference outputs (published sessions, keynote recordings, and exhibitor commitments) are worth reviewing as a live snapshot of where Australian schools' priorities sit in mid-2026. The unreliability of AI detection tools is particularly significant: it shifts the burden of integrity assurance from surveillance back to assessment design itself.

Sources: Teacher Magazine (ACER), ["EDUtech 2026: AI, assessment, and keeping humans in the loop"](https://www.teachermagazine.com/au_en/articles/edutech-2026-ai-assessment-and-keeping-humans-in-the-loop), June 2026; EDUtech, ["EDUtech Australia 2026 programme"](https://www.terrapinn.com/exhibition/edutech-australia/index.stm), 3-4 June 2026; ICC Sydney, ["EDUtech AU"](https://iccsydney.com.au/events/edutech-au/), June 2026.

### 2. Learning First report: three-quarters of NSW secondary teachers say students use AI for assessments, half don't know how to stop it

**Learning First / Information Age (ACS) / Mirage News | Late May 2026**

A major new survey of more than 4,000 NSW teachers and school leaders, released by education consulting group Learning First in late May, found that about half of secondary teachers report students using AI for schoolwork -- and of those, approximately three-quarters say students do so to complete assessments, despite most schools having restrictions in place. Around half of secondary teachers said they do not know how to prevent AI-related plagiarism or cheating. About 80 per cent of teachers and school leaders whose students use AI for schoolwork were worried about its impact on education. The report, "AI Use in Schools: Taking Action Now," drew on a survey conducted across government, Catholic, and independent schools in NSW. Three-quarters of teachers overall said they had used AI themselves for professional duties, predominantly to develop curriculum resources.

**Why it matters for K-12:** These numbers are from NSW schools specifically and represent the sharpest available Australian data on the gap between stated school AI policy and actual student behaviour. The finding that students are using AI for assessments even where restrictions formally apply is not a compliance failure so much as a design problem: if students can complete assessment tasks using AI without detection, the tasks are not doing what they need to do. This is a direct challenge to every school's current assessment approach and demands an honest internal conversation about which assessments are genuinely testing student learning rather than AI capability. For independent schools in Sydney, this data point -- drawn from the same state system -- is directly applicable.

Sources: Learning First, ["AI use in schools -- Taking Action Now"](https://learningfirst.com/research/aiuseinschools), May 2026; Information Age (ACS), ["Australian students using AI to complete assessments"](https://ia.acs.org.au/article/2026/australian-students-using-ai-to-complete-assessments.html), 2026; Mirage News, ["Data urges schools to address student AI use"](https://www.miragenews.com/data-urges-schools-to-address-student-ai-use-1679449/), May 2026.

### 3. Stanford's 2026 K-12 evidence review: 1,100 studies, 20 rigorous ones -- performance gains with AI may not survive the exam

**Stanford SCALE Initiative / GovTech | 2026**

The Stanford Accelerator for Learning's SCALE Initiative has published "The Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A 2026 Review," analysing more than 1,100 academic studies and identifying just 20 that meet high-quality causal standards -- that is, studies that can genuinely attribute changes in outcomes to AI tool use. Across those 20 studies, the picture is mixed: students perform better on tasks when they can use AI tools during the activity, but the evidence is inconsistent when students are assessed without AI support, with some showing performance improvement, others showing no change, and some showing decline. The researchers flag a "widening adoption-evidence gap," noting that schools are integrating AI into daily practice well ahead of any robust research foundation. Key methodological limitations in the broader literature include tracking outcomes only while tools are in use, reliance on self-reported data, and small or narrow samples.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This is the most comprehensive and methodologically careful audit of the AI-in-education evidence base currently available, and its headline finding is sobering: the vast majority of AI classroom tool claims rest on weak research. For a school weighing which tools to invest in, or a professional learning programme asserting that a particular AI application improves outcomes, this report is an important discipline. It does not say AI tools cannot work; it says the evidence that specific tools produce durable learning gains -- not just improved outputs during tool use -- is almost entirely absent. The distinction between "better output with AI assistance" and "better learning that persists when AI is removed" is one that every school leader and curriculum director needs to hold clearly.

Sources: Stanford SCALE Initiative, ["The Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A 2026 Review"](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20Report.pdf), 2026; Stanford SCALE Initiative, ["Understanding the Evidence Base on AI in K-12 Education"](https://scale.stanford.edu/research-in-action/understanding-evidence-base-ai-k12-education), 2026; GovTech, ["Stanford Researchers Find Thin Evidence Behind AI Classroom Tools"](https://www.govtech.com/education/k-12/stanford-researchers-find-thin-evidence-behind-ai-classroom-tools), 2026.

### 4. OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026: AI improves student outputs while undermining the learning underneath them

**OECD | January 2026 (primary publication; widely circulated this week)**

The OECD's Digital Education Outlook 2026, "Exploring Effective Uses of Generative AI in Education," synthesises emerging research on how generative AI affects teaching and learning across OECD member countries, including Australia. Its central finding is that successfully completing tasks with generative AI does not necessarily translate into learning: students using general-purpose AI tools often produce higher-quality outputs, but these advantages tend to disappear -- or reverse -- when AI access is removed, such as in exams. The report documents what it calls "metacognitive laziness": a reduction in the productive cognitive struggle that genuine learning requires. The OECD's recommendation is pointed -- general-purpose AI must be used with explicit pedagogical purpose or replaced by purpose-built educational AI tools designed to support, rather than bypass, the cognitive effort of learning. The Outlook also notes teacher concerns: 72 per cent of teachers express worry that AI will allow students to present AI-generated work as their own.

**Why it matters for K-12:** The OECD has global authority and its policy recommendations carry significant weight with Australian federal and state governments. The "metacognitive laziness" finding is the international research anchor for the concern being voiced locally by the UTS/Paul Ramsay Foundation cognitive atrophy work and the Learning First assessment data. These are now converging across a peer-reviewed, multi-country evidence base. For classroom teachers, the practical implication is that permitting students to use general-purpose AI tools without redesigning the cognitive demands of the task is not a neutral choice -- it risks systematically reducing the kind of thinking the task was meant to develop. Purpose-built educational AI, designed to maintain productive struggle, is a meaningfully different intervention.

Sources: OECD, ["OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026: Exploring Effective Uses of Generative AI in Education"](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026_062a7394-en.html), January 2026; CIDDL, ["Summary of OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026"](https://ciddl.org/summary-of-oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026/), 2026; Digital Skills and Jobs Platform (EU), ["OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026: how generative AI can support learning when used with purpose"](https://digital-skills-jobs.europa.eu/en/latest/news/oecd-digital-education-outlook-2026-how-generative-ai-can-support-learning-when-used), 2026.

### 5. eSafety Commissioner: AI-generated intimate image reports involving students under 18 have more than doubled

**eSafety Commissioner | Ongoing; updated guidance June 2026**

The eSafety Commissioner has this week urged schools to report deepfake incidents as reports of digitally altered intimate images -- including AI-generated content -- involving people under 18 have more than doubled over the past 18 months, compared with the total number of reports received in the preceding seven years. Four in five of these reports involve the targeting of females. The Commissioner has written to education ministers urging schools to adhere to mandatory reporting obligations and has released an updated Toolkit for Schools with a step-by-step guide for handling deepfake incidents. In a related enforcement action (from March 2026), eSafety issued a formal Direction to Comply to one of the most-visited nudify services, which had attracted nearly 40,000 Australian visits per month. The eSafety Commissioner is also hosting a webinar on 25 June 2026 (3:45 pm AEST) for school educators covering AI companion chatbots, deepfakes, and AI-facilitated cyberbullying. The Children's Online Privacy Code, which closed for public consultation on 5 June 2026, is expected to impose new obligations on edtech providers handling student data when finalised by 10 December 2026.

**Why it matters for K-12:** This is an active safeguarding emergency, not a future risk. Australian school communities -- including independent schools in Sydney -- have already experienced incidents. The toolkit is practical and worth distributing to pastoral care and student wellbeing teams now. The mandatory reporting angle is particularly important: the Commissioner's letter to education ministers means the expectation of consistent school responses is now formally on the ministerial agenda, and schools that have not yet briefed their leadership teams and updated safeguarding policies should do so. The June 25 webinar is a direct, no-cost professional learning opportunity for staff.

Sources: eSafety Commissioner, ["eSafety urges schools to report deepfakes as numbers double"](https://www.esafety.gov.au/newsroom/media-releases/esafety-urges-schools-to-report-deepfakes-as-numbers-double), 2026; eSafety Commissioner, ["Deepfake damage in schools"](https://www.esafety.gov.au/newsroom/blogs/deepfake-damage-in-schools-how-ai-generated-abuse-is-disrupting-students-families-and-school-communities), 2026; eSafety Commissioner, ["AI-assisted image-based abuse: Navigating the deepfake threat (webinar, 25 June)"](https://www.esafety.gov.au/parents/webinars/ai-assisted-image-based-abuse-navigating-the-deepfake-threat), June 2026; eSafety Commissioner, ["eSafety moves against services used to nudify Australian school children"](https://www.esafety.gov.au/newsroom/media-releases/esafety-moves-against-services-used-to-nudify-australian-school-children), March 2026.

## Worth Watching

**1. TRAINE study: first results from Australia's longitudinal AI-in-schools tracker expected this month**

The Tracking AI in Education (TRAINE) study, led by Dr Samantha-Kaye Johnston and Professor Therese Hopfenbeck at the University of Melbourne's Assessment and Evaluation Research Centre, is a five-year longitudinal investigation of how generative AI is influencing teaching, learning, and assessment in Australian secondary classrooms. The first results are expected for publication in June 2026. This will be the first substantial Australian-specific longitudinal data on how AI tools are changing classroom practice at the secondary level -- a different and more trustworthy evidence base than the survey data that has dominated Australian AI-in-schools reporting so far. Watch the [AERC research page](https://education.unimelb.edu.au/aerc/research/projects/Tracking-AI-in-Education-TRAINE) for the release.

**2. ESA AI professional learning Module 2 now available -- free for all Australian teachers**

Education Services Australia, in partnership with Microsoft, has released the second module in its free AI professional learning series for teachers, aligned with the Australian Professional Standards for Teachers and the national AI in Schools Framework. Module 1 covered foundations; Module 2 focuses on AI implementation in classroom contexts. Access is free for teachers across Australia and New Zealand. At a moment when independent schools are making investment decisions about staff professional learning, a free, standards-aligned national resource is worth evaluating as a baseline offering before layering school-specific content. Details at [ESA](https://www.esa.edu.au/news-insights/training-for-teachers-boosts-confidence-in-safe-and-effective-ai-use-in-classrooms).
