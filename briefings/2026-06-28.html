National agencies move towards a single commission, while adoption data shows AI use has outrun training everywhere

Issue of 28 June 2026

At a glance

The design proposal for a Teaching and Learning Commission merging ACARA, AITSL, AERO and ESA went to ministers this month, concentrating national AI-in-education policy in one future body. Microsoft's six-country survey, which includes Australia, finds 92% of students using AI while 77% have never been formally trained, and both Microsoft and Google announced AI features that will arrive inside platforms schools already run.

Key signals

  • The Teaching and Learning Commission would become the single national locus for curriculum, teacher standards, research and digital resources, and therefore for AI policy.
  • Microsoft's AI in Education Report: 92% of students and leaders use AI; 77% of students and 53% of educators report no formal training; most want regular training provided.
  • Google is embedding Gemini directly into Classroom, and Microsoft is adding free AI tools to 365 Education. AI will arrive via feature updates, not procurement decisions.
  • Stanford's SCALE review of 800+ studies finds only 20 causal ones; performance gains tend to fade when the AI is removed, except in tools with pedagogical guardrails.
  • 52 AI-in-education bills are active across 25 US states, signalling where regulation in the English-speaking world is heading: mandatory school AI policies and limits on AI replacing teachers.

Considerations for the Executive

  • Ensure AI governance covers feature updates to existing platforms (Google Workspace, Microsoft 365), not only new tool approvals.
  • Use the Microsoft training-gap data to justify structured AI professional learning in the next planning cycle.
  • Adopt a procurement question backed by the Stanford review: is this tool designed to support the learning process or just to produce output?

Australian Teaching and Learning Commission: design proposal delivered to ministers this month

Australian Government Department of Education | June 2026

The working group established at the October 2025 Education Ministers Meeting was due to deliver its initial design proposal for a new Teaching and Learning Commission this month. The proposal, if adopted, would merge four national agencies – ACARA (curriculum and NAPLAN), AITSL (teacher standards and professional learning), AERO (education research), and Education Services Australia (digital curriculum resources) – into a single national body. The 2026-27 Budget has committed $5.6 million over two years to develop the proposal. A separate AITSL scoping report on the first review of the Australian Professional Standards for Teachers in 15 years was due to be presented at the same time. Education sector bodies including the Australian Education Union, principals' associations, and the Australian Council for Student Voice have all lodged positions ahead of the design; the AEU's central condition is that classroom teachers must be represented in the Commission's governance, not merely consulted.

Why it mattersEvery national framework that shapes how Australian schools integrate AI – the Australian Framework for Generative AI in Schools, teacher professional standards, the evidence base schools draw on, and the digital curriculum resources they use – currently sits across these four agencies with imperfect coordination. If the Commission proceeds, it would become the single locus for national AI-in-education policy, professional learning standards, and the quality assurance of EdTech products. For an independent school building an AI strategy, understanding the Commission's likely structure and priorities is worth tracking closely: what replaces the current Framework review cycle, who sets teacher AI capability standards, and which research body schools can cite for procurement decisions could all change over the next two years.

Sources: Australian Government Department of Education, "Teaching and Learning Commission (Budget factsheet)", 2026; The Mandarin, "National teaching, learning commission to bring greater coordination", 2026; The Educator K-12, "What principals need from the Teaching and Learning Commission", 2026; AEU, "Teaching and Learning Commission proposal must have teachers at its core", 2026; Education Matters Magazine, "Principals and teachers push for voice in new commission", 2026.

Microsoft AI in Education Report 2026: 92% adoption, 77% of students still untrained

Microsoft / ISTELive 26 | 24 June 2026

Microsoft released the third edition of its annual AI in Education Report on 24 June, timed ahead of ISTELive 26. The report surveyed 3,345 respondents across K-12 and higher education in six countries – the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, Brazil, Japan, and Saudi Arabia. Headline findings: 92% of students and education leaders and 88% of educators now use AI for school-related purposes; 78% of leaders, 76% of educators, and 65% of students say their use has increased over the past year. The training gap is the sharpest finding: 77% of students and 53% of educators say they have not received formal AI training, while 66% of educators and 52% of students want training provided monthly or quarterly. Alongside the report, Microsoft announced free additions to Microsoft 365 Education for the coming school year, including AI-assisted Unit Plans, Student AI Guidelines and Learning Groups in Assignments, Learning Zone classroom experiences, Copilot Notebooks, and a Study and Learn Agent.

Why it mattersAustralia is one of the six surveyed countries, so the data is at least partially reflective of Australian schools rather than US-only context. The training gap finding – nearly four in five students using AI without any formal instruction in how to use it responsibly or effectively – is the most actionable number in this report. It maps directly to the professional learning gap the ESA Module 2 series is trying to address (covered last week). For a school planning AI professional learning for Term 3, this data provides straightforward evidence for why structured, not ad hoc, training is necessary. The free Microsoft 365 additions are worth assessing if your school already uses that ecosystem: Copilot Notebooks and the Study and Learn Agent in particular are student-facing tools that will need to be reflected in your acceptable use policy.

Sources: Microsoft, "Microsoft's New AI in Education Report highlights widespread adoption and increasing demand for support", 24 June 2026; PRNewswire, same release via PR wire, 24 June 2026; Open Data Science, "Microsoft's 2026 AI in Education Report Shows Schools Need More AI Training", June 2026; Windows Report, "Microsoft 365 Education Gets Free AI Teaching and Learning Tools Ahead of ISTELive 2026", June 2026.

Google at ISTELive 2026: Gemini embedded in Classroom, free AI training for six million educators

Google for Education | June 2026

Google made a cluster of announcements at ISTELive 2026 this week. The most substantive: a connected Classroom app in Gemini that uses a class's actual assignments, grades, and materials to help teachers analyse progress and draft tailored activities, maintaining existing privacy guardrails. For students, Google is rolling out study notebooks – a Gemini-powered adaptive learning space that generates personalised lessons and quizzes based on a student's learning goal and adapts as the student progresses. Teachers will be able to assign class-specific study notebooks directly in Google Classroom and receive insights on where individual students or cohorts need support. Google also announced a partnership with ISTE+ASCD to provide free Gemini AI training to all six million US K-12 and higher education teachers, and committed Google.org funding to support long-term partners working at the intersection of AI and education equity.

Why it mattersThe Classroom app in Gemini is significant because it integrates AI into an existing workflow rather than requiring teachers to switch contexts. If your school uses Google Workspace for Education, these features will arrive without additional procurement decisions. The more important design principle is what they illustrate: AI tools that draw on actual class materials rather than generic knowledge are more likely to produce relevant, pedagogically appropriate outputs. The six-million-educator training commitment in the US will generate a large body of practitioner experience by end of year; Australian educators should be watching the resources and case studies that emerge from it, even if the scale is very different. The study notebook feature for students warrants careful review against your school's assessment integrity policy before it is used in assessment contexts.

Sources: Google for Education, "Building AI tailored for education, with educators in the lead", June 2026; Google for Education, "Supporting students with connected AI tools for more personalised learning", June 2026; ISTE 2026 collection, "Supporting teaching and learning with connected AI tools", June 2026; EdTech Innovation Hub, "Google and ISTE+ASCD launch free Gemini AI training for 6 million US educators", June 2026.

Stanford SCALE review: of 800+ AI-in-K12 studies, only 20 are causal – and performance gains fade when AI is removed

Stanford SCALE Initiative | 2026

Stanford's Center to Support Excellence in Learning (SCALE) published a comprehensive review of the evidence base for AI in K-12 education, analysing more than 800 academic papers and identifying just 20 causal studies that rigorously measure how AI tools affect student or educator outcomes. The core finding: most studies focus on technical development, model performance, or observational data rather than measured impact on learning. Crucially, AI can improve student performance while in use, but those gains appear to fade when the technology is removed – particularly with general-purpose chatbots. AI tools built with pedagogical guardrails, such as tutoring systems that give hints or guide reasoning rather than providing answers, show more durable outcomes. The review explicitly calls out the risk of procurement and policy decisions being driven by tool availability and vendor claims rather than measured evidence.

Why it mattersThis is the most rigorous independent review of the AI-in-education evidence base currently available. For those leading AI integration advising on tool adoption, the "performance without learning" finding is the essential caution: students who use AI as a shortcut to output can appear to do well while building no transferable capability. This directly connects to the cognitive atrophy concerns raised by UTS researchers in March. The distinction Stanford draws – between general-purpose tools (weaker evidence) and purpose-built pedagogical AI (more promising) – gives you a practical lens for evaluating tool proposals at your school. "Is this tool designed to support the learning process or to produce an output?" is now a defensible procurement question backed by peer-reviewed research.

Sources: Stanford SCALE Initiative, "Understanding the Evidence Base on AI in K-12 Education", 2026; Stanford SCALE, full report PDF, 2026; GovTech, "Stanford Researchers Find Thin Evidence Behind AI Classroom Tools", 2026; EdTech Innovation Hub, "Stanford report finds limited evidence behind AI impact in K-12 classrooms", 2026.

US state legislatures: 52 AI-in-education bills active across 25 states – international policy signal for Australian schools

FutureEd / EdWeek | June 2026

FutureEd's newly released 2026 State AI in Education Legislative Tracker is monitoring 52 active bills across 25 US states addressing artificial intelligence in schools. The bills cover three broad areas: what students learn about AI (AI literacy curricula), how schools integrate AI into coursework (permitted uses, conditions of use), and what guardrails govern AI provider behaviour (transparency, data handling, parental consent). Notable examples: South Carolina's H.B. 5253 would establish some of the strongest protections yet, requiring written parental opt-in consent for AI tool use and explicitly prohibiting AI from replacing licensed teachers in core instruction. Oklahoma's Responsible Technology in Schools Act would require all districts to adopt written AI policies before the 2027-28 school year. Several bills move beyond guidance frameworks to hard legislative requirements enforced on schools and vendors.

Why it mattersAustralian schools typically operate two to three years behind US state-level regulatory movements in education technology. The concentration of legislative activity – 52 bills, not one or two outliers – signals that a policy floor for AI in schools is forming in the English-speaking world. Two provisions in particular are worth noting as likely to reach Australia: mandatory written AI policies at school level (analogous to the NSW Department of Education's existing guidance but potentially with legislative force), and prohibitions on AI replacing licensed teacher instruction (which has implications for how AI-assisted tutoring products are marketed and used). Schools that build these protections into their AI governance now will be ahead of where regulation is likely to land.

Sources: FutureEd, "2026 State AI in Education Legislative Tracker", June 2026; Pursuit, "Latest AI in Education News and Policies: May-June 2026", June 2026; EdWeek, "More Teachers Are Using AI in Their Classrooms. Here's Why", 2026.

Where the evidence agrees

Independent sources pointing the same way

Adoption is running far ahead of both training and evidence

Three independent sources this week arrive at the same finding: schools are integrating AI faster than the evidence base or professional training can keep pace with; the risk is not future adoption – it is uninformed adoption that is already happening at scale.

Sources: Microsoft AI in Education Report (June 24): 77% of students, 53% of educators with no formal training; Stanford SCALE Review: only 20 causal studies from 800+ papers; OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026: performance gains without learning

Major platform providers are embedding AI into existing school tools, not requiring new ones

The route through which AI enters most classrooms will not be a dedicated AI platform requiring a procurement decision – it will be a feature update to Google Workspace or Microsoft 365 already in use; schools need AI governance that applies to existing platforms, not only new tool approvals.

Sources: Google Gemini in Classroom, Microsoft 365 Education free additions, ESA/Microsoft partnership (June 21)

General-purpose AI tools show weaker learning outcomes than purpose-built pedagogical AI

Three separate research sources confirm the same principle: the question is not whether to use AI in classrooms but how it is designed – tools that elicit thinking show better outcomes than tools that substitute for it; this gives schools a practical criterion for evaluating AI products.

Sources: Stanford SCALE, OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026, UTS cognitive atrophy report (March 2026)

Where experts disagree

Live debates worth understanding before deciding

Is the AI-in-education momentum something to build on or contain?

One viewMicrosoft and Google frame near-universal AI adoption as a foundation for schools to move from experimentation to responsible implementation – the trajectory is set and the task is to steer it well. (Microsoft Report, June 24; Google ISTE, June 2026)
The other viewStanford SCALE and the OECD find that adoption driven by tool availability rather than evidence risks locking in practices with no demonstrated learning benefit – or active harm through cognitive offloading – before schools have the evidence to evaluate them. (Stanford SCALE; OECD 2026)
The tensionBoth positions contain truth: the adoption wave is real and cannot be reversed; but the vendor framing of "responsible implementation" glosses over the fact that almost no high-quality evidence yet exists for what responsible student AI use looks like in a K-12 classroom; schools need to hold both simultaneously.

Should AI detection tools be used to police academic integrity?

One viewAI detection tools are being used widely – 68% of US middle and high school teachers used them in 2023-24 – and represent a measurable institutional response to the academic integrity challenge that schools face under growing pressure from school leaders and parents.
The other viewDetection tools produce false positive rates of up to 61% for essays written by non-native English speakers, have been disabled by multiple universities citing equity concerns, and create a punitive dynamic that may not reduce AI use. A study published in 2026 documenting structural detection limits confirms these tools cannot reliably differentiate human from AI writing. (arXiv: AI Detectors Fail Diverse Student Populations, June 2026; GradPilot: AI Detection Bias)
The tensionThe false positive rate for non-native English speakers is particularly relevant for Sydney independent schools with culturally and linguistically diverse student populations; relying on AI detection tools without understanding these equity risks exposes schools to disciplinary actions that could be unjust and legally contestable.

Worth watching

Developments to keep an eye on

Teaching and Learning Commission design: what happens to AI governance when the agencies merge?

The working group's design proposal, due to ministers this month, has not yet been made public. When it is, the most important question for AI in schools is whether the Commission's mandate explicitly includes AI policy and EdTech quality assurance – or whether those functions remain fragmented across jurisdictions. The Paul Ramsay Foundation and UTS have both called for a national quality assurance function for EdTech; the Commission is the obvious home for it. Watch for the design proposal's release and whether it addresses the EdTech quality assurance gap that Australian researchers have been identifying since 2024.

TRAINE longitudinal study: first-wave results still pending

The University of Melbourne's AERC Tracking AI in Education (TRAINE) study – a five-year longitudinal investigation of how generative AI is changing teaching, learning, and assessment in Australian secondary classrooms – has signalled a "new research launch" from the Faculty of Education but results have not yet been publicly released. When published, this will be the most substantive longitudinal Australian-specific evidence available on how AI is actually changing classroom practice. The TRAINE project page is worth checking weekly until it appears.
